# Structural Empowerment Sensing: Decoding the Structure–Activity
Relationship and Detection Application of G‑Quadruplex Aptamer


## Abstract
  G-Quadruplex (G4) aptamers have emerged as versatile functional nucleic acid architectures for biosensing because of their unique conformational plasticity, programmable topologies, and intrinsic signal-regulation capabilities. Unlike conventional recognition elements that mainly function as passive binding scaffolds, G4 aptamers can actively regulate signal generation and transduction through topology-dependent structural dynamics. Increasing evidence indicates that biosensing performance is closely governed by conformational energetics, folding kinetics, loop organization, ion coordination, and nano-interfacial coupling. This review summarizes recent advances in structure-guided G4 aptamer biosensing from the perspective of “structural empowerment”, where conformational architectures directly determine sensing behavior and analytical output. We first discuss the structural fundamentals of G4 aptamers, including topology diversity, thermodynamic stability, folding pathways, and environmental responsiveness, emphasizing their influence on molecular recognition and signal accessibility. We then analyze the mechanistic relationship between G4 conformational transitions and multiple signal transduction modes, including fluorescence, electrochemical, colorimetric, surface-enhanced Raman scattering, photoelectrochemical, and catalytic sensing systems. Particular attention is given to the integration of G4 architectures with CRISPR platforms, nanozymes, DNA amplification circuits, metal–organic frameworks, and nanointerfacial engineering strategies. Importantly, this review establishes a structure–performance framework correlating G4 topological features and kinetic behaviors with analytical outcomes. Current limitations, including structural polymorphism, ionic dependence, reproducibility issues, and limited quantitative predictability, are critically discussed. Finally, future opportunities in adaptive biosensing, intelligent molecular interfaces, and AI-assisted structural design are highlighted. This review establishes a quantitative structure–performance framework by correlating G4 structural descriptors, conformational energetics, kinetic parameters, and analytical outputs, providing a basis for predictive design of G4-based sensing systems.


## Introduction
  Nucleic acid aptamers ,  have emerged as versatile molecular recognition elements in analytical chemistry, owing to their high designability, excellent chemical stability, and strong affinity toward a broad range of targets. However, despite their widespread application, the development of aptamer-based analytical systems remains largely driven by sequence-level optimization with limited integration of higher-order structural principles. As analytical demands evolve toward higher sensitivity, controllability, and system-level integration, it has become increasingly evident that sequence affinity alone is insufficient to ensure a robust analytical performance. Instead, structural organization and conformational dynamics play a decisive role in governing the recognition behavior, signal transduction, and system stability. This shift highlights a fundamental limitation in current aptamer design: the lack of a unified framework linking the sequence, structure, and analytical function.

  In this context, G4 aptamers ,  have attracted growing attention as a model system for structure-driven analytical sensing. Distinguished by a well-defined structural hierarchy, rich topological diversity, and environment-responsive stabilization mechanisms, G4 systems provide a unique platform in which structural features can be directly translated into functional outputs.


### Analytical
Aptamers and the Rise of Structure-Based
Sensing
  The evolution of aptamer-based analytical systems can be broadly categorized into three conceptual stages. The first stage focuses on sequence-dominated recognition, where analytical performance is primarily governed by binding affinity and selectivity. The second stage introduces recognition–signal coupling, typically achieved through optical or electrochemical labels. The third and emerging stage advances toward structure-regulated sensing, in which conformational transitions actively participate in signal generation and amplification.

  In the first two stages, aptamers are generally treated as flexible recognition probes with limited consideration of their structural organization. However, as analytical systems become increasingly complex, they reveal intrinsic limitations. Sequence affinity alone cannot simultaneously ensure sensitivity, stability, and reproducibility, particularly under heterogeneous or dynamic conditions. Consequently, increasing attention has shifted toward higher-order structures and their conformational dynamics as key determinants of the analytical performance.

  Among structurally defined aptamers, G-quadruplexes stand out due to their unique formation mechanism via Hoogsteen hydrogen bonding, where guanine bases assemble into planar G quartets that further stack into ordered architectures. These structures exhibit diverse topologies, including parallel, antiparallel, and hybrid forms, along with tunable stability governed by environmental factors. ,

  More importantly, these structural features extend beyond passive stability and directly enable functional behavior. G4 architectures can act as intrinsic signal transduction units, where conformational transitions, stacking interactions, and ion coordination collectively modulate analytical outputs. ,  In this sense, the emergence of G4 aptamers represents a conceptual transition from a “sequence-dominated” paradigm to a “structure-dominated” analytical framework.


### Structural Empowerment: From Structure to
Function
  To rationalize the unique role of G4 in analytical systems, we introduce the concept of structural empowerment, defined as the process by which higher-order molecular structures actively enhance recognition performance and signal output through their conformational properties, stabilization mechanisms, and environmental responsiveness.

  Within this framework, the functionality of G4 is not merely encoded at the sequence level but is fundamentally governed by its three-dimensional structural organization. The stacked G-quartet scaffold imposes conformational constraints that reduce structural heterogeneity, thereby improving binding selectivity and reproducibility. Meanwhile, the coordination of metal ionssuch as K+ and Pb2+within the channel stabilizes folded states and lowers the entropic cost of structure formation, enabling G4 to maintain defined conformations even under complex analytical conditions. ,

  Crucially, these features indicate that G4 structures operate within a tunable conformational landscape where stability, dynamics, and function are intrinsically coupled. This coupling provides a physicochemical basis for translating structural regulation into an analytical performance.

  When further integrated with functional cofactors or interfacial environments, these structural advantages can be amplified into functional outputs. For example, the formation of G4–hemin complexes introduces peroxidase-like catalytic activity, enabling a transition from passive recognition to catalytic signal amplification. ,  Similarly, confinement within nanostructured interfaces enhances conformational stability and signal consistency by restricting structural fluctuations. ,

  Taken together, these observations suggest that G4 structures not only determine whether molecular recognition occurs but also critically govern how signals are generated, amplified, and integrated within analytical systems.


### Scope and Objectives of
This Review
  On the basis of these considerations, this review aims to establish a structure-oriented framework for understanding and designing G-quadruplex aptamer-based analytical systems. Rather than providing a descriptive summary of existing studies, we focus on elucidating the underlying structural logic that connects the molecular architecture to functional performance.

  Specifically, this review is organized around a central theme of “structure–activity–application,” emphasizing how structural features are characterized, regulated, and ultimately translated into analytical function. First, the fundamental structural characteristics, topological diversity, and stabilization mechanisms of G4 are discussed (Section  ), providing a basis for understanding their conformational behavior. Next, the roles of ions, small molecules, and interfacial environments in modulating G4 structure and activity are examined (Section  ). This is followed by a systematic analysis of G4-based analytical strategies, including signal generation, recognition mechanisms, and amplification pathways (Section  ).

  Finally, emerging directionsincluding artificial intelligence-assisted design, in situ structural characterization, and integrated analytical systemsare discussed to highlight future opportunities and challenges (Section  ).

  Importantly, in this framework, structural features are considered quantitative variables rather than descriptive characteristics, enabling the establishment of relationships among conformational populations, transition kinetics, and sensing performance metrics. Despite these advantages, the translation of G4-aptamer-based sensors from ideal buffer systems to biologically and environmentally complex matrices remains challenging. In intracellular or serum-containing environments, G4 probes may suffer from nuclease-mediated degradation, competition between endogenous cations such as Na+ and K+, nonspecific adsorption by serum proteins, and matrix-induced shifts in folding equilibria. Therefore, a structure-oriented review of G4 aptamer sensing should not only summarize analytical performance but also critically evaluate how chemical modification and nanointerface engineering preserve target-sensing conformations under realistic conditions.


## Structural Basis and Structure–Activity
Relationships of G-Quadruplexes

### Molecular Construction
and Topological Diversity
  G4 are higher-order nucleic acid structures formed by guanine-rich sequences through Hoogsteen hydrogen bonding. ,  Their fundamental structural unit, the G-tetrad, consists of four guanine bases arranged in a planar configuration stabilized by a cyclic hydrogen-bonding network involving N7, O6, and N1 positions. Stacking of multiple G-tetrads along the helical axis generates a compact π-conjugated core, leading to reduced solvent exposure and enhanced thermodynamic stability compared to canonical duplex or hairpin structures. However, the analytical relevance of G4 does not arise solely from structural stability, but from how stability is selectively achieved and dynamically regulated. A central determinant of this regulation is cation coordination within the G4 channel. ,  Metal ions coordinate with O6 carbonyl groups, mitigating electrostatic repulsion and stabilizing stacked tetrads. Importantly, different cations do not merely “stabilize” G4 to different extentsthey reshape the conformational landscape. K+, with an optimal ionic radius and coordination geometry, promotes compact and well-defined structures, whereas Na+ supports a broader ensemble of metastable states with increased conformational heterogeneity. −   This distinction has direct analytical implications: ionic conditions can be used not only to control structural stability but also to tune conformational homogeneity, folding pathways, and signal reproducibility.

  The formation of G4 is not a static assembly event but a dynamic folding process governed by cooperative environmental factors. It typically involves nucleation, stacking growth, and subsequent conformational rearrangement, with competition among different folding pathways giving rise to conformational polymorphism. As illustrated in Figure  A, in the presence of potassium ions, conformational transitions follow relatively ordered pathways: interconversion among different folding states (basket, propeller, hybrid-I, hybrid-II) proceeds through intermediate states (I1–I4, such as hairpins and triplexes) and transition states (T1–T4). The associated energy barriers (e.g., 0.70 and 1.13 kcal/mol) indicate a structured energy landscape, contributing to enhanced stability and well-defined folding routes. This suggests that K+ stabilizes G4 and promotes sequential folding pathways. In contrast, Figure  B shows that although similar intermediates exist under Na+ conditions, the overall energy distribution is more dispersed and the structures more flexible, with lower or irregular transition barriers. This reflects the weaker stabilizing effect of Na+, leading to higher conformational diversity and folding behavior closer to a parallel, multi-pathway mechanism.

  Beyond ionic effects, molecular crowding, dielectric environment variations, and local hydration rearrangements also significantly influence folding kinetics and stability regimes. Accordingly, G4 stability is commonly characterized by melting temperature, free energy changes, and ion dependence, while differences in enthalpic and entropic contributions among conformations further reflect stacking efficiency and solvent reorganization. ,  These thermodynamic and kinetic parameters collectively constitute the physical basis for the structural regulation and functional expression of G4.

  Topologically, G4 structures are commonly classified into parallel, antiparallel, and hybrid forms. Parallel G4s exhibit uniform strand orientation and compact propeller loops, typically associated with high stability and characteristic CD signals at ∼260 nm. ,  Antiparallel G4s display mixed strand orientations and syn/anti glycosidic conformations, resulting in greater flexibility and CD signatures around ∼295 nm. ,  Hybrid forms combine features of both and are frequently observed under physiological conditions. ,  However, this canonical classification represents only a coarse-grained description. Increasing evidence indicates that even within a single topology, multiple sub-states with comparable energies coexist due to subtle variations in stacking registry, loop geometry, and terminal interactions. −    Moreover, higher-order assemblies formed via inter-G4 stacking or cooperative interactions further expand structural complexity. −

  Taken together, these observations suggest that G4 behavior is best described within a multidimensional energy landscape framework, where experimentally observed conformations arise from both thermodynamic stability and kinetic partitioning. −   Controlling G4 function requires regulating not only its final structure but also the folding pathways and accessible conformational ensemble.

  Nevertheless, topology classification should be regarded as a global structural description rather than a complete predictor of sensing behavior. Although parallel, antiparallel, and hybrid G4s differ in strand orientation, loop arrangement, and terminal exposure, analytical performance is ultimately governed by more refined structural parameters, including loop length, groove geometry, terminal G-tetrad accessibility, solvent exposure, and folding/unfolding kinetics. Therefore, the topology of G4 should be further decomposed into quantitative molecular descriptors that can be correlated with binding affinity, signal activation efficiency, and background leakage. This descriptor-based perspective provides the conceptual transition from structural classification to structure–activity decoding.


### Local Structural Elements and Recognition
Interface Shaping
  While the G-tetrad core provides structural rigidity, the functional adaptability of G4 primarily originates from its peripheral elements, particularly loop regions and terminal interfaces. ,  Loops connect adjacent G-tetrads and define overall topology through their length, sequence composition, and spatial arrangement. Depending on connectivity, loops are classified as propeller, lateral, or diagonal types, each imposing distinct conformational constraints. ,  In general, shorter loops favor compact and thermodynamically stable conformations, whereas longer or more flexible loops increase conformational entropy, promoting polymorphism and reducing stability. −   Importantly, loops are also the most solvent-exposed and dynamically responsive regions of G4, often acting as the primary sites for environmental sensing and structural adaptation. ,  Crucially, loops function as more than structural linkersthey define the effective recognition interface of G4. Variations in loop geometry and composition directly modulate surface accessibility, local electrostatics, and binding-site architecture. Similarly, terminal regions play a critical role in modulating G4 behavior. End-stacking interactions between terminal bases and outer G-tetrads enhance structural stability and resistance to thermal perturbation. ,  In addition, terminal sites frequently serve as docking platforms for ligands, cofactors, or inter-G4 interactions, enabling higher-order assembly.

  From a functional perspective, loops and termini together determine how structural information is exposed to an external environment. Target specificity, signal responsiveness, and environmental adaptability of G4 systems can be rationally tuned by engineering loop and terminal structures without disrupting the core scaffold.

  In addition to loop and terminal organization, groove geometry and solvent accessibility are important local descriptors that determine how G4 structures interact with external molecules. Groove width controls the accommodation and orientation of small ligands or cofactors, whereas the solvent-accessible surface area of terminal G-tetrads determines the availability of π-stacking platforms for aromatic dyes and hemin. These local parameters explain why G4 structures with similar global topologies may display different ligand-binding affinities, catalytic activities, or fluorescence responses. Thus, loops, grooves, and terminal surfaces together constitute the molecular recognition interface of G4 aptamers.


### Structure–Activity
Relationships: From
Affinity to Energy Landscape
  The SAR of G4 extends beyond simple sequence–affinity correlations and is more appropriately understood as a multilevel coupling among sequence encoding, conformational organization, and functional output. At the primary level, loop regions act as critical determinants of molecular recognition. Even when the G-tetrad core remains conserved, variations in loop composition can induce substantial changes in binding affinity, often spanning orders of magnitude. Such effects arise from loop-mediated reshaping of binding interfaces and conformational accessibility. Similarly, G-tract length and strand architecture influence conformational integrity and stability, thereby modulating affinity landscapes. In many systems, increased G-tract number enhances folding stability and reduces K_d, although the effect is strongly context-dependent. Terminal modifications provide an additional layer of control. By enhancing π–π stacking or imposing conformational constraints, terminal engineering improves geometric complementarity with ligands, particularly for planar aromatic systems such as macrocycles.

  In multivariable analyses, loop length often exhibits a nonlinear relationship with K_d: within short loop regimes, minor length variations can cause significant fluctuations in K_d, whereas beyond a certain threshold, this effect tends to plateau. A similar trend is observed in terminal aromatic matching: planar aromatic molecules with better geometric complementarity to G-tetrad surfaces generally show lower K_d values, whereas structurally similar but less planar molecules exhibit reduced affinity. Overall, the binding affinity of G4 is not dictated by a single sequence factor but arises from the synergistic interplay among the loop encoding, G-tract architecture, and terminal organization.

  Beyond binding strength, functional output of G4 also depends on its interaction modes with different targets. G4 structures feature exposed G-tetrad surfaces, topology-dependent grooves, and negatively charged phosphate backbones, enabling multilevel interactions with small molecules, proteins, metal ions, and functional ligands. For small-molecule recognition, π–π stacking remains the most prevalent and fundamental interaction mode. ,  Electrostatic interactions often act synergistically, as high-affinity ligands typically contain permanent positive charges or protonatable groups that favorably interact with the negatively charged phosphate backbone.

  In contrast, protein–G4 recognition relies more on large-scale interface complementarity and multivalent interactions. High-resolution structural studies reveal that some G4-binding proteins utilize α-helices and loop regions to form relatively flat binding surfaces that directly cover exposed G-tetrads, while positively charged regions enriched in Arg/Lys residues establish extensive electrostatic and hydrogen-bond interactions with the phosphate backbone, enabling both high affinity and topology selectivity.

  Furthermore, G4 can bind functional cofactors such as hemin to form catalytically active complexes. In typical G4–hemin DNAzymes, hemin binds primarily through π–π stacking with terminal G-tetrads, conferring peroxidase-like activity. In advanced analytical and nanotechnological systems, such complexes can further integrate with cationic carriers via electrostatic assembly, achieving synergistic coupling of catalysis, targeting, and signal amplification.

  For G4 and other noncanonical nucleic acid structures, describing SAR solely in terms of binding affinity is insufficient. Increasing evidence indicates that functional output depends not only on ligand binding but also on how binding alters thermodynamic stability, folding/unfolding kinetics, and the conformational energy landscape. Thus, SAR understanding is shifting from a single affinity-based description toward an integrated thermodynamic–kinetic–conformational framework.

  Accordingly, G4 SAR should not be evaluated solely by equilibrium affinity. Therefore, SAR should evolve from qualitative structure comparison toward a descriptor-based predictive model, where structural variables can be quantitatively associated with energetic states, kinetic transitions, and analytical outputs. Instead, the same apparent K d may correspond to different sensing behaviors when the underlying association and dissociation processes differ. For sensing systems, k on determines how rapidly target binding or cofactor recruitment occurs, whereas k off determines the lifetime of the active signaling state. This distinction is particularly important in fluorescence, catalytic, SERS, and electrochemical platforms, where the analytical signal is often generated under non-equilibrium conditions. Increasing evidence demonstrates that functional output depends on how binding perturbs the thermodynamic and kinetic landscape of the system. Thermodynamically, parameters such as ΔG and T_m reflect the cumulative contributions of stacking, electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and solvent reorganization. −    However, these parameters are not always directly correlated, indicating significant entropic contributions and conformational complexity. ,  Kinetically, G4 stabilization can arise through distinct mechanisms: reduction of unfolding rates or enhancement of folding rates. ,  Notably, ligands with similar thermodynamic stabilization effects may exhibit fundamentally different kinetic behaviorssome prolong the lifetime of folded states, while others accelerate folding by lowering nucleation barriers. ,  This distinction is particularly critical in dynamic analytical environments, where non-equilibrium conditions dominate, and folding kinetics may determine functional performance more directly than equilibrium stability.

  Furthermore, G4 folding follows a multistep pathway involving intermediate states and kinetic partitioning. Ligands or modifications can reshape the energy landscape by stabilizing specific intermediates or redirecting folding flux, rather than simply lowering the energy of the final state. ,

  Similar phenomena have been observed in i-motif systems, where certain nucleoside modifications modulate sugar conformation or substituent effects, significantly slowing unfolding and stabilizing intermediates, allowing their capture within experimental time scales. ,  Therefore, a more comprehensive SAR framework should incorporate three dimensions: thermodynamic driving forces, kinetic control factors, and energy landscape remodeling effects. ,  Within this three-dimensional perspective, different ligands or modification strategies can be categorized as “stabilizing factors,” “folding promoters,” or “conformational remodelers”. This framework not only deepens the understanding of the intrinsic nature of G4 structure–activity relationships but also provides a direct theoretical foundation for performance enhancement through programmable structural interventions.

  Taken together, Section   establishes that G4 activity is governed by coupled affinity, thermodynamics, kinetics, and energy-landscape remodeling. The next step is to identify which structural variables quantitatively encode these effects. Therefore, Section   focuses on molecular descriptorsincluding loop length, base composition, groove width, terminal G-tetrad exposure, and solvent-accessible surface areaand discusses how these parameters regulate K d, k on, k off, signal activation, and background leakage in G4-based sensing systems.


### Quantitative Structural Descriptors Governing
G4 Activity and Sensing Performance
  Although conventional classification of G-quadruplexes into parallel, antiparallel, and hybrid topologies provides a fundamental framework for describing structural diversity, such categorical definitions are insufficient to predict the analytical performance. For precision sensing applications, the functional behavior of G4 aptamers is determined not only by their global topology but also by a series of quantitative structural descriptors that define the conformational accessibility, molecular recognition capability, and signal transduction efficiency. These descriptors include the loop length and composition, G-tract organization, terminal G-tetrad accessibility, groove geometry, solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), electrostatic surface distribution, and folding kinetics. Collectively, these parameters establish the relationship among sequence encoding, three-dimensional structure, and analytical output.

  The loop architecture represents one of the most important structural parameters regulating G4 activity. The length, nucleotide composition, and spatial arrangement of the loops determine the conformational entropy and flexibility of the folded structure. Short and rigid loops generally reduce the number of accessible conformational states, thereby increasing the population of preorganized binding competent structures and reducing nonspecific interactions. In contrast, extended or flexible loops increase conformational diversity and may facilitate structural adaptation toward different targets but can also increase the background structural leakage. Therefore, loop engineering provides a direct strategy to balance the recognition adaptability and structural precision. In biosensing systems, loop-mediated regulation influences not only the equilibrium binding affinity but also the association and dissociation kinetics by controlling the accessibility and residence time of functional conformations.

  Beyond loop characteristics, quantitative parameters associated with the G-rich core strongly determine structural stability and ligand responsiveness. The number of stacked G-tetrads, guanine tract length, and neighboring nucleotide identity regulate quartet stacking efficiency, Hoogsteen hydrogen-bond networks, and cation coordination. These factors collectively determine the folding free energy and melting temperature, which define the equilibrium distribution between folded and unfolded states. Importantly, structural stability does not directly correspond to sensing performance. Instead, an optimal balance between rigidity and dynamic accessibility is required. For example, RCA-generated G-rich sequences can efficiently fold into G4 structures in the presence of K+ and provide multiple binding sites for methylene blue through π–π interactions between the planar G-quartet surface and aromatic indicators, thereby enhancing electrochemical signal output.

  The geometry and accessibility of terminal G-tetrads represent another critical descriptor for predicting ligand and cofactor interaction. The exposed terminal quartet surface provides the primary interface for aromatic stacking interactions with small molecules such as hemin, thioflavin T (ThT), and thiazole orange (TO). Molecular recognition is therefore strongly influenced by the terminal solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), quartet planarity, and local electrostatic environment. In the TO-modified carbon dot system, the fluorescence response was shown to be strongly topology-dependent: CDs-TO exhibited good selectivity toward parallel G4 and dsDNA over antiparallel G4 and single-stranded DNA, and the G4/hemin peroxidase activity inhibition experiment further indicated that CDs-TO interacts with G4 mainly through an end-stacking mode. This result suggests that terminal G-tetrad accessibility and stacking geometry directly regulate fluorescence activation efficiency. Similarly, G4–hemin catalytic activity depends on the formation of a stable π-stacking interface between hemin and terminal G-quartets. Enhanced accessibility of terminal surfaces facilitates hemin positioning and promotes peroxidase-like catalysis, whereas insufficient exposure limits catalytic activation. The Cu-hemin@G4 nanozyme system further demonstrates this principle: compared with isolated Cu-hemin MOF, incorporation of G4 markedly enhanced peroxidase-like activity, indicating that the rigid G4 scaffold and local nucleic acid microenvironment can improve active-site exposure, substrate enrichment, and catalytic turnover. Therefore, terminal G-tetrad exposure should be regarded as a quantitative activity descriptor rather than a merely structural feature.

  Groove dimensions and surface electrostatic characteristics provide additional descriptors for explaining target selectivity and signal generation. The width and chemical environment of G4 grooves influence ligand accommodation, molecular orientation, and interaction strength. Small fluorescent ligands such as ThT and TO require appropriate confinement environments to restrict intramolecular rotation and enhance fluorescence emission. In contrast, electrochemical and Raman sensing platforms depend more strongly on the ability of G4 structures to regulate spatial organization of signal molecules or nanointerfaces. For example, human telomeric G4 structures can modulate the distance between Cy3 reporters and Ag@Au nanoparticles through reversible folding-to-duplex conformational switching, resulting in opposite SERS and fluorescence responses. In this case, G4 topology acts as a nanoscale distance regulator, linking conformational geometry with plasmonic signal transduction. Target-induced G4 folding can also remodel nanointerfacial catalytic states. In the Pb2+ SERS sensing system, the Pb2+ aptamer adsorbs on AuNP surfaces and suppresses AuNP-catalyzed nanoplasmonic reactions in the absence of Pb2+. Upon addition of Pb2+, the aptamer folds into a stable G-quadruplex, releasing the AuNP surface and restoring nanocatalytic activity, which leads to enhanced SERS intensity. This example illustrates how ion-induced G4 formation can convert a molecular binding event into a measurable nanoplasmonic response through topology-controlled interfacial accessibility. Importantly, structural descriptors should be integrated with kinetic parameters rather than considered solely through equilibrium affinity. Two G4 structures with similar K d values may generate significantly different sensing outputs because analytical responses depend on the lifetime and accessibility of active conformations. The association rate determines how rapidly functional structures interact with targets or cofactors, whereas the dissociation rate controls signal persistence and reversibility. In catalytic systems, the residence time of cofactors such as hemin directly affects catalytic turnover. In fluorescence systems, the lifetime of dye-bound conformations determines fluorescence enhancement efficiency. Therefore, quantitative structure–activity analysis requires simultaneous consideration of equilibrium affinity, kinetic stability, and conformational transition rates.

  These structural parameters also provide a molecular explanation for background leakage suppression, which is essential for precision sensing. In conventional G4-based reporters, spontaneous folding of G-rich sequences may generate active structures even without target stimulation, producing false-positive signals. Structural engineering strategies, including split G4 design, phosphorothioate modification, and conformational preorganization, suppress leakage by increasing the energetic barrier between inactive and active states. In the SCas12a/psHG3 system, phosphorothioate-modified G-rich reporters remain in a constrained hairpin state before target activation. Target recognition activates SCas12a-mediated cleavage at the loop region, releasing the prelocked psG3 fragment, which then binds ThT to generate a turn-on fluorescent signal. This mechanism reduces background fluorescence and converts target-triggered structural reconstruction into a low-leakage output. Similarly, target-induced reconstruction of G4/hemin systems can transform inactive precursors into catalytically active DNAzymes. In the β-lactoglobulin dual-mode biosensor, the G4/hemin DNAzyme catalyzes TMB oxidation in the absence of target, while target binding disrupts the DNAzyme structure, decreases peroxidase-like activity, and restores carbon dot fluorescence through attenuation of the inner filter effect. In SERS-based amplification, self-amplifying circuits can generate G4 motifs that bind hemin to form DNAzymes, catalyzing ROS production and inducing silver-shell etching of plasmonic nanoprobes. These examples further demonstrate that G4 activity is governed by controlled transitions among inactive, partially folded, and fully active conformational states.

  Therefore, the structure–activity relationship of G4 aptamers should be described as a multidimensional quantitative framework rather than a simple topology-dependent phenomenon. Topology provides the global structural identity, whereas molecular descriptors including loop parameters, G-tetrad accessibility, groove geometry, SASA, and electrostatic distribution determine binding affinity, kinetic behavior, and signal activation. Establishing quantitative correlations among these descriptors, molecular parameters, and analytical metrics including LOD, linear range, response time, and background level will enable predictive design of next-generation precision G4 sensing platforms.


## Structure-Empowerment Strategies for Performance
Enhancement
  Building upon the conformational energy landscape framework established in Section  , the functional performance of G4 can be understood as the result of coupled thermodynamic stability, kinetic accessibility, and conformational state distribution, rather than a single stability parameter. Accordingly, performance enhancement is no longer achieved by simply “increasing stability,” but by actively engineering the conformational energy landscape.

  In this context, structure-empowerment strategies can be viewed as programmable interventions that reshape conformational freedom, redistribute energy barriers, and redirect functional output pathways. At different levels, these strategies are manifested asIntrinsic landscape preorganization through chemical and nucleic acid modifications;extrinsic landscape modulation via ions and cofactors;environmental landscape confinement through nano interfaces and spatial restriction.


### Chemical
and Nucleic Acid Modifications: Intrinsic
Conformational Preorganization and Kinetic Gating
  Chemical and nucleic acid modifications represent a form of intrinsic energy landscape preorganization in which conformational freedom and folding propensity are encoded at the sequence level prior to target interaction. Rather than merely stabilizing G4 structures, these modifications regulate entropy, local flexibility, and kinetic barriers, thereby biasing folding pathways toward functionally productive states.

  LNA significantly reduces backbone conformational freedom by locking the ribose ring into the C3′-endo conformation, thereby decreasing the entropic penalty during binding. In various G-rich and duplex-coupled systems, LNA modification generally results in pronounced increases in T_m and binding affinity, while not substantially altering the overall folding topology of G4. ,  In aptamer and probe design, LNA is particularly suitable for stabilizing duplex support regions or recognition arms, where functional conformations can be indirectly constrained to improve recognition fidelity and signal reliability. ,  From an energy landscape perspective, LNA effectively narrows the conformational ensemble and lowers entropic costs, corresponding to a strong conformational locking effect.

  In contrast, 2′-OMe modification exhibits a milder conformational tuning effect. While preserving an RNA-like conformational tendency, this modification enhances nuclease resistance and local stability through steric shielding, and usually does not significantly perturb the original folding topology. ,  Therefore, 2′-OMe is more suitable for introduction into loop regions or flexible linkers, where it enhances system stability while retaining the conformational adaptability required for function. This reflects a local flexibility modulation mechanism, in which the energy landscape is subtly reshaped without altering its global topology.

  Phosphorothioate (PS) modification represents another type of empowerment strategy centered on backbone chemical remodeling. By altering the electronic properties and local spatial characteristics of the phosphate backbone, PS modification not only improves the biostability of nucleic acids but also significantly influences their conformational dynamics. In G-rich structures and CRISPR/Cas-related systems, PS modification is often used to construct “prelocked” conformations, allowing probes to maintain a relatively high T_m in the inactive state while rapidly releasing the functional structure upon a specific triggering event, thereby achieving a low background and high signal-to-noise output. ,  In this sense, PS introduces a kinetic gating mechanism, where energy barriers between inactive and active states are deliberately engineered.

  The effectiveness of chemical modification in G4 aptamer sensing depends strongly on the modification site. Modifications introduced into loop regions generally regulate local flexibility, nuclease accessibility, and target-induced conformational adaptation without directly disrupting the G-quartet core. Therefore, loop-localized 2′-OMe or limited LNA incorporation can improve nuclease resistance while preserving the topology required for target recognition. In contrast, modifications within G-tract regions must be used cautiously, because excessive alteration of guanine residues or backbone geometry may perturb Hoogsteen hydrogen bonding, syn/anti glycosidic conformations, quartet planarity, or hemin/dye-accessible terminal surfaces. Backbone-oriented PS modification is particularly useful at terminal or exposed positions, where nuclease attack is more likely, and in stem or hairpin regions designed to maintain inactive preorganized states before target activation. However, extensive PS substitution may alter electrostatic interactions, metal-ion coordination, and nonspecific protein binding; thus, partial and site-directed PS modification is generally preferable to global backbone replacement.

  From a practical perspective, chemical modification should be evaluated by two criteria simultaneously: whether it prolongs probe lifetime in nuclease-rich media and whether it preserves the target-sensing G4 topology. A modification that increases T_m or serum stability is not necessarily beneficial if it locks the aptamer into a nonproductive topology or suppresses target-triggered conformational switching. Therefore, rational G4 probe design requires coupling nuclease resistance assays with structural validation methods such as CD spectroscopy, melting analysis, native gel electrophoresis, or fluorescence/SERS/electrochemical readout under serum-relevant ionic conditions.


### Ion and
Cofactor Regulation: Conformational
Induction, Functional Activation, and Catalytic Amplification
  If chemical modifications primarily define the intrinsic energy landscape of G4, then metal ions and small-molecule cofactors provide an extrinsic regulatory layer, enabling dynamic and reversible modulation of conformational states through environmental inputs. Importantly, this level of regulation not only induces and stabilizes G4 structures but also facilitates the conversion of conformational changes into amplifiable functional outputs, forming a structure-to-function transduction module.

  The formation of G4 is highly dependent on the coordination-driven stabilization of the central channel within the G-tetrad stack by metal ions. Among these, K+ is the most representative physiologically relevant ion and can effectively induce G-rich sequences to transform from disordered or hairpin states into stable parallel or hybrid G4 conformations. As shown in Figure  A, the aptamer folds from a loose linear or hairpin-like structure into a G-quadruplex featuring upper and lower G-quartet planes under the action of K+/Mg2+, thereby constructing a rigid nanoscaffold with exposed functional interfaces. In energy landscape terms, ion coordination defines the accessible conformational basin and stabilizes folded states. From a structural perspective, Figure  B illustrates a spatial switching process driven by a reversible G4 assembly. The folded G4 provides a continuous π-stacking interface that supports stable hemin binding (“closed” state), whereas disruption of the structure leads to a disordered configuration that cannot maintain cofactor positioning (“open” state). Upon target-induced hybridization, spatially separated fragments are brought together, reconstructing the G4 topology and restoring the functional interface. This process exemplifies a topology-controlled conformational transition, where structural reconfiguration governs cofactor positioning and functional activation.

  Compared with K+, Pb2+ exhibits stronger coordination affinity and can induce G4 formation under a lower ionic strength while conferring higher thermal stability and rigidity. It is therefore frequently employed both as a model ion for strong stabilization and as a trigger in conformational-switching systems for heavy metal detection, ,  effectively reshaping the energy landscape toward deeper and more rigid minima.

  A major challenge for translating ion-regulated G4 sensors into real biological matrices is that the ionic environment is not equivalent to optimized buffer conditions. Although K+ generally favors compact and catalytically competent G4 conformations, physiological media contain multiple endogenous cations, including high concentrations of Na+ as well as Mg2+, Ca2+, and other metal ions. These ions can compete for electrostatic screening, alter hydration shells, and shift the equilibrium among parallel, antiparallel, hybrid, and partially folded states. As a result, a G4 sequence that shows a high signal gain in K+-optimized buffer may exhibit reduced topology fidelity, slower folding kinetics, or increased background leakage in serum or intracellular environments. To address this issue, ion-responsive G4 aptamer sensors should be designed with topology robustness rather than maximal buffer-state stability as the primary criterion. Site-selective LNA or 2′-OMe incorporation can preorganize loop and support regions to reduce Na+-induced conformational dispersion, whereas PS-modified backbones can stabilize inactive or prelocked states before target activation. In addition, nano-interface confinement can locally enrich favorable ions, screen backbone repulsion, and reduce the extent to which bulk ionic fluctuations perturb the target-sensing topology. Therefore, evaluation under physiologically relevant Na+/K+ ratios and serum-containing media should be considered essential for validating G4-based sensing platforms.

  On the basis of ion-induced folding, small-molecule cofactors further regulate conformational homogeneity and functional output. TMPyP4 stabilizes G4 structures through π–π stacking and electrostatic interactions, suppressing competing duplex formation and thereby improving signal consistency. −   Hemin, in contrast, acts as both a structural and catalytic cofactor. Upon binding to the terminal G-tetrad, it forms a G4–hemin complex that exhibits peroxidase-like activity. ,  This step marks a critical transition from conformational formation to catalytic activation, transforming G4 from a structural recognition unit into a functional catalytic module. Once formed, G4–hemin DNAzymes catalyze H2O2-mediated oxidation of substrates such as ABTS, TMB, or luminol, generating amplified optical or electrochemical signals. ,  In many systems, a “conformational release” strategy is employed: G-rich sequences are initially constrained in inactive structures, and upon target recognition, are released to fold into active G4 configurations. ,  When coupled with nucleic acid amplification strategies such as RCA and HCR, this mechanism enables exponential signal amplification, achieving detection limits down to the fM or even aM level. −

  Hemin represents a unique cofactor that bridges G-quadruplex structural formation and catalytic signal generation. Unlike conventional enzyme mimics where catalytic activity originates from predefined protein architectures, G4–hemin DNAzymes rely on the cooperative organization of nucleic acid scaffolds and metalloporphyrin cofactors. Upon G4 folding, hemin is positioned above the terminal G-quartet through π–π stacking interactions, while the negatively charged phosphate environment and ordered quadruplex framework facilitate substrate accessibility and catalytic microenvironment formation. This structural arrangement generates peroxidase-like activity, enabling H2O2-mediated oxidation of chromogenic substrates including TMB, ABTS, and luminol. Importantly, catalytic efficiency is not solely determined by hemin binding affinity but is governed by multiple structural descriptors, including terminal G-tetrad accessibility, quartet planarity, topology, loop organization, and conformational stability. From a structure–activity perspective, G4–hemin DNAzyme activity represents a direct consequence of conformational regulation. Parallel or compact G4 structures with exposed terminal quartets generally provide more favorable hemin docking geometries, whereas steric restriction or incomplete folding reduces catalytic accessibility. In addition, ion-mediated stabilization and ligand-induced conformational remodeling can regulate the population of catalytically competent states, thereby controlling catalytic turnover and signal amplification efficiency. Therefore, G4–hemin DNAzymes should be considered as structure-encoded catalytic modules, where conformational landscapes determine enzymatic activity rather than merely serving as passive assembly frameworks.


### Nanointerfaces
and Confinement Engineering:
From Structural Stabilization to System Integration
  Beyond molecular and solution-phase regulation, nanointerfaces and confinement effects extend G4 control to the level of system-scale energy landscape engineering. Unlike intrinsic modifications, which encode sequence-level properties, nanointerfaces dynamically modulate the local microenvironment, thereby reshaping folding equilibria, kinetic pathways, and functional outputs in situ.

  AuNPs, owing to their controllable surface chemistry and excellent electron-transfer properties, have become core materials for constructing G4-functional interfaces. Immobilization of G-rich sequences via Au–S bonds enables the formation of highly dense and orientationally ordered G4 assemblies, improving conformational stability and amplifying signal responses. ,  Moreover, AuNP interfaces facilitate efficient transduction of conformational changes into electrical signals by lowering the charge-transfer resistance, ,  effectively coupling structural transitions to measurable outputs.

  In addition to metallic nanoparticles, MOFs and carbon-based nanomaterials regulate G4 behavior through spatial confinement. The ordered pore structures and tunable microenvironments of MOFs restrict conformational freedom, suppress competing folding pathways, and promote G4 formation. ,  When combined with G4–hemin DNAzymes, these systems act as “nanoreactors,” enhancing catalytic efficiency and signal amplification. ,

  The stabilizing effect of MOF-based and nanoconfined interfaces should be understood not only as an increase in probe loading but also as a local physicochemical regulation of the G4 folding landscape. Spatial confinement within ordered pores restricts the conformational entropy of G-rich strands, suppresses nonproductive folding pathways, and increases the residence time of the target-sensing topologies. In addition, the confined microenvironment can alter the local hydration and dielectric properties around the nucleic acid scaffold. Reduced water activity, reorganized hydration shells, and localized charge compensation may collectively favor G quartet stacking, cation retention, and hemin or dye positioning. These effects are particularly important in serum- or cell-derived samples, where bulk ionic strength and protein-rich matrices may otherwise destabilize or mask the active G4 conformation. Nanointerface chemistry also determines whether G4 aptamers can retain function in complex matrices. Serum proteins, extracellular vesicles, lipids, and small metabolites can nonspecifically adsorb onto metal or carbon surfaces, block aptamer accessibility, or generate false signals. Hydrophilic coatings, zwitterionic layers, PEGylated spacers, low-fouling electrode interfaces, and size-selective MOF pores can reduce nonspecific adsorption while maintaining access to small targets or cofactors. Therefore, interfacial design should be evaluated by both signal amplification efficiency and matrix resistance capability, including antifouling behavior, nuclease protection, and preservation of G4 topology under serum-containing or intracellular-mimicking conditions.

  Furthermore, molecular crowding and charge shielding represent forms of “soft confinement.” Excluded-volume effects increase the thermodynamic stability of G4 structures, while charge shielding reduces electrostatic repulsion between DNA backbones, allowing stable folding under lower ionic strength or complex sample conditions. −   These effects are particularly important for translating G4 systems from ideal buffer environments to real biological and environmental matrices.

  Overall, the enhancement of G4 performance is undergoing a paradigm shift from empirical stabilization toward predictive energy landscape engineering. Whether through intrinsic conformational preorganization (LNA, 2′-OMe, PS), extrinsic modulation (ions and cofactors), or environmental regulation (nanointerfaces and confinement), these strategies converge on a unified objective, precise control over folding pathways, conformational distributions, and structure-to-function transduction efficiency.

  A key future challenge lies in establishing quantitative relationships among structural descriptors, kinetic parameters, and analytical performance metrics while developing programmable and dynamically responsive multimodal regulatory platforms. Such advances will be essential for advancing G4 systems from empirical probe design to truly predictable and amplifiable functional nucleic acid engineering.

Therefore, in fluorescence sensing, G-quadruplex topology acts as a signal-selection filter, linking structural configuration to detection selectivity and sensitivity.

### General Principles of G-Quadruplex-Enabled
Sensing System
  Taken together, although fluorescence/colorimetric, SERS, and electrochemical sensing systems differ substantially in signal modality, detection format, and instrumental configuration, they can be unified under a common mechanistic paradigm governed by structure-controlled signal transduction. Across these platforms, G-quadruplexes do not merely serve as passive recognition scaffolds; rather, they function as programmable structural nodes that mediate the translation of molecular recognition events into measurable analytical outputs through well-defined physical pathways.

  More specifically, G-quadruplex-enabled sensing can be generalized into a hierarchical process in which structural organization dictates conformational state transitions, which in turn regulate signal generation mechanisms and ultimately determine analytical performance. Within this framework, different sensing modalities correspond to distinct physical realizations of the signal transduction. In optical systems, G-quadruplexes modulate fluorescence activation and catalytic chromogenic reactions through topology-dependent dye binding and DNAzyme formation. In SERS-based platforms, they regulate plasmonic responses by controlling nanointerfacial states, electromagnetic field couplings, and catalytic amplification processes. In electrochemical systems, they govern electron transfer pathways via interfacial organization, catalytic microenvironment engineering, and integration with amplification circuits. Therefore, the fundamental distinction among these sensing modes does not lie in the nature of the signal itself but in how G-quadruplex structures are coupled to specific physical signal-transduction mechanisms.

  From an evolutionary perspective, a clear trend can be identified across all three classes of systems. Early-stage designs primarily relied on direct conformational readout or simple target-induced switching, whereas more advanced systems increasingly emphasize catalytic amplification, large-scale structural reconstruction, and circuit-level integration. This progression reflects a paradigm shift from structure reporting to structure-driven signal engineering, in which G-quadruplexes evolve from being detectable structural motifs to active components that shape the signal landscape and amplification behavior of the entire sensing system. Another important unifying feature is that high-performance G-quadruplex-based sensors consistently rely on multi-level coupling strategies. These include coupling between structure and signal pathways, between catalytic processes and amplification mechanisms, and between interfacial organization and signal stability. Such coupling enables simultaneous optimization of sensitivity, robustness, and anti-interference capability. In this sense, analytical performance is not determined by any single functional component, but by the degree of integration within a structurally coordinated system. From a design standpoint, several general principles emerge. First, precise control over G-quadruplex folding and conformational transitions is essential for predictable signal output. Second, background suppression can be effectively achieved through structural preorganization, split designs, or constrained activation mechanisms. Third, signal robustness can be enhanced by implementing dual-channel or ratiometric strategies that mitigate environmental perturbations. Finally, efficient coupling between G-quadruplex structures and physical signal pathwayswhether optical, plasmonic, or electrochemicalis critical for maximizing transduction efficiency.

  Looking forward, further advances in this field will depend less on the discovery of new sensing formats and more on the establishment of predictive structure–signal–performance relationships. In particular, quantitative understanding of how G-quadruplex topology, conformational stability, local microenvironment, and interfacial interactions collectively influence signal generation and amplification will be crucial. Such insights will enable the rational design of next-generation sensing systems with improved reliability, programmability, and applicability in complex analytical environments. Ultimately, the development of G-quadruplex-based sensors is expected to move toward a more unified and theory-driven framework, in which structural design principles can be systematically translated into high-performance analytical functionalities.


### Translation from Ideal
Buffer Systems to Complex
Biological and Environmental Matrices
  Although many G4-aptamer-based sensors demonstrate impressive sensitivity in optimized buffer systems, their performance in real samples is governed by additional matrix-dependent factors. Biological and food-related matrices contain nucleases, serum proteins, salts, metabolites, lipids, and suspended particles that may degrade nucleic acid probes, alter G4 folding equilibria, compete with target binding, or interfere with optical, Raman, and electrochemical readouts. Therefore, analytical performance should not be evaluated solely by the LOD and linear range but also by topology retention, nuclease resistance, antifouling capability, recovery in real samples, and signal reproducibility under matrix-matched conditions.

  In optical and colorimetric systems, matrix coloration, light scattering, endogenous peroxidase-like components, and nonspecific protein adsorption may affect chromogenic or fluorescent signals. Chemically modified loops and protected backbones can improve probe lifetime, whereas ratiometric or dual-mode outputs reduce matrix-induced signal fluctuation. In SERS platforms, the key challenge is maintaining stable plasmonic hotspots and reporter–surface distances in protein-rich environments; surface passivation and G4-controlled spatial organization are therefore critical. In electrochemical systems, serum proteins and macromolecules may foul electrode surfaces and alter charge-transfer resistance, making low-fouling interfaces, oriented immobilization, and internal-reference or ratiometric strategies particularly valuable.

  Future studies should therefore report not only analytical figures of merit in buffer but also systematic validation in complex media, including nuclease challenge tests, serum incubation stability, Na+/K+ variation, recovery in real samples, and structural confirmation of G4 topology after exposure to matrix components. Such information is essential for distinguishing systems that merely function under optimized conditions from those that genuinely preserve structure-guided sensing capabilities in practical analytical environments.

  From an application perspective, integration with microfluidics, flexible electronics, and wearable systems enables controlled environments for real-time and continuous sensing. ,,,  Meanwhile, G4 is increasingly used as a coupling node in multimodal platforms, simultaneously regulating fluorescence, SERS, and electrochemical signals to improve robustness and reliability. ,,  Emerging strategies incorporating feedback regulation and adaptive amplification further indicate a transition toward programmable and responsive analytical systems. ,  Despite these advances, challenges remain, including conformational heterogeneity, interfacial nonspecific interactions, limited reproducibility, and the lack of unified predictive models linking the sequence design to sensing performance.

  A critical bottleneck for the practical deployment of G4 aptamer sensors is the lack of a standardized evaluation under biologically relevant stress conditions. Many reported systems show excellent sensitivity in optimized buffers but do not systematically examine nuclease degradation, Na+/K+ competition, serum protein adsorption, or matrix-induced topology switching. Future studies should therefore integrate chemical modification, nanointerface confinement, and matrix-matched validation into a unified design workflow. Site-selective modification should be used to protect nuclease-sensitive regions while preserving G4 topology, whereas nanointerfaces should be engineered to provide antifouling protection, local ion regulation, and hydration-controlled confinement. Such efforts will be essential for transforming G4 aptamer sensors from high-performance model systems to reliable analytical tools for clinical, food, and environmental applications.

  AI represents another promising direction for future G4 sensor development. Compared with protein engineering, AI-assisted design of nucleic acid functional structures remains relatively underdeveloped, particularly for G-quadruplex aptamers, owing to the scarcity of experimentally validated sequence–structure–function datasets. Future integration of machine learning, molecular dynamics simulations, and high-throughput structural characterization may enable predictive modeling of G4 folding landscapes and facilitate rational sequence optimization beyond empirical SELEX-based selection. Such data-driven design strategies could ultimately contribute to precision chemistry by establishing quantitative relationships between sequence, conformational dynamics, and analytical performance.

  Looking forward, three directions are particularly important: (i) data-driven design, enabling predictive sequence–structure–signal relationships; (ii) dynamic structural characterization, revealing spatiotemporal coupling between conformation and signal; and (iii) multimodal system integration, enabling adaptive and robust sensing in complex environments. Overall, the future of G4-based sensing will depend on establishing predictive and integrative design frameworks that couple structural programmability with signal-transduction efficiency, thereby supporting the development of reliable and intelligent analytical systems.


## Conclusion
  G-Quadruplex (G4) aptamers have emerged as highly programmable nucleic acid recognition elements, offering unique opportunities for the development of advanced biosensing platforms. Beyond their conventional role as molecular recognition probes, G4 aptamers possess intrinsic structural adaptability, enabling their higher-order conformations to serve as active regulators of recognition, signal generation, and analytical performance. In this review, we highlighted the concept of structural empowerment sensing, emphasizing that the functional output of G4-based sensors is not solely determined by sequence composition or target binding affinity, but also by the dynamic interplay among molecular folding, conformational transitions, and signal transduction pathways. Understanding these structure–activity relationships provides a fundamental basis for rationally designing next-generation G4 aptamer sensing systems.

  Recent advances in fluorescence, colorimetric, surface-enhanced Raman scattering, electrochemical, and nanoenabled sensing strategies demonstrate that structural regulation can significantly enhance sensitivity, selectivity, and adaptability. The integration of G4 aptamers with functional nanomaterials, catalytic cofactors, and emerging signal-amplification technologies further expands their analytical capabilities. However, challenges remain in precisely controlling G4 conformational landscapes, establishing quantitative relationships between structural parameters and sensing performance, and translating laboratory developed systems into robust practical applications. Addressing these issues will require interdisciplinary efforts that combine structural biology, computational modeling, nanotechnology, and analytical chemistry.

  Looking forward, the rational engineering of G4 aptamers will increasingly rely on a structure-guided design philosophy in which molecular architecture is considered an active component of sensing function rather than merely a passive scaffold. The development of predictive models for G4 folding behavior, artificial intelligence-assisted sequence optimization, and integrated sensing platforms may further accelerate the transition from an empirical design to programmable molecular engineering. Ultimately, exploiting the structural intelligence of G-quadruplex aptamers will provide new opportunities for constructing highly efficient, versatile, and application-oriented sensing technologies.

# Efficient genome editing in ASC-derived porcine lung organoids via GFP-guided enrichment


## Background
  Adult stem cell-derived organoids are invaluable tools for disease modeling and translational medicine owing to their tissue-specific correspondence. However, their complex three-dimensional structure and low genome-editing efficiency have posed significant challenges for functional genetic studies. To address these limitations, this study aimed to establish porcine lung organoid (PLOs) and to develop a robust, high-efficiency genome-editing platform by leveraging the high physiological and anatomical similarity between pigs and humans.


## Methods
  PLOs were derived from lung tissues and characterized by immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry analysis. To enhance genome-editing efficiency using CRISPR/Cas9 and prime editing, a GFP-guided enrichment strategy was used by co-targeting a GFP transgene and endogenous loci, followed by the selection of GFP-negative organoids. Clonal expansion from single organoids was performed to generate genetically homogenous lines. Genetic modifications were validated through cDNA sequencing and western blot analysis. Structural integrity was assessed using AlphaFold2 modeling, and functional changes were evaluated using the forskolin-induced swelling assay.


## Results
  The established PLOs exhibited sustained self-renewal capacity and expression of surfactant protein C alongside both proximal epithelial and mesenchymal markers, reflecting a composite lung organoid identity. GFP-guided enrichment significantly enriched edited cells; the mutation ratio in the GFP-negative group was significantly higher than that in the GFP-positive group. This system successfully generated fully gene-edited clones with high genetic homogeneity, and effectively enriched for precisely edited organoids, even when applying complex modalities such as prime editing.


## Conclusions
  The PLOs and genome-editing platform developed in this study provide a powerful resource for translational medicine. By enabling precise genetic manipulation in a model that closely mimics human lung physiology, this system offers an essential platform for recapitulating human lung pathologies, studying zoonotic infectious diseases, and validating the efficacy of gene therapies.

## Background
  Organoids are advanced three-dimensional (3D) culture systems that possess a stem cell niche, enabling long-term maintenance in vitro and reliable recapitulation of in vivo tissues and organs [1]. These characteristics position organoids as powerful in vitro model systems that are widely applied in various research fields, including toxicology, developmental biology, and physiology [2–6]. Moreover, genetic engineering of organoids allows them to recapitulate tissue-specific genetic alterations or correct disease-associated mutations, thereby expanding their applications in studies of genetic disorders and the development of novel cell therapies. In recent years, genetically engineered organoids have been employed in diverse areas, such as disease modeling, drug screening, regenerative medicine, and functional genomics [7–9].

  Adult stem cell-derived (ASC-derived) organoids are generated from mature tissues and therefore exhibit a higher degree of physiological maturity and more accurately replicate tissue-specific features than organoids derived from pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) [1, 10]. This makes ASC-derived organoids particularly valuable for studying adult organ diseases. However, because they require a complex microenvironment composed of stem cells, extracellular matrix (ECM), and various growth factors, ASC-derived organoids must be maintained in a 3D culture system. This complexity poses technical challenges for genetic manipulation, often resulting in reduced cell viability and increased heterogeneity, which limits their utility in genetic studies [9, 11]. Therefore, strategies to overcome these limitations are urgently needed.

  Pigs serve as highly valuable animal models in biomedical research because of their high anatomical and physiological similarities to humans [12–14]. Particularly, pigs recapitulate human physiological and immunological functions more accurately than rodents and offer advantages such as greater accessibility compared to non-human primates. These comprehensive similarities, spanning anatomical, physiological, and genetic dimensions, enable the modeling of complex human diseases that are difficult to replicate in rodents, including musculoskeletal and neurodegenerative disorders [15–18]. Building on these parallels, the integration of gene-editing technologies has significantly advanced porcine organ research, leading to successful xenotransplantation of the heart [19], kidney [20], and liver [21], thereby demonstrating their significant clinical value.

  Among various organs, the porcine lung closely resembles the human lung in anatomical size, epithelial cell composition of the airways [22], and genetic proximity, making it a more effective representative of human pulmonary physiology than rodent lungs [23]. Notably, these unique advantages of the porcine lung have enabled the recapitulation of diseases such as cystic fibrosis [24–26], which cannot be modeled in mice. Through successful reproduction of such conditions, the porcine lung has become an invaluable translational resource, serving as a robust model for viral pathogenesis [27], genetic disorders [16, 17, 24, 25], and preclinical studies [28] in human translational medicine.

  Recent studies have actively utilized ASC-derived organoids from porcine airway tissues for viral assessments [29, 30]. These organoids serve as a critical translational tool, providing a viable alternative to human tissues, which are often difficult to obtain due to ethical constraints and limited availability. However, most research to date has focused primarily on the airways and related viral infection studies. To fully mimic the physiological functions of the lung, organoid models that recapitulate its diverse epithelial populations, including type II alveolar (AT2) cells, the key stem cell of alveolar tissue [31, 32], are needed. Although such research has been actively conducted in mice for translational purposes [33, 34], porcine organoid models incorporating the distal alveolar compartment have primarily relied on PSC-derived models for developmental biology [35], and significant limitations remain in histologically recapitulating the human distal lung in vitro.

  To address these limitations, in this study, we established ASC-derived porcine lung organoids (PLOs) for the first time to facilitate translational research on human diseases. Furthermore, to overcome the persistent challenge of low gene-editing efficiency in ASC-derived organoids, we introduced a GFP-guided enrichment system, thereby enhancing the utility of PLOs in translational research.


## Materials and methods

### Media
  The media used in this study were based on a previously published human lung organoid study [1, 36]. The basal medium was prepared using Advanced DMEM/F12 (Gibco, 12634034) as the base, supplemented with 50 μg/mL Primocin (Invivogen, Ant-pm-1), 100 U/mL and 100 μg/mL Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122), 10 mM HEPES (Sigma), 1 × GlutaMAX (Gibco, 12634034), 0.5 × N2 supplement (Gibco, 17502048), 0.5 × B27 supplement (Gibco, 17504044), 1.25 mM N-acetylcysteine (Sigma, A9165), and 5 mM Nicotinamide (Sigma, N0636). The culture medium was prepared by adding 500 ng/mL R-Spondin 1 (Peprotech, 120–38), 25 ng/mL FGF7 (Peprotech, 100–19), 100 ng/mL FGF10 (Peprotech, 100–26), 100 ng/mL Noggin (Peprotech, 120-10c), 500 nM A83-01 (Selleckchem, S7692), 10 μM Y-27632 (Sigma, SCM075), and 500 nM SB202190 (Selleckchem, S1077) to the basal medium.


### Isolation and culture of porcine lung organoids (Fig. 1A)
  Porcine lung tissue was obtained from piglets that have been euthanized by farm personnel for routine management purposes, independent of this study. Since the samples were collected post-mortem from animals not sacrificed specifically for research, this study did not require formal International Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) approval, in accordance with the IACUC guidelines of Seoul National University regarding the use of animal byproducts. After collection, the lung tissue was transferred to 10% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco) PBS (Gibco, 100100023) solution for transportation to the lab. After mechanical dissociation of the obtained lung tissue using scissors, tissues were incubated with Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (STEMCELL Technologies, 07174) for 30 min. Dissociation was terminated by dilution with the same volume of basal medium. The suspension was filtered through a 70 μm nylon mesh and centrifuged at 500 × g for 3 min. After removing the supernatant, 1 mL of RBC lysis buffer (Invitrogen, 00-433-57) was added to remove red blood cells. Cells were washed with 1 mL of basal medium, centrifuged, and resuspended in Matrigel (Corning, 356255). Matrigel domes were plated in 48-well plates (Corning, 3548) and overlaid with 350 μL of culture medium. Primary organoids were cultured for 1–2 weeks at 38℃ 5% CO2 incubator. Once visible, organoids were manually isolated using a mouth pipette with pulled glass capillary tube, dissociated with TrypLE Express (Gibco, 12605010) for 4 min, and passaged.

  For cryopreservation, organoid-containing domes were treated with Cell Recovery Solution (Corning, CLS354253) to remove Matrigel. After centrifugation and PBS wash, organoids were resuspended in 1 mL of CryoStor CS10 (STEMCELL Technologies, 07931) and frozen.


### Immunostaining
  For immunofluorescence staining, established PLOs were released from Matrigel using Cell Recovery Solution (Corning, CLS354253) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 20 min at room temperature. Fixed PLOs and porcine lung tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned. Sections were mounted on glass slides and incubated with anti-surfactant protein C (SP-C) antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-518029, AF546 conjugate), anti-aquaporin5 (AQP5) antibody (Abcam, ab317295), and anti-cytokeratin5 (KRT5) antibody (Abcam, ab193895). Slides were imaged and analyzed using a slide scanner (Carl Zeiss, Axio Scan.Z1).

  For immunohistochemical staining, sections were incubated with anti-SOX2 antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-365823), anti-α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) antibody (Invitrogen, 14-9760-82), and anti-vimentin antibody (Invitrogen, MA5-11883) followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and detection using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin.


### Guide RNA (gRNA) synthesis and vectors
  Porcine CFTR and CD163 targeting gRNAs were designed using an online tool (CRISPR RGEN tool; http://www.rgenome.net/) and synthesized using a precision gRNA synthesis kit (Invitrogen, A29377). Guide RNA for GFP knockout used in this study was identical to that described in our previous work[37]. The gRNA sequences used in this study are described in Supplementary Table 1.

  Vectors used in this study were identical to those described in our previous work [38]. Briefly, the PiggyBac transposon system vectors were obtained from Addgene (Plasmid #20960) and the Sanger Institute (Hinxton). The CAG-GFP vector was constructed using the In-Fusion® HD Cloning Kit (TAKARA, 639649). The prime editing (PE) plasmids used in this study were provided by the laboratory of Henry Kim (Yonsei University, Seoul, Republic of Korea).


### Organoid electroporation and GFP-guided enrichment (Fig. 1B)
  Organoid electroporation was conducted following previous research [39] with some modifications. Briefly, organoids were harvested by degrading Matrigel using Cell Recovery Solution, followed by centrifugation. The organoid pellet was then dissociated into single cells using TrypLE Express supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632 at 37 °C for 5–10 min and mechanically dissociated by pipetting. The reaction was quenched by adding cold basal medium supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632, and resuspended cells were filtered through 40 µm nylon mesh and then centrifuged. The cell pellet was washed twice by resuspending in 6 mL of cold Opti-MEM (Gibco, 31985062) and centrifuging under the same conditions. For electroporation, 10 μg of Cas9 RNP complex (2:1:1 ratio of Cas9 protein (Invitrogen, A36496) and 2 gRNAs) or 10 ug of DNA vector was prepared per reaction. Organoids were resuspended in 100 μL of Opti-MEM per reaction and transferred to an electroporation cuvette (NEPA GENE, NPG-EC-002S). Electroporation was performed under following conditions (Poring pulse: 175 V, 5 ms pulse, 50 ms interval, 2 pulses, 10% decay rate, polarity: + , Transfer pulse: 20 V, 50 ms pulse, 50 ms interval, 5 pulses, 40% decay rate, polarity: ± , Current limit: off) yielding an impedance between 25 and 55Ω using Super Electroporator NEPA21 Type II (NEPA Gene). Immediately after electroporation, 1 mL of basal medium containing 10 μM Y-27632 was added to the cuvette, and the cells were transferred to a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. Cells were allowed to recover at room temperature for 1 h, then centrifuged at 500xg for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in Matrigel and plated for further culture.

  For GFP-guided enrichment, the electroporated organoids were separated into two groups after 5–7 days of electroporation: GFP-positive (GFP( +)) and GFP-negative (GFP(-)). Total 2 selection was conducted for entire categorization.


### Establishment of Porcine embryonic fibroblast (PEF) and electroporation
  Porcine fetuses at embryonic day 20 (E20) were obtained from the uterus of slaughtered pigs. Skin tissue was dissected from the fetus using a surgical blade and minced into small pieces. The minced tissue was digested in 1 mL of 1U/mL Collagenase type I (Gibco, 17100017) in HBSS (Gibco, 14025092) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 4 h. Following digestion, the tissue was centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min, washed once with PBS, and cultured in DMEM (Cytiva, SH30243.01) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) at 37 °C and 5% CO₂.

  Cultured PEFs were used to evaluate gRNA activity for CFTR and CD163 gene editing. Electroporation was performed using the Neon Transfection System (Invitrogen), delivering 2 μg of Cas9 RNP complex (1:1 ratio of Cas9 protein and gRNA) per sample.


### Single organoid expansion (Fig. 1C)
  To minimize heterogeneity, organoids were dissociated into small clumps prior to single organoid expansion. Matrigel domes containing organoids were treated with Cell Recovery Solution to remove Matrigel, and the collected organoids were dissociated using TrypLE supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632. An equal volume of basal medium containing 10 μM Y-27632 was added to stop the reaction. The suspension was filtered through a 40 μm nylon mesh to remove large clumps, mixed with Matrigel, and plated as domes.

  After 5–6 days of culture at 38 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator, organoids with visible lumens were harvested using Cell Recovery Solution. Single organoids were isolated under a stereomicroscope and transferred to PCR tubes. Each organoid was treated with 20 μL of TrypLE containing 10 μM Y-27632 for 3 min, followed by addition of 200 μL basal medium and centrifugation. After removing the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in 5 μL Matrigel and plated into 96-well plates. Each well was then added with 100 μL culture medium and incubated under the same conditions.


### Genotyping
  Genomic DNA was extracted from organoids using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, 69504). To validate CFTR and CD163 genetic mutations, PCR was performed using i-StarTaq™ DNA polymerase (iNtRON Biotechnology, 25165). Mutation analysis was conducted by treating PCR products with T7 Endonuclease I (New England Biolabs, M0689). For sequencing-based analysis, library preparation was performed through three rounds of nested PCR. Primers used for PCR and library construction are listed in Supplementary Table 2. Deep sequencing was conducted using MiniSeq System (Illumina).


### cDNA sequencing
  Total RNA was extracted from organoids using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74104), and residual gDNA was removed using the RNase-Free DNase Set (Qiagen, 79254). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized from the extracted RNA using the RNA to cDNA EcoDry Premix (TAKARA, 639542). To analyze the gene-edited regions, the target sequences were amplified via PCR using the synthesized cDNA as a template, followed by Sanger sequencing.


### Western blotting
  Organoids were lysed using RIPA buffer, and equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST and incubated with a monoclonal anti-CFTR antibody (CF3, Invitrogen) diluted in 5% skim milk/TBST, followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated mouse secondary antibody. Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system.


### Protein structure prediction using AlphaFold2
  Protein structure prediction was performed using AlphaFold2. The wild-type CFTR amino acid sequence was obtained from the UniProt database (webstie), and the mutant sequence was derived from complementary DNA sequencing of CFTR mutated organoids and translated in silico. The resulting amino acid sequences were used as input for AlphaFold2 with default parameters. Predicted structures were evaluated based on the predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) confidence scores and visualized using PyMOL.


### Forskolin induced swelling (FIS) assay
  For the FIS assay, organoids were prepared by passaging 3 days before the analysis. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing 20 μM forskolin (MedChemExpress, HY-15371). Live-cell imaging was performed for 24 h using an EVOS M7000 imaging system maintained at 38 °C and 5% CO2.


### Statistical analysis
  All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.1 for Windows (GraphPad Software, USA, www.graphpad.com). To evaluate the significance of the enrichment of gene-editing efficiency between different groups, Fisher’s exact test (two-sided) was employed. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

### Establishment of PLO and transgenesis with GFP
  To establish PLOs, porcine lung tissue was harvested from piglets culled for routine management. Distal lung regions were isolated and cultured to establish primary PLOs (Fig. 1A). The organoids stably retained their characteristic morphology even after more than 20 passages, indicating sustained self-renewal capacity (Fig. 2A).   To characterize the cellular composition of the established organoids, immunostaining was performed and compared with native porcine lung tissue (Fig. 2C, D). The organoids exhibited expression of the AT2 cell-specific marker surfactant protein C (SP-C), indicating the presence of a distal alveolar epithelial population. Concurrently, the organoids expressed keratin 5 (KRT5), a marker of basal cells, together with the transcription factor SOX2 which is associated with proximal airway and epithelial progenitor identity. In contrast, aquaporin-5 (AQP5), a marker of terminally differentiated AT1 cells, was not detected, suggesting that differentiation toward the mature AT1 lineage was limited under the current culture conditions. Beyond these epithelial markers, vimentin immunoreactivity was observed within the organoids, indicating the persistence of a mesenchymal cell population. However, α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) was not detected, suggesting that this mesenchymal population did not include contractile myofibroblasts or smooth muscle cells. These results indicate that the established PLOs exhibited a multi-lineage characteristic, comprising both proximal and distal epithelial lineages together with a mesenchymal component.

  Next, to enable GFP-guided enrichment of gene-edited organoids, transgenesis of GFP was performed in the PLOs. Based on our previous study demonstrating that the PiggyBac (PB) transposon system allows long-term transgene expression without silencing [38, 40], the PB-CAG-GFP vector was delivered along with the Ef1α-Transposase vector into PLOs via electroporation (Supplementary Fig. 1A).

  Because organoids possess a multicellular structure, insufficient dissociation may result in heterogeneity and hinder efficient transfection. To address this, organoids were thoroughly dissociated using TrypLE dissociation buffer, followed by mechanical dissociation through repeated pipetting with a micropipette. To eliminate large cell clumps and ensure a uniform cell suspension, the dissociated organoids were filtered through a 40-μm nylon mesh prior to electroporation. Following electroporation, GFP-expressing organoids were identified under a fluorescence microscope. Individual GFP-positive (GFP( +)) organoids were manually isolated and expanded to establish a stable PLO line with 100% GFP expression (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. 1B).

  To validate the CFTR-edited organoids beyond the genomic level, extensive analysis was performed, including cDNA sequencing and western blotting. Although genetic mutations near the targeted start codon were confirmed at the cDNA level, CDS analysis revealed a compensatory mechanism involving the utilization of an alternative start codon downstream of the mutation site, which allowed for continued protein translation (Fig. 3G). Western blotting showed normal protein translation in CFTR-edited organoids, and protein structure prediction using AlphaFold indicated that the chloride channel and transmembrane regions of the mutant CFTR protein remained largely intact (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B) despite mutations in the N-terminal region. Subsequent functional validation using forskolin-induced swelling (FIS) assays demonstrated that CFTR-edited organoids retained normal chloride channel function, similar to that of the wild-type (Supplementary Fig. 4C, Supplementary Materials 3–8). These results confirmed that DNA to protein levels modifications were successfully induced. However, alternative translation initiation downstream of the original start codon led to the production of a functional protein isoform despite successful gene editing. To verify whether the gene-editing platform remained effective at clinically relevant loci, exon 11 of the CFTR gene, a region clinically significant for cystic fibrosis mutations, was targeted (Supplementary Fig. 4D). Following the same protocol as the previous experiments, sgRNAs targeting exon 11 and GFP were co-delivered as RNPs into GFP PLOs. Following this, the mutation efficiencies between the GFP( +) and GFP( −) groups were compared. The GFP( −) group exhibited a significantly higher gene-editing efficiency of 93.8%, whereas the GFP( +) group showed a considerably lower efficiency of 26.7% (P < 0.0000001; Supplementary Fig. 4E, Supplementary Table 3). These findings demonstrate that GFP-guided selection consistently yields high editing efficiency at clinically relevant loci, further proving the versatility of our system.

To identify an optimal pair of sgRNAs capable of inducing precise truncation at exon 11, four candidate sgRNAs flanking the target site were designed (Supplementary Fig. 5A). ## Discussion
  Organoids derived from ASCs offer physiologically relevant models owing to their tissue-specific maturity. However, in human disease-related research, ASC-derived organoids face significant limitations as a result of the low accessibility of human tissues, often being restricted to a limited number of obtainable samples or predominantly disease-derived sources. Furthermore, the multicellular structure and dependence on a supportive ECM of ASC-derived organoids pose complex challenges for efficient and uniform genome editing, thereby limiting the scope of functional genetic studies and disease modeling. To address these challenges, this study successfully established a long-term culture of ASC-derived PLOs for up to 22 passages for the first time, recapitulating the high physiological similarity between pigs and humans. To further maximize the utility of these models, we developed a robust gene-editing system by combining PiggyBac transposon-based GFP labeling, GFP-guided selection, and single-organoid culture.

  The PLOs established in this study demonstrated their potential as a sustainable bioresource, maintaining stability over 20 passages and surviving freeze–thaw cycles. Immunostaining revealed that these organoids constitute a multi-lineage population, comprising AT2 cells together with proximal airway/progenitor and mesenchymal characteristics, reflecting the cellular diversity retained from native porcine lung tissue. Additionally, by overcoming the traditional limitations of ASC-derived organoids, our approach achieved superior editing efficiency and homogeneity. Specifically, GFP-guided selection effectively enriched for successfully gene-edited cells as previously demonstrated in bovine fibroblasts [37], and subsequent clonal expansion through single-organoid culture led to the establishment of completely gene-edited organoids. We anticipate that the PLOs and the efficient gene-editing strategy established in this study will serve as a powerful tool for modeling human genetic diseases, as well as versatile functional tools for studying zoonotic infectious diseases and validating the efficacy of gene therapies.

  Completed gene-edited organoid clones were successfully generated through GFP-guided selection and single-organoid expansion. Although functional knockout of the CFTR protein was not achieved due to alternative translation initiation, multi-omics analyses confirmed that the intended genetic modifications were present at the DNA, mRNA, and protein levels. Importantly, structural modeling using AlphaFold2 (Supplementary Fig. 4B) supported this observation by predicting that the core functional domains of CFTR remained structurally intact despite N-terminal modifications. Given our success with various gene-editing strategies, which already confirmed that the GFP-guided selection system is highly effective across diverse and complex editing modalities, we anticipate that employing even more strategic approaches, such as simultaneously targeting multiple sites or focusing on critical functional domains, will lead to the successful production of definitive functional knockout organoids.

  To investigate whether our selection principle could be extended to more complex genome-editing tools beyond the standard CRISPR/Cas9 system, the effectiveness of the system was assessed using PE. As PE enables highly precise mutations and genetic modeling without double-strand breaks, its compatibility with our GFP-guided selection system is essential for establishing accurate disease models in ASC-derived organoids. Although PE efficiency in the enriched population remained relatively low, likely due to technical constraints such as suboptimal editor configuration or the challenges of delivering large plasmid vectors through the complex ECM of organoids, this outcome provided a crucial mechanistic insight. The GFP-guided selection system does not necessarily depend on the synchronized kinetics of nuclear gene-editing between the selection marker and the target editor. Instead, the loss of GFP fluorescence serves as a reliable indicator that the editing machinery, whether delivered as an RNP or a plasmid vector, has successfully bypassed the cell membrane and reached the intracellular environment. This implies that once both components are co-delivered into the same cell, the GFP( −) phenotype effectively enriches for cells that have received the editing tools, regardless of the delivery formats or subsequent editing mechanisms. Consequently, we anticipate that further optimization or the use of RNP-based delivery will enable the efficient production of PE organoid clones in future applications.

  Compared to previous strategies, our method offers distinct advantages in both editing efficiency and organoid viability. In terms of efficiency, our system overcomes the inherent limitations of manual picking [7] and plasmid-based co-transfection [42]. Manual picking not only lacks the selectivity required for precise isolation of gene-edited organoids but is also labor-intensive, often resulting in low reproducibility. Furthermore, methods that rely on the co-transfection of selection markers via plasmid vectors, or knock-in donor vectors, are frequently constrained by the lower delivery efficiency of plasmids compared to RNP systems. In contrast, our RNP-based selection system ensures that both the selection marker and the editing machinery are delivered with similarly high efficiency, thereby overcoming delivery bottlenecks and achieving superior enrichment of edited clones. Beyond efficiency, our system uniquely addresses the physiological and temporal limitations associated with conventional sorting methods. Antibiotic selection [1], such as puromycin treatment, can directly impair stem cell viability and disrupt the extracellular environment (e.g., Matrigel) through the accumulation of necrotic debris, which hinders proper organoid development. Similarly, although fluorescently tagged gene editors can be used for FACS-based selection [43], the transient nature of RNP expression, typically lasting only a few days, necessitates sorting at a very early stage. This timing often imposes significant cellular stress on organoids before they have sufficiently matured, thereby limiting the types of organoid models that can be successfully established [44]. By contrast, the GFP-guided selection system developed in this study provides a non-invasive alternative that enables continuous selection over multiple passages without subjecting organoids to such acute physical and physiological stress. By minimizing editing-induced stress, this method offers a highly versatile tool which can be applied to a wide range of organoid types. This feature makes it particularly well-suited for functional modeling (e.g., disease modeling or viral infection), where maintaining high physiological fidelity is crucial. Consequently, our GFP-based selection system facilitates highly reproducible gene editing in ASC-derived organoids, effectively reducing heterogeneity and preserving stem cell viability throughout the entire editing workflow.

  Despite these advancements, some limitations remain. The PLOs established in this study exhibited a multi-lineage cellular identity, retaining both proximal and distal epithelial progenitor characteristics together with a minor mesenchymal population, likely reflecting the cellular diversity inherent to the primary lung tissue from which they were derived. Notably, a similarly multi-lineage epithelial identity has been reported in murine bronchioalveolar lung organoids [45]. Furthermore, human alveolar progenitors have been shown to retain phenotypic lineage plasticity toward bronchial cell-type progeny, including KRT5-expressing basal-like cells, even in adult stem cell-derived organoid culture [46]. These previous findings indicate that a mixed proximal–distal epithelial identity can arise from lung-derived progenitor populations, consistent with the multi-lineage phenotype observed in the present PLOs. While these unique characteristics may broaden the scope of lung biology that can be modeled using this platform, it also indicates that the current PLOs do not represent a fully resolved, lineage-restricted alveolar model. Furthermore, the absence of detectable AQP5 expression suggests that differentiation into mature, functional AT1 epithelial cells has not yet been achieved. Therefore, studies, such as lineage-specific sorting or refinement of cultured conditions, are required to evaluate the capacity of PLOs to perform higher, lineage-specific physiological functions. Additionally, the applicability of our method to other types of ASC-derived organoids remains to be confirmed. However, we anticipate that this strategy could be successfully adapted to various organoid systems, given the underlying mechanisms identified in this study.

  This study represents the first report of gene-edited organoids derived from porcine ASCs. As pigs are increasingly utilized in diverse research areas, such as xenotransplantation [19–21], genetic disorders [16, 17, 24, 47–49], and the production of disease-resistant livestock [41, 50, 51], we anticipate a surge in research employing gene-edited porcine organoids. Notably, because certain conditions, such as CFTR-mutation-induced cystic fibrosis, exhibit phenotypes in pigs [24, 25] but not in rodents, pigs serve as a superior comparative medicine model. Owing to their greater genetic and physiological similarity to humans [14, 23], particularly regarding the conservation of airway receptor profiles and lung architecture, PLOs and the gene-editing platform established in this study will not only serve as models for livestock and zoonotic infectious diseases, including influenza [52] and coronaviruses [53], but also as essential translational models for recapitulating complex lung pathologies and validating advanced gene therapies.


## Conclusion
  In conclusion, we established ASC-derived multi-lineage PLOs encompassing alveolar epithelium for the first time and developed a GFP-guided enrichment strategy to enhance genome-editing efficiency in ASC-derived organoids. By combining this enrichment method with single-organoid culture, we successfully generated gene-edited organoids with high homogeneity. Furthermore, we demonstrated the technical versatility of our system by applying it to more sophisticated genome-editing approaches, such as dual gRNA-mediated truncation and PE. Our findings suggest that this system serves as a robust translational medicine platform, enabling the recapitulation of human diseases and advancing research in both livestock and biomedical sciences.

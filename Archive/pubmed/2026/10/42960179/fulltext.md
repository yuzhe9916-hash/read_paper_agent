# CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knock-out of abnormal chemosensory jump 6 (acj6) gene impacts Helicoverpa armigera antennal lobe structure


## Highlights
  •CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knock-out of Haacj6 in Helicoverpa armigera results in a significant increase in the volume of the antennal lobe (AL), with no overt morphological alterations to the overall brain structure.•The number of AL glomeruli is elevated from 75 in wild-type to approximately 100 in Haacj6 mutant.•Haacj6 mutant exhibit broader variability in glomerular volume compared to wild-type, while the mean glomerular volume remains unchanged.•Haacj6 is an essential regulator of antennal lobe precise structure and insect olfactory neural circuit formation.

## Introduction
  Olfaction plays essential roles in insect survival by guiding key behaviors such as feeding, mating, oviposition and avoiding predators and pathogens (Mansourian and Stensmyr, 2015). Each of these behaviors is triggered by distinct sets of odors, with its specificity determined by the specialized olfactory pathways that detect them. In insects, these odor cues are captured by different odorant receptor neurons (ORNs) housed in olfactory sensilla on the antennae and other head appendages; each ORN expresses one or a few odorant receptors (ORs) that mediate odor detection (Hansson and Stensmyr, 2011; Martin et al., 2013). The odor signals are sent by ORN axons to the primary olfactory center, antennal lobe (AL), which consists of many glomeruli (Homberg et al., 1989; Hansson and Anton, 2000). From each glomerulus, the synaptic targets of ORNs, projection neurons (PNs), send information to higher olfactory brain centers (e.g., lateral horn), where, behavioral decisions are formed (Homberg et al., 1989; Galizia and Rössler, 2010; Rybak et al., 2016; Someya et al., 2025).

  Beyond odor detection itself, the specificity of the olfactory system is also determined by the neural circuits via which sensory information is routed (Yan et al., 2020; Mika and Benton, 2021). Glomeruli in the AL are the basic units for processing olfactory information, composed of complex neural circuits. A defining feature of the insect AL is the remarkable stereotypy of its glomerular architecture: within a species, the number, size, shape, and spatial organization of glomeruli are highly conserved among individuals (Kazawa et al., 2009; Vicker and Christensen, 2003), and conserved organizational principles are shared across insect taxa (Hildebrand and Shepherd, 1997; Galizia and Rössler, 2010). This invariant glomerular map is essential for reliable olfactory processing and provides a sensitive anatomical readout for detecting genetic perturbations that disrupt olfactory circuit development or maintenance (Jefferis et al., 2002; Komiyama and Luo, 2006). The glomerulus is composed of several types of neurons, and glomerular formation is regulated by multiple factors (Oland and Tolbert, 1996). ORN axons are crucial for glomerular formation and stability in insects (Oland et al., 1990; Tissot et al., 1997). In the absence of ORNs, the AL may still develop, albeit with reduced volume; however, glomeruli fail to form (Trebels et al., 2021). Furthermore, glial cells generally form the borders of glomeruli and play important roles in maintaining glomerular stability (Tolbert and Oland, 1990; Baumann et al., 1996; Tolbert et al., 2004). Multiple factors, including several transcription factors (TFs), are involved in regulating the formation of glomeruli. For instance, the TFs atonal and amos regulate glomerular formation by controlling ORN subtype specificity, development and targeting (Okumura et al., 2016); whereas ten-m and ten-a modulate glomerular formation by instructing the specificity of synaptic matching between ORNs and PNs (Hong and Luo, 2014; Yan et al., 2020). The POU‑domain TF acj6 has emerged as an important regulator that links OR gene choice with axonal and dendritic targeting (Komiyama et al., 2003, 2004; Komiyama and Luo, 2007; Bai and Carlson, 2010; Jafari et al., 2012; Li et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2025). In Drosophila, the TF acj6 is expressed broadly across antennal and maxillary palp ORNs as well as in a subset of PNs, and functions throughout development (from larval to pupal, and adult stages) to promote the expression of specific OR genes while repressing others (Clyne et al., 1999; Komiyama et al., 2003, 2004; Bai et al., 2009; Bai and Carlson, 2010). Genetic disruption of acj6 leads to both mis-expression of ORs and mistargeting of ORN and PN projections, highlighting its dual role in specifying sensory identity and wiring logic (Komiyama and Luo, 2007; Xie et al., 2022). Comparable functions are observed in lepidopteran species, Bombyx mori, where acj6 homologs regulate OR gene expression and shape the arborization of projection neurons that mediate pheromone processing (Fujii et al., 2011; Namiki et al., 2017).

  Recently, the acj6 gene was also identified in the cotton bollworm, Helicoverpa armigera (Xie et al., 2025). As a member of the family Noctuidae (Lepidoptera), H. armigera ranks among the most destructive agricultural pests globally, resulting in severe damage to cotton, corn, vegetables, and other economically important crops (Wu et al., 2008; Jones et al., 2019). To develop olfaction-based and eco-friendly pest control strategies, extensive research efforts have been devoted to the structure of its olfactory system, chemical signal perception, and the molecular mechanisms underlying olfaction (Liu et al., 2022, 2023; Fan et al., 2022). Despite these advances, the regulatory mechanisms governing the development and functional modulation of the olfactory system in H. armigera remain largely elusive. In particular, it remains unclear whether the TF acj6 is implicated in olfactory processes in this species, and what specific regulatory role it may exert in these pathways. In the present study, we generated acj6 knock-out mutant of H. armigera using the CRISPR/Cas9 genome-editing system. Our neuroanatomical analyses demonstrated that both the absolute and relative volumes of the AL were significantly increased in the acj6 mutants compared with wild-type moths. On this basis, we further investigated the number and spatial organization of glomeruli within the AL to elucidate the specific role of acj6 in the development of the olfactory neural circuit in H. armigera.


## Materials and methods

### Insect rearing
  The H. armigera moths were collected in a cotton field in Xinxiang, Henan Province, China in 2018. The larvae of H. armigera were reared on an artificial diet in a climate-controlled incubator at 27 ± 2°C, 75 ± 5 % relative humidity, with a 16: 8 h (light: dark) photoperiod. Adults were provided with a 10 % (v/v) honey solution (Wang et al., 2025).


### Synthesis of sgRNA and CRISPR/Cas9
  The Haacj6 gene sequences were retrieved from NCBI under accession numbers XM_049846298.2 and XM_021339138.3. The Haacj6 transcript sequence was validated by alignment against the reference H. armigera genomic sequence (NCBI Gene ID: LOC110379464). Two sgRNAs targeting the Haacj6 gene were designed using the CRISPR/Cas9 design tool on the CRISPOR website (http://crispor.tefor.net/). The target sequences for sgRNA-Cas9 (ATAAGATGAAGTCCGGCGGC and ACGTCGGGCTGTAGCGCCCC) were selected within exon 1 (Supplementary Fig. S1A). The specificity of these sgRNA target sequences was verified by searching against the H. armigera genome and the GenBank database. The sgRNAs were synthesized in vitro by GenScript (Nanjing, China). The Cas9 protein was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Shanghai, China).


### Egg collection and microinjection
  Freshly laid eggs (within 2 h post-oviposition) were collected, surface-sterilized with 1 % (v/v) sodium hypochlorite solution, and rinsed thoroughly with distilled water. The eggs were then aligned on a microscope slide and immobilized using double-sided adhesive tape (Wang et al., 2016).

  Approximately 2.5 nL of a mixture containing the two sgRNAs and Cas9 protein was injected into individual eggs using a Nanoject III microinjector (Drummond Scientific). The final concentration of each sgRNA was 300 ng/µL, and the Cas9 protein concentration was 300 ng/µL. All microinjections were completed within 2 h. After injection, the eggs were incubated under the same environmental conditions as described above. After eclosion, the G0 adults were screened for Haacj6 mutations.


### Genomic DNA extraction and mutant screening
  Primers flanking the CRISPR target sites were designed and used to PCR-amplify the target region from genomic DNA of individual insects. Genomic DNA was extracted using an EasyPure Genomic DNA Kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The PCR reaction mixture (30 µL total volume) consisted of 15 µL of 2× EasyTaq PCR Mix, 1.0 µL of each forward and reverse primer (10 µM), 2 µL of DNA template, and 11 µL of DEPC-treated water. The PCR protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 94°C for 5 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 30 s, annealing at 52°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 35 s; followed by a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. The PCR products were sent to Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) for Sanger sequencing. Sequencing chromatograms were analyzed using Chromas 2, and the sequences were aligned using DNAMAN 6.0 (Supplementary Fig. S1B). The confirmed G0 mutant adults were subsequently used for immunocytochemistry experiments.


### Immunocytochemistry
  Immunocytochemistry was performed to visualize the glomerular structures within the AL, following previously established methods (Zhao et al., 2016a, 2016b). The brains were carefully dissected from the head and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 2 h. After fixation, the samples were washed with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with 5 % normal goat serum (NGS) for 24 h. The brains were then incubated with primary antibody SYNORF1 (obtained from the Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, University of Iowa, Iowa city) for 5 days at 4 °C. Following PBS washes, the samples were incubated with a Cy2-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody (Invitrogen, Eugene, OR, USA) for 3 days at 4 °C. After additional washes, the brains were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and mounted in methyl salicylate.


### Confocal image acquisition and digital three-dimensional reconstruction
  Transparent brains were scanned by using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 510, META Zeiss, Jena, Germany). Cy2 fluorescence was excited using the 488 nm line of an argon laser. The confocal images were obtained with a resolution of 1024×1024 pixels at intervals of 3 μm. Amira software (Amira 5.3, Visage Imaging, Fürth, Germany) was employed to label and reconstruct neuropils in the brain and glomeruli in the AL. The volumes of neuropils and glomeruli were measured using the Tissue Statistics module in Amira, and the quantitative data were imported into Microsoft Excel for further analysis.


### Neuropils and glomerular nomenclature
  Neuropils in the brain were named according to the suggestion in Drosophila brain by the insect brain name working group (Ito et al., 2014). The glomeruli in the AL were identified and named based on previous studies in H. armigera (Zhao et al., 2016a, 2016b). In the present study, only a subset of glomeruli was selected for detailed analysis, as many could not be reliably identified due to ambiguous morphological boundaries.


### Statistical analysis
  The volumes of the brain and each individual glomerulus were measured and calculated based on reconstruction results. The relative volume of a neuropile was defined as the ration of its volume to the brain volume, and the relative volume of a given glomerulus was defined as the ration of its volume to the total glomerular volume. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A two-tailed independent-samples t-test was used to compare the volumes of the corresponding glomeruli between the two genotypes. A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess the main effects of sex and genotype, as well as their interaction, on the number of AL glomeruli. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0).


## Results

### H. armigera Haacj6 mutant exhibited enlarged antennal lobes in male
  Immunostaining with the anti-synapsin antibody SYNORF1 delineated the neuropil architecture of the brain in both wild-type (male n = 6, female n = 7) and Haacj6 mutant (male n = 14, female n = 9) of H. armigera (Fig. 1A). The whole brain was subdivided into six discrete neuropil regions, namely AL, optic lobe (OL), anterior optic tubercle (AOTU), mushroom body (MB), central body (CB), and midbrain (Fig. 1B–D). Morphologically, these neuropil regions exhibited no discernible differences between wild-type and mutant in males. Quantitative analysis further revealed comparable volumes across all these regions between the two strains, with the exception of the AL (Fig. 1E). The AL volume in the Haacj6 mutant (51.21 ± 4.06 105 µm3) was significantly larger than that in wild-type individuals (33.08 ± 3.83 105µm3) (P≤0.001) (Fig. 1E). In terms of relative volume, the AL was significantly larger in the mutant than in wild-type individuals (wild type: 2.88 ± 0.31 %; mutant: 4.17 ± 0.36 %; P=0.001), whereas the OL showed a slight opposite trend (wild type: 16.93 ± 0.53 %; mutant: 14.33 ± 0.93 %; P=0.006) (Fig. 1F). ### The number of antennal lobe glomeruli was significantly increased in H. armigera Haacj6 mutant
  Despite the volume change, immunostaining results demonstrated that the ALs of the Haacj6 mutant exhibited a glomerular layer architecture comparable to those of wild-type individuals in both sexes (Figs. 2 & 3). Following the reconstruction protocol of Zhao et al. (2016a, 2016b), all glomeruli were individually identified and rendered into three-dimensional models (Fig. 2B & D; Fig. 3B & D; Fig. 4A–D). Although no sexual dimorphism in glomerular number was detected across genotypes (F1,32 = 0.102, p = 0.752), mutants showed a pronounced increased in the number of glomeruli compared with the wild-type (F1,32 = 59.798, p < 0.001). Specifically, wild-type males possessed 75.00 ± 1.67 glomeruli per AL (mean ± SD, n = 6, range 73 – 77), whereas mutants displayed 98.36 ± 7.96 (n = 14, range 80 – 109, Fig. 4E), revealing a marked Haacj6 - dependent increase. A similar pattern was observed in females, with wild-type showing 73.86 ± 0.83 glomeruli (mean ± SD, n = 7, range 72 – 75), and mutant showing 99.11 ± 13.86 per AL (mean ± SD, n = 9, range of 89 – 122 across individuals), with the mutant displaying a higher glomerular number (Fig. 4F). From the above results, it can be seen that the change in the number of AL glomeruli in females after Haacj6 knockout was greater than that in males. ### The volume of antennal lobe glomeruli displayed a broader variation in H. armigera Haacj6 mutant
  The greatly elevated variance of glomerular number in the mutant suggests Haacj6 plays a stabilizing role in the developmental processes that define AL glomerular organization. To assess whether this stabilizing role also extends to glomerular volume, the absolute and relative volumes of each glomerulus were obtained from three-dimensional reconstruction. A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess the main effects of sex and genotype, as well as their interaction. Overall, the Haacj6 mutation leads to an increase in total glomerular volume (mutant vs. wild-type: t = 4.302, df = 25.590, p < 0.001). In this context, genotype, not sex, was the major driver of the volumetric change, as the total volume of glomeruli within each AL did not differ between males and females (t = 1.385, df = 28.716, p = 0.177). To determine whether the mutation also expands variation in glomerular size, systematic volumetric parameters were compared across sexes and genotypes, including the maximum and minimum volume, mean glomerular volume (in both absolute and relative terms), and the coefficient of variation (CoV) across glomeruli within the AL. In summary, these comparisons revealed that within each sex, mutants consistently showed broader size ranges and greater total volume: larger maximums, smaller minimums, higher CoV, and unchanged mean volume (males: higher total, higher max, lower min, higher CoV; females: same pattern).   With respect to the sex factor, neither the mean nor the minimum differed significantly in either absolute (mean: t = 0.704, df = 34, p = 0.486; minimum: t = 1.064, df = 34, p = 0.295) or relative volumes (mean: t = 0.891, df = 34, p = 0.379; minimum: t = 1.199, df = 34, p = 0.239). By contrast, the maximum exhibited a significant sexual difference in both absolute (t = 7.009, df = 23.32, p < 0.001) and relative volume (t = 11.371, df = 32.056, p < 0.001). Regarding genotypes, significant differences were identified for the minimum (t = 5.455, df = 1.386, p < 0.001) and maximum glomeruli (t = 2.967, df = 33.383, p = 0.005), whereas the mean value showed no statistical difference in the absolute volumes (t = 0.326, df = 27.938, p = 0.747). In relative volumes, both the minimum (t = 6.224, df = 14.167, p < 0.001) and mean value (t = 12.110, df = 25.136, p < 0.001) differed significantly between genotypes, while the maximum showed no difference (t = 1.618, df = 34, p = 0.115). Additionally, the CoV of glomerular volume differed significantly between the two sexes (t = 5.839, df = 34, p < 0.001) and between genotypes (t = 4.788, df = 34, p < 0.001). These results indicated that genotype is a major factor contributing to the increased volume of AL glomeruli in the mutant.

  In males, the Haacj6 mutant exhibited a wider range of glomerular volume compared to the wild-type (Fig. 5A & B). The total glomerular volume of the wild-type males was significantly smaller than that of the mutant (wild type: 2.30 ± 0.10 106 μm3; mutant: 3.41 ± 0.75 106 μm3) (p <0.001) (Fig. 5C). The maximal glomerular volume in mutant was significantly larger than that in the wild-type (wild type: 2.02 ± 0.18 105 μm3; mutant: 3.05 ± 0.74 105 μm3) (p=0.004) (Fig. 5D). Conversely, the minimum glomerular volume in the mutant was significantly smaller than that in the wild-type (wild type: 4.67 ± 0.84 103 μm3; mutant: 2.56 ± 0.59 103 μm3) (p <0.001) (Fig. 5E). Similarly, in the relative volume, the largest glomerulus (wild type: 8.79 ± 0.92 %; mutant: 9.78 ± 2.12 %) (p =0.161) was larger and the smallest (wild type: 0.25 ± 0.11 %; mutant: 0.09 ± 0.05 %) (p <0.001) was smaller in the mutant, even though the difference between genotypes was not significant for the largest (Fig. 5F & G). The CoV of glomerular volume was lower in the wild-type than that in the mutant (wild type: 0.62 - 0.83; mutant: 0.86 - 1.53) (p <0.001) (Fig. 5H). These results confirmed that the glomerular volume in the Haacj6 mutant exhibited greater variability than in the wild-type. Notably, the mean volume of glomeruli was not significantly different between the two genotypes (wild type: 3.06 ± 0.15 104 μm3; mutant: 3.15 ± 0.79 104 μm3) (p =0.685) (Fig. 5I). These results demonstrated a correlation between increased glomerular number and total volume in the mutant.

  Consistent with the male results, the mutant showed a broader range of glomerular volume than wild-type females (Fig. 6A & B). The total glomerular volume of wild-type was slightly smaller than that of the mutant (wild type: 2.31 ± 0.21 106 μm3; mutant: 2.79 ± 0.39 106 μm3) (p =0.081) (Fig. 6C). The maximal volume in the mutant was significantly larger than that in the wild-type (wild type: 0.73 ± 0.07 105 μm3; mutant: 1.15 ± 0.36 105 μm3) (p =0.008) (Fig. 6D). Meanwhile, the minimum glomerular volume in mutant was significantly smaller than that in the wild-type (wild type: 5.98 ± 1.56 103 μm3; mutant: 2.46 ± 0.40 103 μm3) (p ≤0.001) (Fig. 6E). In relative volume, the largest glomerulus (wild type: 3.17 ± 0.30 %; mutant: 3.47 ± 0.25 %) (p =0.065) was larger and the smallest (wild type: 0.26 ± 0.08 %; mutant: 0.10 ± 0.05 %) (p ≤0.001) was smaller in the mutant, even though the difference between genotypes was not significant for the largest (Fig. 6F & G). Furthermore, the CoV of glomerular volume was significantly higher in the mutant than in the wild-type, confirming the greater variability in mutant glomerular volume (wild type: 0.38 - 0.43; mutant: 0.58 – 0.89) (p <0.001) (Fig. 6H). Similar to males, the mean volume of glomeruli was not significantly different between the two genotypes (wild type: 3.13 ± 0.26 104 μm3; mutant: 2.88 ± 0.61 104 μm3) (p =0.323) (Fig. 6I). This result further supports the correlation between increased glomerular number and total volume in the Haacj6 mutant.

### Both phenotypic variation and stability were detected in the antennal lobe glomeruli of Haacj6 mutant
  In wild-type insects, AL glomeruli are generally consistent in shape, size, and spatial arrangement across individuals. In contrast, both male and female Haacj6 mutants exhibited substantial variations in glomerular morphology and position. Consequently, only a few glomeruli in the mutant could be reliably identified and matched to their wild-type counterparts. In males, three glomeruli, the cumulus (CU), the anterior dorsomedial unit (DMA), and the posterior dorsomedial unit (DMP), were consistently recognizable (Fig. 8A-I). These units, located most laterally in the AL, adjacent to the antennal nerve entry point, collectively form the macroglomerular complex (MGC). Although the overall arrangement of the MGC was comparable between wild-type and mutant individuals, the CU unit exhibited notable morphological variability in the mutant (Fig. 8A-I). Quantitative analysis across genotypes revealed that the absolute volume of the MGC was larger in the mutant (wild type: 2.85 ± 0.23 105 μm3; mutant: 3.77 ± 1.24 105 μm3) (p =0.017), whereas its relative volume did not differ between genotypes (wild type: 12.41 ± 1.14 %; mutant: 12.17 ± 2.18 %) (p =0.754) (Fig. 8J). At the level of individual MGC units, the volume scaling patterns varied. The largest glomerulus, CU, showed an increased absolute volume in the mutant (wild type: 2.02 ± 0.18 105 μm3; mutant type: 3.06 ± 1.15 105 μm3) (p <0.001), while its relative volume remained unchanged (wild type: 8.79 ± 0.92 %; mutant type: 9.79 ± 2.11 %) (p =0.156) (Fig. 8K). ## Discussion
  In the present study, the Haacj6 mutant exhibited a notable increase in the AL glomerular number relative to the wild-type H. armigera, pointing to a previously unrecognized role of Haacj6 in the regulating AL compartmentalization. This phenotype contrasts with findings in D. melanogaster and B. mori, where loss of acj6 does not alter glomerular number (Clyne et al., 1999; Komiyama et al., 2003, 2004; Namiki et al., 2017). Across insects, the number of AL glomeruli is generally highly conserved within each taxon and shows little intraspecific variation (Schachtner et al., 2005). For instance, fruit flies and mosquitoes possess approximately 50 glomeruli, parasitoid wasps around 200, and moths and butterflies 60 – 80 (Zhao et al., 2016a, 2016b). This stability is largely attributed to the genetically determined repertoire of olfactory receptor genes, as olfactory receptor neurons expressing the same receptor typically converge onto a specific glomerulus, resulting in a conserved one-receptor–one-glomerulus organizational pattern (Hallem et al., 2004; Couto et al., 2005; Dong et al., 2020; Tang et al., 2024; Benton et al., 2025). Nevertheless, substantial increases in glomerular number have evolved in certain insect lineages, such as ants (100 – 600 glomeruli), bees (100 – 210) and locusts (> 1000). These increases are generally associated with lineage-specific expansions of olfactory receptor gene families and the evolution of complex olfactory demands, such as sophisticated chemical communication in social insects or enhanced detection of diverse environmental odor cues. Unlike social insects or other lineages with naturally expanded AL repertoires, H. armigera does not exhibit an inherent evolutionary propensity for increased glomerular numbers. Thus, the extra glomeruli observed in the Haacj6 mutant most plausibly reflect a direct role of Haacj6 in AL development. In D. melanogaster and B. mori, where loss of acj6, where some ORs gene expression in ORNs were reduce or absent, the number of glomeruli per AL did not change, which indicated that glomerular number was determined independently by ORs expression (Jefferis et al., 2004; Sakuma et al., 2014; Namiki et al., 2017). However, in H. armigera, further investigation is required to determine whether the Haacj6 regulates ORs expression and whether ORs determine the number of glomeruli per AL.

  Formation of AL glomeruli relies on multiple cellular components, including ORNs, PNs, and glial cells. The number of glomeruli per AL is determined by ORN, PN, glial cells and ORN – PN matching. The increased number of glomeruli per AL in H. armigera may have originated from glomerular fragmentation, whereby ORN cohorts expressing the same identity fail to merge into a single PN‑defined compartment, thereby giving rise to multiple smaller, partially segregated “sub-glomeruli”. In the absence of ORNs, the AL can form but remains reduced in volume and fails to segregate into discrete glomeruli across diverse insects, including Manduca sexta, Apis mellifera, Periplaneta americana, and D. melanogaster (Trebels et al., 2021). Furthermore, ORN axons are known to induce glomerular formation and are required for their stabilization (Tolbert and Sirianni, 1990; Salecker and Boeckh, 1996). The dendrites of PNs prepattern the glomerular shape before the arrival of ORN axons in D. melanogaster (Jefferis et al., 2004). In the D. melanogaster acj6 mutant, wiring specificity is compromised: subsets of ORNs (e.g., OR43a, OR47a and OR83c) form ectopic arborizations and rarely project to their correct glomerular targets, whereas others (e.g., OR59c, OR71a and GR21a) form ectopic arborizations in addition to projecting to their correct glomerular targets in almost all cases (Komiyama et al., 2004). PNs innervating DL1, VA1lm, VA3, and VM7 glomeruli in D. melanogaster acj6 mutant exhibit diffuse, unfocused dendritic arbors (Komiyama et al., 2003). Similarly, dendritic spillover and reduction have also been observed in PNs of the B. mori acj6 mutant (Namiki et al., 2017). Cross-species evidence indicates that acj6-dependent mistargeting of ORN axons and PN dendrites may likewise be induced by Haacj6, the orthologous regulator in H. armigera, consistent with the results reported here. Hence, misrouted ORN axons or broadened PN dendritic fields might result in creating additional, anatomically discrete arborization sites that are established as glomerulus-like units in H. armigera. Moreover, non-neuronal cells such as glia also contribute to AL glomerular development. Glial cells form the boundary of the glomeruli and play important role in maintaining stability of the glomeruli (Oland et al., 2008). In M. sexta, glial cells proliferate, extend processes, and migrate between newly formed protoglomeruli, right after the initial entry of ORN axons into the AL (Oland et al., 1996). In glia-deficient ALs, protoglomeruli initially form but subsequently disintegrate due to insufficient boundary stabilization (Tolbert and Oland, 1990; Baumann et al., 1996). In H. armigera Hacj6 mutant, the boundaries between glomeruli were more blurred that of the wild type. Reason for the increase in the number of glomeruli might be the blurred boundaries. Diffuse PN dendrites and/or altered glial scaffolding weakening glomerular borders, leading to fuzzy or misplaced internal partitions rather than true increases in glomerulus number.

  In wild-type insects, the shape, size, and location of each glomerulus are highly stereotyped within a given species and sex (Rospars, 1988). Interestingly, despite the widespread structural changes in glomerular organization caused by the Haacj6 mutation, three subsets of functionally specialized glomeruli remain identifiable, the male‑specific MGC, CO2-specific LPOG, and a small group of posterior glomeruli. Within the MGC, the CU glomeruli are enlarged in the Haacj6 mutant compared with the wild-type, a feature also observed in the male B. mori acj6 mutant, where the toroid and CU units also showed increased volume (Namiki et al., 2017). Another functional unit, the LPOG, also remain identifiable. This unit is ventrally localized in the AL and receives input from labial palp ORNs that express the gustatory receptors (GRs), GR1, GR2 and GR3, which mediate carbon dioxide detection (Chen et al., 2025). Beyond the function-specific glomeruli, several posterior glomeruli, G36, G37, G53, G70, G73 could be reliably identified in both wild-type and mutant of H. armigera. Although the receptor types within these glomeruli have not been characterized in moths, their homologs in D. melanogaster receive inputs from ionotropic receptor (IR)-expressing sensory neurons. These patterns suggest that the receptor identity of the corresponding sensory neuron may be relevant to their robustness to structural disruption. In particular, GR- and IR-positive sensory neurons in the antenna of H. armigera may be less dependent on Haacj6 for proper axon targeting, which could explain why glomeruli receiving these inputs remain identifiable in the mutant.


## Conclusion
  This study shows that the Haacj6 mutation profoundly reshapes the architecture of the antennal lobe, altering glomerular structure, increasing glomerular number, and shifting glomerular volume. These broad developmental defects indicate that the gene is essential for the correct assembly of olfactory circuits, a process with strong consequences for odor processing and ultimately for survival, since inaccurate sensory wiring can compromise the interpretation of ecologically relevant cues. In contrast, glomeruli that might receive input from GR and IR expressing sensory neurons remain largely unaffected, suggesting that these receptors defined pathways rely less on Haacj6- dependent mechanisms or may follow partially distinct developmental programs. The similarity of these phenotypes across insect species demonstrates that the role of acj6 is deeply conserved, not only within Lepidoptera but also in Diptera and likely in additional lineages. Together, these findings highlight acj6 and its orthologs as key regulators of olfactory system development and as central contributors to the evolutionary stability of olfactory map organization across insects.

# Genome-wide CRISPR screens identify shared and context-specific regulators of podocyte resistance to oxidative injury


## Abstract
  Podocyte injury is a convergent driver of glomerular disease progression, yet the genetic determinants that govern podocyte vulnerability to oxidative stress remain incompletely defined. Here, we performed genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screens in differentiated human podocytes exposed to two complementary injury models: Adriamycin (ADR), a clinically relevant inducer of podocyte damage, and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), a direct oxidative stress challenge. Human podocytes were transduced with a genome-wide sgRNA library, differentiated under non-permissive conditions and subjected to optimized stress conditions that imposed selective pressure while preserving library complexity. Across five independent replicates per condition, sequencing-based sgRNA profiling demonstrated high reproducibility, balanced library representation and clear treatment-specific selection. The ADR screen identified 819 depleted and 456 enriched genes, whereas the H2O2 screen identified 277 depleted and 215 enriched genes. Integrated analysis revealed both model-specific dependencies and 27 shared depleted genes across the two injury conditions, implicating stress adaptation, chromatin regulation, mitochondrial metabolism, inflammatory signaling and cytoskeletal organization as candidate processes required for podocyte survival. These genome-wide screens provide a functional framework for prioritizing genes that regulate podocyte resilience to oxidative injury and offer a resource for mechanistic studies of glomerular disease.


## Introduction
  Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects nearly 10% of the global population, imposing a substantial and growing public health burden worldwide [1]. Despite its diverse etiologies, podocyte injury and loss represent a convergent pathway driving the progression of most forms of CKD [2].

  Podocytes are highly specialized, terminally differentiated cells that constitute a critical component of the glomerular filtration barrier. Through their interdigitating foot processes and slit diaphragm complexes, podocytes maintain selective permeability and prevent protein leakage into the urine [3]. Notably, podocytes exhibit minimal regenerative capacity under physiological conditions, and their limited ability to proliferate renders them particularly vulnerable to cumulative injury [4]. Although compensatory mechanisms, such as parietal epithelial cell differentiation, have been proposed, these processes are insufficient to restore podocyte number and function in the context of sustained injury [5].

  Loss of podocytes leads to denudation of the glomerular basement membrane, promoting maladaptive adhesion, segmental sclerosis, and ultimately irreversible decline in kidney function [6]. Clinically, podocyte injury is a hallmark of multiple glomerular diseases, including focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), diabetic kidney disease, hypertensive nephropathy, and minimal change disease [7]. Podocytes are continuously exposed to a hostile microenvironment characterized by high intraglomerular pressure, mechanical stress, metabolic perturbations (such as hyperglycemia and lipotoxicity), immune-mediated injury, and circulating toxins [8,9]. The combination of high metabolic demand and limited regenerative capacity places podocytes at exceptional risk of injury, making them a central bottleneck in kidney disease progression.

  Experimentally, Adriamycin (ADR) has been widely used to model podocyte injury both in vitro and in vivo, recapitulating key features of human FSGS [10,11]. ADR induces podocyte damage through multiple mechanisms, including oxidative stress, DNA damage, and mitochondrial dysfunction [12]. However, traditional approaches based on candidate gene studies remain limited by low throughput and an inability to systematically identify causal regulators of podocyte vulnerability.

  Genome-wide CRISPR screening provides an unbiased and high-throughput strategy to interrogate gene function at scale and establish causal relationships between genetic perturbations and cellular phenotypes [13]. Despite its transformative impact in other fields, CRISPR-based functional genomics has not yet been systematically applied to the study of podocyte biology.

  Here, we performed genome-wide CRISPR knockout screens in differentiated human podocytes exposed to ADR or H2O2. ADR was used as a complex podocyte injury model with oxidative and genotoxic components, whereas H2O2 was used as a direct oxidative stress model. By comparing these two screens, we aimed to distinguish shared regulators of podocyte resistance to oxidative injury from model-specific dependencies. Our results define a functional genomic landscape of podocyte stress vulnerability and identify candidate pathways for future mechanistic and therapeutic studies.


## Methods

### Cell culture
  Conditionally immortalized human podocytes (F-biology, FBCL2160) were maintained under permissive conditions at 33 °C in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin to sustain proliferative growth. To induce differentiation, cells were transferred to 37 °C and cultured under non-permissive conditions for 10 days, with medium replaced every 2–3 days. For genome-wide CRISPR screening, proliferating podocytes were transduced with the lentiCRISPR v2-based sgRNA library under permissive conditions. Because the lentiCRISPR v2 backbone enables simultaneous expression of Cas9 and sgRNA, no separate Cas9-expressing podocyte line was generated. Following lentiviral transduction and puromycin selection, library-positive cells were expanded to maintain adequate sgRNA representation and then shifted to 37 °C for 10 days to induce differentiation before injury selection.


### Genome-wide CRISPR library and lentiviral transduction
  A human genome-wide CRISPR knockout library (Ubigene, LIBR-H001A-P100) targeting 19,050 genes with 65,383 sgRNAs, together with 1,000 non-targeting control sgRNAs, was used for screening. The library was cloned into the lentiCRISPR v2 backbone, which contains both the Cas9 coding sequence and the sgRNA expression cassette, as well as a puromycin resistance cassette for antibiotic selection.

  Lentiviral particles were used to transduce proliferating podocytes at a low multiplicity of infection (MOI < 0.3), ensuring that most infected cells received a single sgRNA construct. After transduction, cells were selected with puromycin to enrich for successfully transduced cells. The selected cell population was subsequently expanded under permissive conditions while maintaining sufficient library coverage before transfer to non-permissive conditions for differentiation and stress screening.


### CRISPR screen design and sample collection
  After puromycin selection, library-transduced podocytes were expanded under permissive conditions to maintain adequate sgRNA representation. Cells were then shifted to 37 °C for 10 days to induce differentiation. Differentiated podocytes were treated with ADR (0.4 μM; MCE, HY-15142) or H2O2 (200 μM) for 48 h. These conditions were selected based on preliminary dose–response experiments to achieve approximately 50% cell viability, thereby imposing sufficient selective pressure while preserving library complexity. Untreated cells were included as controls. Each condition was performed in five independent biological replicates.

  Differentiated podocytes were treated with ADR (0.4 μM) or H2O2 (200 μM) for 48 h. These conditions were selected based on preliminary dose–response experiments to produce approximately 50% cell loss while maintaining sufficient selective pressure. Approximately 3 × 107 cells per biological replicate were used at the library transduction stage, corresponding to approximately 450 cells per sgRNA based on the total library complexity. To compensate for treatment-induced cell loss, additional culture dishes were used for the ADR- and H2O2-treated groups. Approximately 1 × 107 surviving cells were collected from each replicate at harvest, corresponding to approximately 150-fold cell-based sgRNA representation. Each condition was performed in five independent biological replicates. Untreated cells served as controls, and each condition was performed in five independent biological replicates.


### Genomic DNA extraction and sgRNA amplification
  Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from cell pellets using the commercial kit (Vazyme, DC112) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell numbers were adjusted to ensure sufficient representation of the sgRNA library in each sample. The integrated sgRNA sequences were amplified from gDNA by PCR using primers specific to the lentiCRISPR v2 backbone (forward primer: 5′-ATTTCTTGGGTAGTTTGCAGTTT-3′; reverse primer: 5′-GACTCGGTGCCACTTTTTCA-3′). PCR amplification generated a ∼213 bp fragment containing the sgRNA cassette.

  Each PCR reaction contained up to 10 μg of gDNA, and multiple parallel reactions were performed per sample to maintain library complexity and minimize amplification bias. PCR products were pooled and purified prior to next-generation sequencing.


### Next-generation sequencing and CRISPR screen analysis
  Purified PCR products were subjected to next-generation sequencing on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform, and paired-end sequencing was performed. Sequencing reads were processed and aligned to the reference sgRNA library using the MAGeCK (v0.5.9) count module to generate read counts for each sgRNA [14]. Normalization was performed based on non-targeting control sgRNAs.

  Differential enrichment analysis was conducted using the MAGeCK test module to compare ADR- or H2O2-treated podocytes with untreated controls. Candidate genes were defined using a nominal P value <0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (log2FC) ≥1. Genes with log2FC ≤ −1 were classified as depleted, whereas those with log2FC ≥1 were classified as enriched. No additional cutoff was applied to the RRA score, which was used to rank genes within the positive- and negative-selection analyses. After correction for multiple testing, no genes met an FDR threshold of <0.05 in either screen. Given the exploratory nature of this study and its aim of prioritizing candidates for subsequent validation, the nominal P-value threshold together with the effect-size cutoff was therefore used to define the primary candidate gene sets.


### Other bioinformatic analyses
  All downstream bioinformatic analyses were performed in R (v.4.3.0). Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using normalized sgRNA abundance profiles to assess sample clustering and reproducibility. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.10.1). Multiple-testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and terms with an adjusted P value <0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Enriched terms were ranked according to adjusted P values. The overlap between candidate gene sets from ADR- and H2O2-treated screens was visualized using the ggVennDiagram package (v1.5.4). Enrichment of the overlap between the two depleted gene sets was assessed using a one-sided hypergeometric test, with all 19,050 genes targeted by the CRISPR library used as the background gene set. Other visualizations, including PCA plots, enrichment plots, bar plots and scatter plots, were generated using ggplot2 (v3.5.2).


## Results

### A Dual-injury genome-wide CRISPR screening strategy in differentiated human podocytes
  To identify genes that regulate podocyte survival under oxidative injury, we established a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screening workflow in conditionally immortalized human podocytes. Cells were maintained under permissive conditions at 33 °C, transduced with a human genome-wide sgRNA library at low multiplicity of infection and selected to enrich for library-containing cells. After expansion to preserve library representation, cells were shifted to 37 °C for 10 days to induce podocyte differentiation before stress exposure.

  Two injury conditions were used. ADR was selected as a podocyte-relevant injury model that induces oxidative stress, DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction [10–12]. H2O2 was used as a direct reactive oxygen species challenge. Treatment conditions were optimized to achieve approximately 50% cell viability, thereby providing sufficient selective pressure while avoiding excessive loss of library complexity. Untreated cells served as controls. Each condition was performed in five independent replicates. Surviving cells were collected after treatment, and integrated sgRNA sequences were quantified by next-generation sequencing.

  This design allowed us to infer the contribution of individual gene knockouts to podocyte fitness under stress. Genes depleted from the surviving cell population were interpreted as candidate protective or fitness-supporting genes whose loss sensitized podocytes to injury. Conversely, genes enriched after treatment were interpreted as candidate sensitizing genes whose loss conferred a survival advantage (Figure 1).


### Genome-wide screens show high reproducibility and treatment-specific selection
  Sequencing quality metrics across all samples are summarized in Table 1. Across all experimental groups, sequencing data exhibited high quality, with GC content tightly distributed around ∼43% and Q30 scores consistently above 93% for both read 1 and read 2. No substantial differences in sequencing quality were observed among ADR-treated, H2O2-treated, and control samples, indicating comparable data quality across conditions. These results demonstrate that the sequencing output was of high fidelity and suitable for downstream quantitative analysis of sgRNA abundance.

  To assess the robustness and reproducibility of the CRISPR screening data, sgRNA read counts were quantified and normalized across all samples, including five independent replicates per condition.

  PCA revealed clear separation between ADR-treated, H2O2-treated, and control groups, with samples clustering tightly within each condition (Figure 2(A)). The first principal component (PC1), accounting for approximately 54.7% of the total variance, effectively distinguished the three experimental groups, indicating that treatment effects represent the major source of variation in the dataset. In addition, the distribution of normalized sgRNA counts was largely comparable across all samples (Figure 2(B)). Although a slight shift in peak density was observed in the ADR-treated group relative to control and H2O2-treated samples, the overall distribution patterns remained highly similar, suggesting minimal technical bias and effective preservation of library complexity during the screening process. To further evaluate library uniformity, we calculated the Gini index for each sample. Comparable Gini coefficients were observed across all replicates and conditions (Figure 2(C)), indicating an even distribution of sgRNA representation without significant skewing or loss of diversity. Collectively, these results demonstrate high reproducibility, consistent library representation, and reliable data quality, supporting the validity of downstream analyses.


### The ADR screen identifies genes required for podocyte survival after complex injury
  We first analyzed podocytes exposed to ADR. Compared with untreated controls, the ADR screen identified 456 significantly enriched genes and 819 significantly depleted genes (Figure 3(A) and Table S1). Because depletion from the surviving population indicates reduced fitness after gene knockout, we focused on the 819 depleted genes as candidate genes required for podocyte survival under ADR-induced injury.

  The top 10 most significantly depleted genes included CYP1A2, ABT1, LOC643669, SYTL3, hsa-mir-6817, BOC, SLC25A25, ABHD16B, DDX41, and CCDC8, each showing high concordance across multiple independent sgRNAs, underscoring the robustness of the screen (Figure 3(B)). Some of these genes are potentially relevant to podocyte function or renal biology. For example, CYP1A2 has been reported to modulate oxidative stress in renal cells [15], whereas SLC25A25 encodes a mitochondrial carrier that may influence mitochondrial calcium handling and metabolic adaptation [16]. DDX41 is involved in nucleic acid sensing and innate immune signaling [17], suggesting a possible connection between genotoxic stress and inflammatory responses in injured podocytes.

  Functional enrichment analysis highlighted pathways including acetylation-related processes and membrane-associated complexes. These pathways intersect with known regulators of podocyte cytoskeletal dynamics and signal transduction, which are critical for structural integrity and responses to stress in podocytes [18,19] (Figure 3(C)). These findings suggest that ADR-induced podocyte injury depends on a broad network of genes involved in stress adaptation, membrane organization and cellular homeostasis.


### The H2O2 screen defines genetic dependencies of acute oxidative stress resistance
  We next analyzed podocytes exposed to H2O2 to identify genes required for survival under direct oxidative stress. Compared with untreated controls, the H2O2 screen identified 215 significantly enriched genes and 277 significantly depleted genes (Figure 4(A) and Table S2). We focused on the 277 depleted genes as candidate protective genes whose loss impaired podocyte survival after oxidative challenge.

  The top 10 hits were H2AFB3, LOC643669, hsa-mir-150, SHD, GABRQ, PFKFB2, PCNXL3, CHRAC1, ZNF639, and AKNAD1, each showing high concordance across multiple independent sgRNAs, confirming the robustness of the screen (Figure 4(B)). Although none of these top 10 candidate genes have been directly implicated in podocyte biology, several may plausibly contribute to protection against oxidative stress. For instance, PFKFB2 regulates fructose‑2,6‑bisphosphate levels and glycolytic flux [20], whereas CHRAC1 is involved in chromatin structure and regulation [21], suggesting potential roles in modulating podocyte responses to acute oxidative insult.

  Functional enrichment analysis revealed that the 280 candidate protective genes are closely associated with cellular responses to heat, consistent with the direct oxidative challenge imposed by H2O2, including potential effects on membrane integrity. These genes are also closely associated with carbohydrate derivative catabolic processes and chromatin-related functions, which may represent key mechanisms by which podocytes respond to and withstand oxidative stress (Figure 4(C)). Together, the H2O2 screen nominates metabolic and chromatin-associated pathways as candidate regulators of podocyte oxidative stress tolerance.


### Integrated analysis reveals shared regulators of podocyte resistance to oxidative injury
  To identify shared genetic dependencies under oxidative injury, we intersected the genes depleted in the ADR and H2O2 screens. Among the 819 ADR-depleted and 277 H2O2-depleted genes, 27 genes (LOC643669, hsa‑mir‑150, GABRQ, CHRAC1, ZNF639, MARK2, hsa‑mir‑6817, GTF3C5, LCE3E, PLEKHG4, CYP3A7-CYP3AP1, ZFYVE1, FRMD7, ZNF653, NDUFB11, HIBCH, CDX4, TIGD4, NUPR1, ROS1, BCL3, RNASEH2B, SPN, C22orf39, MASTL, ZNF765, NAA50) were shared between the two conditions (Figure 5(A,B)). This overlap was significantly greater than expected by chance (27 observed versus 11.9 expected; 2.27-fold enrichment; one-sided hypergeometric test, p = 6.68 × 10−5).

  These 27 candidate genes may define a protective transcriptional program that enhances podocyte resilience to oxidative injury. Functionally, they converge on several processes that are central to podocyte survival, including stress adaptation, inflammatory control, cytoskeletal maintenance, mitochondrial metabolism, and genome stability. For example, hsa-miR-150 has been implicated in inflammatory, immune, and profibrotic signaling, including the TGF-β pathway [22]; NUPR1 is a stress-inducible regulator involved in oxidative stress responses, ER stress, autophagy, and cell survival [23]; BCL3 modulates NF-κB-dependent transcription and may restrain injurious inflammatory signaling [24]; MARK2 contributes to microtubule organization and cell polarity [25], both of which are essential for maintaining podocyte architecture; NDUFB11 supports mitochondrial respiration and redox balance [26]. RNASEH2B and MASTL may reflect requirements for genome stability and cell-cycle-related stress control, although their specific functions in differentiated podocytes require further validation [27,28].

  Functional enrichment analysis also revealed that these 27 genes are overrepresented in ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling, DNA replication, branched-chain amino acid degradation (valine, leucine, isoleucine), propanoate metabolism, and beta-alanine metabolism, as well as biological processes including oxidative phosphorylation, ribose phosphate and ribonucleotide metabolic processes, acute inflammatory response, and intrinsic apoptotic signaling in response to DNA damage. Cellular component and molecular function terms were enriched for DNA transcription machinery and membrane structures, including extrinsic components of the membrane, highlighting potential roles in stress sensing, signaling, and structural integrity of podocytes [29] (Figure 5(C)).

  Taken together, the dual-screen strategy distinguishes injury model-specific candidates from a core set of genes whose loss sensitizes podocytes to both ADR and H2O2. These shared genes represent prioritized candidates for mechanistic validation in podocyte injury and glomerular disease models.


## Discussion
  In this study, we performed genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screens in differentiated human podocytes exposed to ADR or H2O2 and identified genetic determinants of podocyte survival under oxidative injury. By using two complementary stress models, we resolved both context-specific and shared genetic dependencies. The resulting candidate gene sets provide a functional genomic framework for studying podocyte vulnerability, oxidative stress responses and glomerular disease progression.

  A major feature of this study is the use of differentiated human podocytes rather than proliferating cells at the time of injury selection. Mature podocytes are highly specialized and have limited regenerative capacity in vivo. Differentiation before stress exposure therefore improves the biological relevance of the screening system. In addition, the use of five independent replicates per condition, together with preserved sgRNA distribution and low Gini index variation, supports the reproducibility of the screen and reduces the likelihood that the identified candidates reflect stochastic library bottlenecking.

  The ADR and H2O2 screens produced distinct but overlapping genetic landscapes. ADR induced broader selective pressure, identifying a larger number of depleted genes than H2O2. This is consistent with the complex effects of ADR, which include oxidative stress, DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction [12]. In contrast, H2O2 represents a more direct oxidative insult and yielded a more restricted candidate set. The divergence between the two screens suggests that podocyte responses to injury are not uniform but depend on the nature and complexity of the stressor. This distinction is important because podocyte injury in vivo is multifactorial and may involve overlapping oxidative, metabolic, inflammatory and mechanical components.

  The 27 shared depleted genes are of particular interest because they may represent common requirements for podocyte survival under oxidative injury. Several candidates point to biologically plausible mechanisms. NUPR1 and BCL3 suggest roles for stress-responsive transcription and inflammatory regulation. MARK2 implicates microtubule polarity and cytoskeletal organization, both of which are central to podocyte structure and filtration barrier function. NDUFB11 highlights mitochondrial respiration and redox control. CHRAC1, ZNF639 and other nuclear regulators suggest that chromatin state and transcriptional adaptation may be important determinants of podocyte stress tolerance. Although many of these candidates have not been directly studied in podocytes, their convergence on stress, metabolism, inflammation and structural homeostasis provides a rational basis for future mechanistic studies.

  This work also highlights the value of genome-wide perturbation screening in kidney disease research. Previous studies of podocyte injury have largely relied on candidate-gene approaches, transcriptomics or proteomics. These approaches are informative but do not directly establish whether a gene is functionally required for survival under stress. In contrast, CRISPR screening provides causal perturbation at genome scale and can identify genes that may not be strongly differentially expressed but are nevertheless essential for stress resistance. Integrating this dataset with human glomerular transcriptomic, proteomic and genetic association datasets may further prioritize candidates with disease relevance [30,31].

  Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the screens were performed in an immortalized human podocyte cell line, which may not fully recapitulate the molecular state of primary podocytes in vivo. Second, CRISPR knockout screens identify genetic dependencies but do not by themselves define the downstream mechanisms by which each gene regulates survival. Third, some candidates include non-coding loci, predicted genes or poorly annotated transcripts, which require careful validation. Fourth, the present study focused on cell survival as the selection phenotype; additional podocyte-relevant phenotypes, such as cytoskeletal integrity, slit diaphragm protein expression, mitochondrial function, albumin permeability and inflammatory signaling, were not directly screened. Finally, individual validation of top candidates using independent sgRNAs, rescue experiments or orthogonal knockdown approaches will be required before specific genes can be considered therapeutic targets.

  In summary, we establish a dual-model genome-wide CRISPR screening platform in human podocytes and identify shared and context-specific regulators of resistance to ADR- and H2O2-induced injury. These data provide a prioritized set of candidate genes and pathways for mechanistic studies of podocyte stress biology and may support the development of new strategies to protect podocytes in chronic kidney disease.

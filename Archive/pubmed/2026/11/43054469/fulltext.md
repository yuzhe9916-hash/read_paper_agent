# SLC38A2-Driven Glutamine Metabolic Reprogramming Promotes Hypoxic Survival in Glioblastoma


## Abstract
  Background: Glioblastoma (GBM) is highly refractory to standard therapies, including anti-angiogenic agents such as bevacizumab. While bevacizumab prunes tumor vasculature, it exacerbates intratumoral hypoxia, which drives a metabolically resilient phenotype that limits survival benefits. This study aimed to identify the metabolic dependencies underlying GBM adaptation to hypoxia and to evaluate whether targeting these dependencies could enhance the antitumor efficacy of bevacizumab. Methods: We analyzed the spatial distribution of key metabolites in GBM patients and orthotopic xenograft mouse models using magnetic resonance spectroscopy and chemical shift imaging. A dual-vector hypoxia fate-mapping system paired with an in vivo genome-wide clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)–CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) knockout screen was employed to identify metabolic vulnerabilities within the hypoxic niche. Metabolic flux was analyzed utilizing 13C5-labeled glutamine tracers. The molecular mechanisms governing solute carrier family 38 member 2 (SLC38A2) regulation were investigated using chromatin immunoprecipitation, cycloheximide chase assays, liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry, and co-immunoprecipitation. The efficacy of targeting SLC38A2 was evaluated through structure-based virtual screening and in vivo orthotopic xenograft models treated concurrently with zonisamide and bevacizumab. Results: Imaging and tissue metabolite analyses revealed a spatial metabolic mismatch in the hypoxic tumor core, characterized by lactate accumulation alongside glutamine depletion. In vivo CRISPR–Cas9 screening identified SLC38A2 as an essential metabolic dependency for GBM cell survival under hypoxic stress. Under hypoxic conditions, hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF-1α) promoted SLC38A2 transcription, while elevated lactate stabilized the SLC38A2 protein through alanyl-tRNA synthetase 1 (AARS1)-mediated lactylation at Lys472. These changes were accompanied by increased glutamine uptake and glutathione biosynthesis, reduced reactive oxygen species levels, and decreased mitochondrial-fission-mediated apoptosis. Pharmacological screening identified the US Food and Drug Administration-approved, brain-penetrant anticonvulsant zonisamide as an inhibitor of SLC38A2. In orthotopic GBM xenograft models, combined treatment with zonisamide and bevacizumab markedly suppressed tumor growth compared with bevacizumab alone. Conclusions: This study defines a spatially organized lactate–glutamine metabolic axis essential for hypoxic adaptation in GBM. The dual transcriptional and posttranslational up-regulation of SLC38A2 represents a critical survival mechanism. This axis can be pharmacologically dismantled by zonisamide, offering a clinically actionable combinational strategy to overcome anti-angiogenic therapy resistance.


## Background
  Glioblastoma (GBM) is highly refractory to current therapeutic interventions. Characterized by aggressive infiltration and a high recurrence rate, GBM largely remains fatal despite maximal surgical resection and chemoradiotherapy [1–3]. Therapy resistance is largely driven by the tumor’s metabolic plasticity, which enables malignant cells to survive within a severely hypoxic microenvironment [4–6]. Instead of undergoing hypoxia-induced cell death, GBM cells rewire their metabolic networks to maintain proliferation [7–9]. Although the link between the hypoxic niche and metabolic reprogramming is a recognized driver of tumor aggressiveness, the specific molecular mechanisms that allow GBM to tolerate such metabolic stress remain elusive.

  This metabolic adaptation not only fuels tumor progression but also underlies the failure of anti-angiogenic treatments, such as bevacizumab [10–13]. By pruning the tumor vasculature, bevacizumab may exacerbate intratumoral hypoxia without ultimately inhibiting tumor growth [14–16]. Instead of succumbing to this microenvironmental stress, GBM cells exploit their metabolic plasticity to adapt to the therapy-induced hypoxia, contributing to therapeutic resistance [15,17,18]. Therefore, uncovering the precise metabolic dependencies that enable GBM cells to survive within these extreme hypoxic niches is critical for overcoming this therapy resistance.

  Understanding the spatial heterogeneity of tumor metabolism is essential for identifying new therapeutic vulnerabilities [19–21]. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) provides a noninvasive window into GBM physiology, although clinical applications have traditionally focused on N-acetylaspartate depletion [22–24]. However, the spatial dynamics and functional interplay of other key metabolites—specifically lactate and glutamine—remain less well characterized. Lactate functions not only as a byproduct of hypoxic glycolysis but also as a signaling molecule that drives tumor progression, partly through protein lactylation [25–27]. Simultaneously, glutamine provides a crucial carbon and nitrogen source [28–30]. Although both metabolites independently contribute to GBM metabolic reprogramming, it is unknown whether they functionally coordinate to support hypoxia tolerance.

  Here, we investigated whether lactate and glutamine metabolism are spatially coordinated within hypoxic GBM niches, identified the molecular mechanisms supporting this metabolic adaptation, and evaluated whether the resulting vulnerability could be therapeutically targeted to overcome resistance to anti-angiogenic therapy.


## Materials and Methods

### Human and animal ethics declarations
  The human primary GBM samples utilized in this study were procured from Qilu Hospital of Shandong University, following a protocol approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was granted ethical approval by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Qilu Hospital (Approval No.: KYLL-2023(ZM)−657).

  All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Laboratory Animal Management and Use Committee of Qilu Hospital, Shandong University (Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee [IACUC]; Protocol No.: DWLL-2024-109). In compliance with ethical guidelines, the maximum permitted volume for orthotopic brain tumors was set at 200 mm3—a threshold determined by intracranial space limitations and signs of neurological impairment. Throughout the study, no tumor exceeded 150 mm3, and no animal exhibited weight loss greater than 20% of its initial body weight. Sex was not considered a variable in the study design. Mice were housed under barrier conditions with a controlled temperature of 24 °C and 50% relative humidity, on a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle.


### Cell culture and reagents
  Human GBM cell line LN229 and murine GBM cell line GL261 were procured from the American Type Culture Collection. LN229 and GL261 cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and maintained under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2, in a humidified atmosphere). Patient-derived glioma stem cell (GSC) lines, GSC-267 and GSC-20, were kindly provided by Dr Krishna P.L. Bhat (The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center, Houston, TX, USA). These lines have been previously established and widely used in prior studies [31,32]. GSCs were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Cat. No. 10565018, Gibco) supplemented with 2% B-27 serum-free supplement (Cat. No. 17504044, Gibco), 20 ng/mL human recombinant epidermal growth factor (Cat. No. 236-EG, R&D Systems), and 20 ng/mL human recombinant basic fibroblast growth factor (Cat. No. 3718-FB, R&D Systems). Tumor spheroids were dissociated using Accutase solution (Cat. No. A6964, Sigma-Aldrich). All cells were maintained in an incubator (Cat. No. 51032719, Thermo Fisher Scientific) set at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 20% O2. Prior to the study, all cell lines were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. The reagents or resources used in this study are shown in Table S1.


### RNA extraction and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction
  Total RNA was isolated from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Cat. No. 15596026, Life Technologies). Subsequently, 1 μg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA with a ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit (Cat. No. FSQ-101, Toyobo) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using the Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Cat. No. 11201ES50, Yeasen) on a Roche LightCycler 480II Real-Time PCR System (Cat. No. 05015243001, Roche). Primer sequences are listed in Table S2. Relative messenger RNA (mRNA) expression levels were normalized to β-actin and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.


### Knockdown and overexpression
  All small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), overexpression plasmids, and lentiviruses were commercially obtained from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). For transient knockdown or overexpression in GBM cells, transfection was performed using siRNAs or plasmids with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Cat. No. L3000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For stable genetic manipulation, lentiviral transduction was employed. The sequences of all siRNAs used in this study are provided in Table S2.


### Xenograft models
  The GSC-267 cell line was first expanded in vivo by intracranial injection to enhance its tumorigenic potential. Tumors exhibiting robust growth were then harvested, dissociated, and recultured for use in subsequent formal tumorigenesis studies. To establish xenograft models, luciferase-expressing human GSC-267 cells (2 × 105 cells in 10 μL of phosphate-buffered saline [PBS]) were stereotactically injected into the frontal lobes of 4-week-old BALB/c nude mice. Tumor progression was monitored on days 7 and 14 using bioluminescence imaging (IVIS Spectrum In Vivo Imaging System, PerkinElmer). For drug combination studies in immunodeficient mice, luciferase-expressing GSC-267 cells (2 × 105 cells in 10 μL of PBS) were similarly implanted into the frontal lobes of BALB/c nude mice. In parallel, for studies in immunocompetent mice, luciferase-expressing GL261 cells (2 × 105 cells in 10 μL of PBS) were injected into the frontal lobes of 4-week-old C57BL/6J mice using the same stereotactic procedure.


### Immunohistochemistry analysis
  Serial 4-μm-thick sections of mouse GBM and adjacent normal tissues were processed for immunohistochemistry as previously described [33]. Briefly, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Subsequently, they were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, sections were treated with an appropriate secondary antibody and developed with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (Cat. No. G1211, Servicebio) for visualization. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-Ki67 (Cat. No. GB111499-100, Servicebio) and anti-cleaved caspase-3 (Cat. No. GB11532-100, Servicebio).


### Immunofluorescence assay
  For immunofluorescence (IF) staining, cells or tissue sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Cat. No. P0099, Beyotime) for 10 to 15 min, followed by PBS washes. Permeabilization was performed using 0.1% Triton X-100 (Cat. No. ST795, Beyotime) in PBS for 5 min. Nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (Cat. No. ST023, Beyotime) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. After washing with PBS, samples were incubated with appropriate fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, samples were mounted with a medium containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; C1002, Beyotime) and imaged using a fluorescence microscope. The primary antibodies used were anti-solute carrier family 38 member 2 (anti-SLC38A2; Cat. No. sc-166366, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and anti-carbonic anhydrase 9 (anti-CA9; Cat. No. 11071-1-AP, Proteintech).


### Dual-luciferase reporter assay
  SLC38A2 promoter wild-type (WT) and mutant (Mut) luciferase reporter plasmids were constructed in the pGL3 vector (GenePharma). After transfection into 293T cells, promoter activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Cat. No. 11402ES60, Yeasen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.


### Co-immunoprecipitation and western blotting
  For endogenous co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), lysates prepared from LN229 and GSC-267 cells were incubated with an anti-SLC38A2 antibody (Cat. No. sc-166366, Santa Cruz) or control immunoglobulin G (IgG; Cat. No. 68860L, Cell Signaling Technology). An aliquot of each lysate was retained as the input control. Immunoprecipitates and input lysates were analyzed by western blotting using antibodies against alanyl-tRNA synthetase 1 (AARS1) and SLC38A2. For immunoprecipitation of Flag-tagged SLC38A2, lysates prepared from LN229, GSC-267, and 293T cells transfected with the indicated constructs were incubated with an anti-Flag antibody (Cat. No. 66008-4-Ig, Proteintech). Both endogenous and Flag-based immunoprecipitation assays were performed using a Protein A/G Magnetic IP/Co-IP Kit (Cat. No. PB201-01/02, Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The captured protein complexes were eluted and subjected to western blot analysis. Details of the antibodies used are provided in Table S3.


### Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry
  Immunoprecipitated protein complexes were first reduced with 10 mmol/L dithiothreitol at 56 °C for 1 h, followed by alkylation in the dark using 55 mmol/L iodoacetamide for 45 min. Trypsin digestion was then performed overnight at 37 °C at an enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:50 (w/w). The digestion was quenched by adding 1% formic acid, and the resulting peptides were desalted using C18 StageTips prior to liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis. For LC–MS/MS, peptide separation was carried out on an EASY-nLC 1200 system (Cat. No. LC140, Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled online to a Q Exactive mass spectrometer. Peptides were loaded onto a C18 reversed-phase column and eluted with a linear gradient from 5% to 100% buffer B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) at a constant flow rate of 300 nl/min. MS data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode. Full MS scans (350 to 1,800 m/z) were acquired at a resolution of 70,000, after which the most abundant precursor ions were selected for higher-energy collision dissociation fragmentation with a normalized collision energy of 27%. Fragment spectra were recorded at a resolution of 17,500, and a dynamic exclusion time of 30 s was applied to prevent repeated sequencing of the same ions.


### Western blot analysis
  Proteins were extracted from cells after the indicated treatments (e.g., hypoxia, zonisamide) using radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (Cat. No. P0013, Beyotime) supplemented with a phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride protease inhibitor (Cat. No. ST506, Beyotime). Protein concentration was determined by a bicinchoninic acid assay (Cat. No. 23225, Thermo Fisher). The samples were then mixed with loading buffer, denatured by boiling, and stored at −20 °C. For immunoblotting, equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Cat. No. 03010040001, Merck Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h at room temperature and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Following washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence substrate (Cat. No. 1705060, Bio-Rad) and imaged according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The antibodies used are shown in Table S3.


### MitoSOX staining imaging
  Tumor-bearing mouse brains were embedded in low-melting-point agarose and sectioned into 300-μm-thick slices using a vibratome (Leica VT1200). The tissue slices were incubated with MitoSOX (Cat. No. HY-D1055, MCE) for detecting mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS). Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope (SP8, Leica Microsystems).


### Hypoxia fate-mapping construct design
  The loxp-DsRed-loxp-eGFP cassette was PCR-amplified from the plasmid pMSCV-loxp-DsRed-loxp-eGFP-Puro-WPRE (Cat. No. 32702, Addgene) using primers incorporating SalI and NotI restriction sites. The resulting PCR product was digested with SalI and NotI, treated with calf intestinal phosphatase (Cat. No. M0290, New England Biolabs), and purified from an agarose gel. This fragment was then ligated into a SalI/NotI-digested pENTR1A vector. Finally, the Gateway system (Cat. No. 11791020, Invitrogen) was used to recombine the entry clone with the destination vector pLenti CMV/TO Zeo DEST (Cat. No. 17294, Addgene), yielding the final lentiviral construct (lentiviral vector 1). Tandem repeats of the hypoxia-response element (HRE; core sequence: GTGTACGTG), spaced by 5-bp random nucleotides and ranging from 1 to 9 copies, were synthesized as gBlocks (Integrated DNA Technologies [IDT]). Additionally, the TK minimal promoter followed by a 770-bp β-globin intron, as well as coding sequences for CRE and Cre recombinase–oxygen-dependent degradation domain (CRE-ODD), were separately synthesized as gBlocks (IDT). The vector backbone pRRLSIN.cPPT.PGK-GFP.WPRE (Cat. No. 12252, Addgene) was linearized by EcoRV and BamHI digestion to remove the PGK promoter and EGFP cassette. The synthesized HRE array, β-globin intron block, and either the CRE or CRE-ODD block were assembled into the linearized backbone using In-Fusion HD cloning (Cat. No. 639648, Clontech).


### Lentiviral transduction and selection
  Lentiviral vector 1 (encoding CMV-loxp-DsRed-loxp-eGFP) or lentiviral vector 2 (encoding 4xHRE-MinTK-CRE-ODD) was co-transfected with the packaging plasmids psPAX2 (Cat. No. 12260, Addgene) and pMD2.G (Cat. No. 12259, Addgene) into 293T cells using PolyJet transfection reagent (Cat. No. SL10088, SignaGen). For stable cell line generation, the filtered viral supernatant produced from vector 1 was harvested 48 h posttransfection and used to transduce LN229 and GSC-267 cell lines in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene (Cat. No. H9268, Sigma-Aldrich) overnight. After 24 h in fresh medium, zeocin (Cat. No. R25001, Invitrogen) was added at a concentration of 100 to 250 μg/mL for selection. Following selection, cells were subsequently transduced with lentivirus encoding the 4xHRE-MinTK-CRE-ODD construct. To establish hypoxia-responsive reporter clones, the transduced cells were exposed to 5% O2 and subjected to fluorescence-activated cell sorting to remove green fluorescent protein (GFP)-positive cells. The sorted population was then single-cell cloned. Clones were screened via image analysis and flow cytometry to identify those that exhibited a switch from Discosoma red fluorescent protein (DsRed) to GFP expression under 0.5% O2, but not under 3% O2 conditions.


### Electron microscopy
  Following the indicated treatments, cells were dissociated, pelleted by centrifugation, and fixed with 4% glutaraldehyde (Cat. No. P1127, Solarbio) for 2 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, the samples were postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for an additional hour at 4 °C. After a series of dehydration steps, the samples were embedded in epoxy resin, cured, and sectioned into 70-nm-thick slices. These ultrathin sections were then post-stained with 5% uranyl acetate and subsequently examined by transmission electron microscopy. Images were acquired randomly, and the mitochondrial area was quantified using the ImageJ software.


### CCK-8 assay and drug treatment
  Cell viability was assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Cat. No. C0038, Beyotime). Cells were seeded into precoated 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well in 100 μL of complete culture medium. After incubation at 37 °C for 12 h, cells were exposed to zonisamide at the indicated concentrations. Following 48 h of treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for an additional 1 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.


### GBM organoid construction
  Glioblastoma organoids (GBOs) were generated with modifications to a previously described method [34]. Briefly, freshly resected GBM specimens were mechanically minced and transferred to 6-well plates containing 2 mL of GBO medium per well. The GBO medium comprised a 1:1 mixture of DMEM/F12 (Cat. No. A4192001, Gibco) and Neurobasal medium (Cat. No. 21103049, Gibco), supplemented with 1% GlutaMAX (Cat. No. 35050061, Gibco), 1% nonessential amino acids, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, 1% N2 supplement (Cat. No. A1370701, Gibco), and 1× B27 supplement (Cat. No. 17504044, Gibco). Cultures were maintained on an orbital shaker (140 rpm) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Within 2 to 3 weeks, tissue fragments typically formed spherical organoid structures suitable for downstream analyses.


### Cellular thermal shift assay
  Cellular thermal shift assays (CETSAs) were performed with minor modifications to a previously reported protocol [35]. Briefly, cells were washed and resuspended in PBS. Aliquots (50 μL; approximately 1 × 106 cells) were heated for 3 min at temperatures from 40 to 67 °C in 3 °C increments using a thermocycler. Following heating, cells were lysed by 3 freeze–thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen and then incubated on ice for 20 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting soluble fractions were mixed with 5 × SDS loading buffer for subsequent immunoblotting.


### Protein purification and surface plasmon resonance analysis
  A pet28a plasmid encoding human SLC38A2 (Homo-SLC38A2) was purchased from GenePharma and transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 cells (Cat. No. CD901-02, TransGen Biotech). Transformed cells were cultured in Luria–Bertani medium supplemented with kanamycin (0.1 mg/mL) at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. Protein expression was induced with 0.5 mmol/L isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG; Cat. No. I8070, Solarbio), and SLC38A2 was purified using a commercial protein purification kit (Cat. No. CW0894S, CoWin Bio). Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) measurements were performed on a SensiQ Pioneer system (ForteBio, Fremont, CA). PCH biosensor chips (Cat. No. 19-0129, ForteBio) were activated with 1 mmol/L NiCl2, and SLC38A2 (50 μg/mL) was subsequently immobilized. Zonisamide binding was then evaluated, and response units (RU) were recorded as an index of binding. Data were normalized to the corresponding control signals and analyzed using the Qdat software (version 2.6.3.0, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-40573-y). Sensorgrams were used to generate binding curves.


### Micro-positron emission tomography/computed tomography imaging of mice
  The indicated LN229 cells (2 × 105 cells in 10 μL) were intracranially injected into BALB/c nude mice (4 to 5 weeks old). Micro-positron emission tomography (micro-PET) imaging was performed 28 d after tumor cell inoculation. Prior to micro-PET/computed tomography (CT) imaging, tumor-bearing mice were deprived of food and water for 8 to 10 h. Mice were then administered approximately 300 μCi of (2S,4S)-4-[18F]FEBGln via tail vein injection, followed by dynamic PET acquisition in 5-min frames using a PET/CT/fluorescence molecular tomography system (Cat. No. PSA094, MadicLab). During imaging, animals were anesthetized with isoflurane (2% to 3% in 1 L/min oxygen) and placed on a heating pad to maintain body temperature. Respiratory rate and signs of distress were continuously monitored. Data acquisition was initiated immediately after intravenous tracer injection. Micro-PET/CT images were analyzed using PMOD (version 4.0, https://www.pmod.com/). Each PET image was manually co-registered to the Mirrione mouse brain template using rigid-body transformation, and the resulting parameters were applied to the corresponding PET datasets. Quantification of the percentage of injected dose per milliliter and standardized uptake values (SUVs) was performed using the Inveon Research Workplace software (version 4.2, https://www.siemens-healthineers.com/) SUVmax values were plotted and analyzed.


### Generation of SLC38A2 knockout in GBM cells
  Two independent single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting human SLC38A2 were used in LN229, GSC-267, and GSC-20 cells, whereas 2 sgRNAs targeting murine Slc38a2 were used in GL261 cells. An sgRNA targeting GFP served as the negative control. All sgRNAs were individually cloned into the lentiCRISPR v2 vector (Cat. No. 52961, Addgene) using standard molecular cloning procedures. Lentiviral particles were produced by transfecting 293T cells with polyethylenimine (1 mg/mL; Cat. No. C0537, Beyotime). Viral supernatants were harvested at 48 and 72 h posttransfection and filtered through 0.45-μm membranes. LN229, GSC-267, GSC-20, and GL261 cells were seeded at 40% to 50% confluency and transduced with the corresponding lentiviral supernatants in the presence of polybrene (Cat. No. H9268, Sigma-Aldrich) at 4 μg/mL. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete culture medium. Puromycin (Cat. No. ST551, Beyotime) selection at 1 μg/mL was initiated 48 h after transduction and maintained for 3 d until all nontransduced control cells were eliminated. Puromycin-resistant cells were plated into 96-well plates by limiting dilution to obtain single-cell clones. Individual clones were expanded and validated by immunoblotting to confirm loss of SLC38A2 protein. The sequences of all human SLC38A2- and murine Slc38a2-targeting sgRNAs are provided in Table S2.


### CRISPR–Cas9 genetic screen
  The all-in-one Human CRISPR Knockout Pooled Library (Cat. No. 73179, Addgene) was used to perform a genome-wide loss-of-function screen in hypoxia fate-mapping-labeled GSC-267 cells. Guide representation in the amplified plasmid library was evaluated by next-generation sequencing (NGS), and libraries with a Gini index <0.1 were used for screening. Lentiviral particles were generated in 293T cells. Briefly, 1 × 108 293T cells were transfected with 64 μg of pooled library plasmid together with psPAX2 (Cat. No. 12260, Addgene) and pMD2.G (Cat. No. 12259, Addgene) using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The molar ratio of pooled library plasmid:psPAX2:pMD2.G was 2.5:2:1. The culture medium was replaced 24 h after transfection, and virus-containing supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h, pooled, and filtered through 0.45-μm membranes. Viral transduction efficiency was determined by infecting 1 × 106 GSC-267 cells with serial dilutions of viral supernatant in complete medium supplemented with polybrene (4 μg/mL; Cat. No. H9268, Sigma-Aldrich), followed by spin infection (800 × g, 25 °C, 2 h). Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h and then selected with puromycin (1 μg/mL). After 4 d of selection, transduction efficiency was calculated as the ratio of viable cells in puromycin-treated wells to those in matched no-puromycin control wells. For the full-scale screen, 180 million cells were infected with 108 mL of pooled viral supernatant using the plate-based spin infection method and selected with puromycin (1 μg/mL) for 5 d, yielding a final infection rate of 23%. Cells were subsequently expanded in complete medium until 4 × 108 cells were obtained and then stereotactically implanted into the frontal lobes of 4-week-old BALB/c nude mice. Twenty-one days after implantation, cells were recovered and genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAGEN Blood & Cell Culture DNA Maxi Kit (Cat. No. 13362, QIAGEN). The sgRNA cassettes were amplified using Illumina-compatible primer sets and Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Cat. No. M0491L, NEB). Amplicons were sequenced by Novogene with an average depth of approximately 500 reads per sgRNA. NGS reads were analyzed using Model-based Analysis of Genome-wide CRISPR–Cas9 Knockout (MAGeCK), and candidate genes were prioritized based on effect size and statistical significance.


### Public data collection
  Publicly available spatial transcriptomic and single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets of glioma were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession numbers GSE286413 and GSE131928, respectively. Transcriptomic data and corresponding clinical information for The Cancer Genome Atlas glioma cohort were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons Data Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). RNA-seq data and clinical annotations for Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) samples were downloaded from the CGGA database (https://www.cgga.org.cn/index.jsp). Transcriptomic profiles of the Rembrandt (GSE108476) GBM cohorts were retrieved from the GEO database. Anatomically resolved transcriptomic data from the Ivy Glioblastoma Atlas Project were additionally analyzed to compare SLC38A2 expression across histologically defined GBM regions, including the leading edge, infiltrating tumor, cellular tumor, microvascular proliferation, and pseudopalisading cells around necrosis (PAN).


### Spatial transcriptomic data processing and analysis
  Spatial transcriptomic data matrices and spatial coordinates were processed using the Seurat package in RStudio. Low-quality spots were excluded according to standard quality-control criteria, and the remaining spots were normalized and scaled for downstream analysis. Principal component analysis was performed for dimensionality reduction, followed by unsupervised clustering and spatial visualization. A total of 10 spatial clusters were identified across the glioma tissue section. Based on their spatial distribution and transcriptional characteristics, cluster 4 was annotated as the tumor margin, whereas cluster 7 was defined as the hypoxic core region. To characterize the spatial heterogeneity of glioma, SpatialFeaturePlot was used to visualize the spatial expression pattern of SLC38A2 as well as the enrichment scores of the hypoxia and glutamine metabolism pathways. The pathway activity for each spot was quantified using module scoring based on predefined gene signatures. In addition, correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the association between SLC38A2 expression and the hypoxia score across all spatial spots.


### Single-cell RNA-seq data processing and clustering
  Single-cell RNA-seq data were analyzed using the Seurat (v4.1.0) package in RStudio. Low-quality cells were filtered out based on standard criteria, followed by data normalization, identification of highly variable genes, principal component analysis, unsupervised clustering, and visualization using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). Glioma cells were classified into 4 major GBM cell subtypes, namely, astrocyte-like, mesenchymal-like, neural progenitor cell-like, and oligodendrocyte progenitor cell-like according to previous research [6]. The R package irGSEA was used to calculate and visualize enrichment scores for the gene signature using the “UCell” method. The relative expression of SLC38A2 and the glutamate transport enrichment score were evaluated across these cell subtypes. UMAP feature plotting was used to display the distribution of SLC38A2 expression among glioma cells.


### Seahorse XF metabolic flux assays
  Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress and Glycolysis Stress assays were performed using the Agilent Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Cat. No. 103015-100, Agilent Technologies) and Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test Kit (Cat. No. 103020-100, Agilent Technologies), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. One day before each assay, the Seahorse XFe96 analyzer (Cat. No. S7800B, Agilent Technologies) was powered on for equilibration, and the sensor cartridge was hydrated overnight in Seahorse XF Calibrant at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator. XF96 cell culture microplates were coated overnight at 4 °C with poly-l-ornithine (Cat. No. P4957, Sigma-Aldrich; 1:10 dilution) and fibronectin (Cat. No. F1141, Sigma-Aldrich; 1:40 dilution). LN229 and GSC-267 cells expressing sgCtrl or either of 2 independent sgRNAs targeting SLC38A2 were cultured under normoxic (20% O2) or hypoxic (1% O2) conditions and treated with PBS or glutathione monoethyl ester (GSH-MEE; Cat. No. HY-W392413, MedChemExpress) as indicated before the assays. On the day of the experiment, cells were collected and resuspended in the corresponding prewarmed assay medium. Cells were seeded into coated XF96 plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in 50 μL of assay medium and incubated at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator for 25 to 30 min to allow attachment. Subsequently, 130 μL of prewarmed assay medium was gently added to each well, followed by an additional incubation for 15 to 25 min before measurement. For the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test, Seahorse XF DMEM assay medium was supplemented with 10 mmol/L glucose, 2 mmol/L l-glutamine, and 1 mmol/L sodium pyruvate, and the pH was adjusted to 7.4. Mitochondrial stress-test compounds were prepared in the assay medium and sequentially loaded into the injection ports of the hydrated sensor cartridge as follows: port A, 20 μL of oligomycin solution to achieve a final concentration of 1 μmol/L; port B, 22 μL of carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP) solution to achieve a final concentration of approximately 1 μmol/L; and port C, 25 μL of rotenone/antimycin A solution to achieve final concentrations of approximately 0.5 μmol/L for each compound. After sensor cartridge calibration, oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured before and after the sequential injection of oligomycin, FCCP, and rotenone/antimycin A. Adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-linked respiration was calculated as the difference between basal OCR and OCR after oligomycin injection. Spare respiratory capacity was calculated as the difference between maximal respiration and basal respiration. Basal respiration, maximal respiration, and nonmitochondrial oxygen consumption were also calculated from the corresponding OCR profiles. For the Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test, Seahorse XF Base Medium was supplemented with 2 mmol/L l-glutamine and adjusted to pH 7.4. The following compounds were prepared in assay medium and loaded into the injection ports of the hydrated sensor cartridge: port A, 20 μL of 100 mmol/L glucose to achieve a final concentration of 10 mmol/L; port B, 22 μL of 10 μmol/L oligomycin to achieve a final concentration of approximately 1 μmol/L; and port C, 25 μL of 500 mmol/L 2-deoxy-d-glucose (2-DG) to achieve a final concentration of approximately 50 mmol/L. Following sensor cartridge calibration, the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was measured before and after sequential injection of glucose, oligomycin, and 2-DG. Glycolysis was calculated as the increase in ECAR following glucose injection relative to the pre-glucose baseline, whereas glycolytic capacity was calculated as the maximum ECAR following oligomycin injection after subtraction of nonglycolytic acidification. After completion of the assays, cells in each well were lysed, and protein concentrations were quantified for normalization. OCR and ECAR data were normalized to the corresponding protein content and analyzed using Wave software (Seahorse Wave Controller 2.6, https://www.agilent.com/en/products/cell-analysis/wave-controller-2-6-seahorse-xfe-analyzer).


### TMRE assay
  Mitochondrial membrane potential was measured using tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE; Cat. No. C2001S, Beyotime). LN229 and patient-derived GSC-267 cells expressing sgCtrl or either of 2 independent sgRNAs targeting SLC38A2 were cultured under normoxic or hypoxic conditions and treated with PBS or GSH-MEE as indicated. Cells were collected, dissociated into single-cell suspensions, and incubated with 100 nmol/L TMRE for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. An aliquot of control cells treated with 20 μmol/L FCCP for 10 min was used as a mitochondrial-depolarization control. After staining, cells were washed once and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry. TMRE fluorescence was collected in the phycoerythrin-compatible channel using identical acquisition settings for all samples. Cell debris and doublets were excluded, and mitochondrial membrane potential was quantified as the median TMRE fluorescence intensity of singlet cells. Values were normalized to the corresponding normoxic sgCtrl. Three independent biological experiments were performed.


### Chromatin immunoprecipitation
  Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were performed using the EZ-Magna ChIP A/G Kit (Cat. No. 17-10086, Millipore) following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde, quenched with glycine, lysed, and sonicated to shear chromatin into fragments of 200 to 1,000 bp. The sheared chromatin was immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (anti-HIF-1α) antibody and control IgG. Immune complexes were washed sequentially with low-salt, high-salt, LiCl, and Tris–EDTA buffers, then eluted and reverse-cross-linked by proteinase K digestion at 62 °C. Purified DNA was subsequently analyzed by qPCR using primers targeting the promoter regions of SLC38A2. The primer sequences for ChIP–qPCR are listed in Table S2.


### Chemical shift imaging in patients with GBM
  Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and chemical shift imaging (CSI) were performed 1 to 2 d before surgery using a 3.0-T MRI scanner (Magnetom Skyra, Siemens Healthcare) equipped with a 20-channel transmit/receive head and neck coil. T2-weighted images were acquired for tumor localization and region-of-interest selection, followed by localized proton CSI with magnetic-field shimming and water suppression. Glx was defined as the combined glutamate and glutamine signal. The software-defined Glx1 component was used for visualization and comparisons between imaging-defined normoxic and hypoxic tumor regions. Spatial maps were displayed as lactate-to-creatine (Lac/Cr) and Glx1-to-creatine (Glx1/Cr) ratios. Creatine was used as the internal reference for calculating the Lac/Cr and Glx1/Cr ratios. Glx1 values annotated on representative spectra were fitted peak integrals expressed in arbitrary units (a.u.).

Combined Glx signals, without subdivision into Glx1, Glx2, and Glx3, were used for visualization and quantification. Lactate and Glx maps were reconstructed, and fitted spectral peak integrals for comparisons between imaging-defined normoxic and hypoxic tumor regions were expressed in arbitrary units

### Structure-based virtual screening
  The predicted 3-dimensional structure of SLC38A2 was obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). A library of 3,067 US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved compounds was obtained from Selleck Chemicals (https://www.selleck.cn/) and initially filtered according to Lipinski’s rule of 5, namely, molecular weight < 500, cLogP < 5, hydrogen-bond donors < 5, and hydrogen-bond acceptors < 10, yielding 1,955 compounds for molecular docking. The SLC38A2 receptor and ligand structures were prepared using AutoDock Tools (version 1.5.6, https://ccsb.scripps.edu/mgltools/1-5-6/). Water molecules, co-crystallized ligands, and ions were removed from the receptor, followed by the addition of polar hydrogens and Kollman charges. Receptor and ligand structures were converted to PDBQT format and subjected to blind docking using AutoDock Vina, with the docking box encompassing the entire SLC38A2 protein. Docking results were visualized and analyzed using Discovery Studio Client (version 19.1.0.18287, https://www.3ds.com/products/biovia/discovery-studio).


### Proximity ligation assay
  The proximity ligation assay (PLA) was performed using the Duolink In Situ PLA kit (Cat. No. 92101, Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were seeded onto chamber slides and subjected to the indicated treatments. After washing with PBS, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde prepared in PBS for 15 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. Cells were then incubated with Duolink blocking solution at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with SLC38A2 and Kla antibodies derived from different species to detect the target proteins. After washing, cells were incubated with species-specific secondary antibodies conjugated with Duolink PLA PLUS and MINUS probes at 37 °C for 1 h. Ligation was performed using Duolink ligation solution for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by rolling-circle amplification with amplification solution for 100 min at 37 °C. After the final washing steps, slides were mounted using Duolink PLA mounting medium containing DAPI and incubated for 15 min. PLA signals were visualized and captured using a Leica SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope. The number of PLA fluorescent puncta was quantified using the ImageJ software (version 1.54r, https://wsr.imagej.net/notes.html).


### Statistical analysis
  Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1. Data are presented as mean ± SD or mean ± standard error of the mean, as indicated. Comparisons between 2 groups were analyzed using 2-tailed Student t tests. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using 1-way or 2-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s or Bonferroni’s post hoc test. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.


## Results

### Hypoxia drives spatial metabolic mismatch and glutamine consumption
  Clinical MRS contains rich, underexploited information about the spatial organization of tumor metabolism. Reanalysis of MRS/CSI data from our GBM patient cohort revealed a spatial metabolic mismatch between lactate and Glx-related signals. Creatine was used as the internal reference for spectral normalization; voxels with high lactate-to-creatine ratios (Lac/Cr), consistent with hypoxic glycolysis, exhibited reduced Glx1-to-creatine ratios (Glx1/Cr) (Fig. 1A). Quantification of the corresponding metabolite signals further showed increased lactate and decreased Glx1 signals in imaging-defined hypoxic regions (Fig. 1B). This imaging-defined mismatch was independently validated in patient-derived specimens, where the tumor core displayed lower glutamine but higher l-lactate compared with the tumor periphery (Fig. 1C).

  To determine whether this spatial pattern represents a generalizable feature of GBM rather than a patient-specific phenomenon, we performed CSI in intracranial xenograft-bearing mice (Fig. 1D). Spatial metabolite maps recapitulated the human observations, demonstrating focal lactate enrichment with spatially coupled Glx depletion within the same tumor territories (Fig. 1E). Consistent with the notion that these lactate-enriched zones correspond to hypoxic niches, quantification showed an elevated lactate magnitude but a decreased Glx magnitude in hypoxic versus normoxic regions (Fig. 1F). A reduction in intracellular glutamine in hypoxic regions could arise from decreased production or increased utilization. To resolve this, we implemented a dual-vector hypoxia fate-mapping system enabling lineage marking of tumor cells experiencing hypoxia (DsRed in normoxia; Cre-mediated switch to GFP under hypoxia) followed by flow cytometric sorting (Fig. 1G) [36]. Metabolite profiling of sorted populations demonstrated that GFP+ hypoxic tumor cells contained higher intracellular l-lactate but markedly lower glutamine than DsRed+ normoxic cells (Fig. 1H), directly confirming that glutamine depletion occurred within hypoxia-exposed tumor cells in vivo. Notably, hypoxia increased the net glutamine uptake in the established adherent LN229 line and in 2 independent patient-derived glioma stem-cell models, GSC-267 and GSC-20 (Fig. 1I), indicating that glutamine depletion in hypoxic cells reflects accelerated acquisition and turnover rather than reduced import. To define the metabolic fate of this increased glutamine influx, we performed metabolic tracing using 13C5-labeled glutamine to follow the incorporation of glutamine-derived carbon into downstream metabolites (Fig. 1J). In GSC-267 cells, hypoxia increased the m5 fractional contribution of intracellular glutamine, glutamate, glutathione (GSH), and citrate. In contrast, the m5 fractions of proline and α-ketoglutarate were reduced under hypoxia (Fig. 1K and Fig. S1A). Functionally, glutamine depletion markedly increased apoptosis under hypoxia compared with normoxia in both LN229 and GSC-267 cells (Fig. S1B). Collectively, these findings linked the spatial pattern of lactate accumulation and glutamine depletion in hypoxic GBM to increased glutamine uptake, enhanced incorporation of glutamine-derived carbon into GSH, and greater dependence on glutamine for cell survival.


### CRISPR screening identifies SLC38A2 as essential for hypoxic survival
  To systematically identify genes required for GBM cell survival within hypoxic tumor niches, we combined a hypoxia fate-mapping system with an in vivo genome-wide CRISPR–CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) knockout screen. Hypoxia fate-mapping GSC-267 cells were transduced with Cas9 and a genome-wide sgRNA library and subsequently implanted intracranially to establish orthotopic xenografts. After 21 d of tumor growth, the tumors were dissociated, and hypoxia-experienced and normoxic tumor cell populations were isolated by flow cytometric sorting. Genomic DNA was extracted from the 2 populations, and the integrated sgRNA sequences were amplified and quantified by high-throughput sequencing (Fig. 2A). Differential sgRNA abundance between the hypoxia-experienced and normoxic populations was analyzed using a maximum-likelihood estimation algorithm. Genes with negative log2 fold-change values were defined as negatively selected candidates, because the depletion of their corresponding sgRNAs in the hypoxia-experienced population indicated that gene loss selectively impaired tumor cell survival under hypoxic stress. Functional enrichment analysis of the top 500 negatively selected genes revealed marked enrichment of pathways associated with oxidative stress responses, apoptotic mitochondrial changes, basic amino acid transport, and glutamate metabolism (Fig. S2A), indicating that redox homeostasis, mitochondrial integrity, and nutrient acquisition are critical for GBM cell persistence within hypoxic niches. Global ranking of the screening results identified SLC38A2 among the top 50 negatively selected genes. SLC38A2 exhibited a strong depletion signal in the hypoxia-experienced population, with a log2 fold change of −6.77 and a P value of 0.004 (Fig. S2B and C). Because SLC38A2 encodes a glutamine-transporting neutral amino acid transporter [37] and was associated with the amino acid transport and glutamate metabolism pathways enriched in our CRISPR screen, we prioritized SLC38A2 for subsequent spatial, metabolic, and functional validation. At baseline, sgCtrl and sgSLC38A2 GSC-267 cells were comparably represented; after 48 h, sgSLC38A2 cells were selectively depleted under 1% O2 but not 20% O2, indicating a hypoxia-specific fitness disadvantage (Fig. 2B). The clinical relevance of SLC38A2 was rigorously validated across multiple dimensions. In patient-derived GBM tissues, IF revealed a striking spatial concordance between SLC38A2 protein expression and the hypoxia marker CA9 (Fig. 2C). Multiplex IF further demonstrated extensive overlap between SLC38A2 and the established glioma cell marker oligodendrocyte transcription factor 2 (OLIG2), whereas morphologically identifiable vascular-like structures showed minimal SLC38A2 staining (Fig. S2D), supporting a predominantly tumor-cell-enriched expression pattern. Spatial transcriptomic and single-cell RNA-seq analyses showed that SLC38A2 expression was highest in the hypoxic tumor core and correlated with hypoxia and glutamine metabolism signatures (Fig. 2D to G and Fig. S2E to G). Analysis of the Ivy Glioblastoma Atlas further revealed region-specific expression of SLC38A2, with the highest expression observed in microvascular proliferation and notable expression also detected in PAN (Fig. S2H). Consistently, GEPIA analysis showed elevated SLC38A2 expression in glioma tissues compared with normal brain tissues (Fig. S2I). Survival analyses in the CGGA glioma and Rembrandt cohorts further showed that higher SLC38A2 expression was associated with poorer overall survival (Fig. S2J). Consistent with these transcriptomic findings, immunohistochemical analysis of clinical specimens showed progressively increased SLC38A2 protein expression with increasing glioma grade (Fig. S2K). Mechanistically, SLC38A2 functions as a system A amino acid transporter, theoretically positioning it as the rate-limiting gateway for the glutamine influx required for GSH synthesis (Fig. S2L). CRISPR-mediated ablation of SLC38A2 in LN229, GSC-267, and GSC-20 cells (Fig. S2M) caused a marked reduction in intracellular glutamine and GSH pools, which was exacerbated under hypoxia and was accompanied by increased apoptosis (Fig. 2H and I and Fig. S2N and O). Finally, in the orthotopic GSC-267 human xenograft model, SLC38A2 depletion reduced bioluminescent tumor burden and prolonged survival compared with the corresponding control group. Consistent antitumor effects were also observed in the syngeneic GL261 model established in C57BL/6J mice, in which Slc38a2 depletion similarly suppressed intracranial tumor growth and extended survival (Fig. 2J and K). Longitudinal body-weight monitoring showed no significant differences among the sgCtrl and SLC38A2-deficient groups in either BALB/c nude or C57BL/6J mice, with no evident body-weight loss during the observation period (Fig. S2P and Q). Together, these findings identify SLC38A2 as a hypoxia-associated metabolic dependency that sustains glutamine–GSH homeostasis and supports GBM cell survival within hypoxic tumor niches.


### SLC38A2 ablation triggers oxidative catastrophe and mitochondrial fission
  Given that hypoxia increases glutamine dependency and promotes glutamine flux into GSH biosynthesis, we asked whether SLC38A2—identified as a key glutamine transporter in hypoxic GBM—functions to buffer oxidative stress [38–40]. LN229 and patient-derived GSC-267 cells were selected as complementary GBM models for mechanistic studies: LN229 provides an established adherent system suitable for genetic and metabolic analyses, whereas GSC-267 retains patient-derived stemlike characteristics and robust intracranial tumorigenicity. To directly define the bioenergetic consequences of SLC38A2 loss, we performed Seahorse Mito Stress and Glycolysis Stress assays under normoxic and hypoxic conditions using 2 independent SLC38A2-targeting sgRNAs. SLC38A2 deletion reduced ATP-linked respiration and spare respiratory capacity in both cell models; the reduction was moderate under normoxia but substantially greater under hypoxia, particularly for spare respiratory capacity (Fig. 3A and Fig. S3A). GSH-MEE partially restored both parameters, indicating that glutamine-derived GSH contributes to mitochondrial energetic flexibility. Glycolysis stress analysis further showed that hypoxia increased glycolysis and glycolytic capacity in control cells, whereas SLC38A2 deletion attenuated both responses and GSH-MEE partially restored them (Fig. 3B and Fig. S3B). Thus, SLC38A2 loss compromises both mitochondrial respiration and compensatory glycolytic adaptation. Consistent with this bioenergetic impairment, SLC38A2 depletion caused a pronounced increase in intracellular ROS under hypoxia in LN229, GSC-267, and the independent GSC-20 model, and this increase was alleviated by GSH-MEE (Fig. S3C). MitoTracker imaging demonstrated marked hypoxia-associated mitochondrial fragmentation, with reduced mitochondrial length and area in LN229 cells (Fig. 3C and D) and a concordant phenotype in GSC-267 cells (Fig. S3D). MitoSOX analysis showed that SLC38A2 depletion further amplified hypoxia-induced mitochondrial ROS in both models (Fig. 3E and Fig. S4A), whereas mitochondrial mass remained unchanged (Fig. S4B and C). Direct assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential by TMRE staining revealed only a modest decline after SLC38A2 deletion under normoxia but pronounced mitochondrial depolarization under hypoxia; GSH-MEE partially restored TMRE fluorescence in both LN229 and GSC-267 cells (Fig. 3F and Fig. S4D). Transmission electron microscopy further demonstrated severe hypoxia-associated mitochondrial ultrastructural damage after SLC38A2 depletion, which was mitigated by GSH-MEE (Fig. S4E and F). These redox and mitochondrial defects translated into increased annexin V-positive apoptosis, most prominently under hypoxia, with partial rescue by GSH-MEE in LN229 and GSC-20 cells and concordant vulnerability in GSC-267 cells (Fig. 3G and Fig. S4G and H). Collectively, these findings establish that the SLC38A2–glutamine–GSH axis coordinates redox buffering, mitochondrial membrane potential, respiratory reserve, and glycolytic adaptation during hypoxic stress.


### HIF-1α and lactate dually regulate SLC38A2 expression
  To define how SLC38A2 is up-regulated in hypoxic GBM, we first interrogated hypoxia-inducible factors. Under hypoxia, SLC38A2 induction was largely abrogated by HIF-1α knockdown (Fig. 4A and Fig. S5A and B), whereas hypoxia-inducible factor 2 alpha (HIF-2α) depletion did not suppress SLC38A2 expression (Fig. S5C to E). Motif analysis identified 3 candidate HIF-1α-binding sites in the SLC38A2 promoter, and ChIP–qPCR demonstrated selective enrichment at the P2 site (Fig. 4B). A WT SLC38A2 promoter reporter was strongly activated by HIF-1α, whereas mutation of P2 abolished this activation (Fig. S5F), establishing HIF-1α-P2 as a hypoxia-responsive cis-element. Because hypoxic tumor regions were also lactate-rich, we examined posttranslational regulation. Galloflavin did not significantly reduce the hypoxia-induced increase in SLC38A2 mRNA but attenuated SLC38A2 protein accumulation (Fig. S5G and H), indicating that lactate is dispensable for transcriptional induction but contributes to protein stabilization. Cycloheximide chase analysis showed that endogenous SLC38A2 degraded more slowly under hypoxia than under normoxia (Fig. S5I), and exogenous l-lactate similarly prolonged SLC38A2 half-life (Fig. 4C). LC–MS/MS identified lactylation of SLC38A2 at Lys472 under hypoxia (Fig. 4D) and ubiquitination at Lys468 and Lys472 (Fig. S5J), suggesting modification-dependent control of protein turnover. To identify the responsible regulator without a priori candidate selection, we performed unbiased immunoprecipitation–MS of endogenous SLC38A2-associated proteins in LN229 cells. AARS1 was detected in the SLC38A2 immunoprecipitate but not in the isotype-matched IgG control (Tables S4 and S5), leading us to prioritize it for mechanistic validation. Co-IP confirmed the association between SLC38A2 and AARS1 in LN229 and GSC-267 cells (Fig. 4E). AARS1 knockdown reduced SLC38A2 abundance, whereas proteasome inhibition with MG132 restored SLC38A2 levels. Separately, l-lactate increased SLC38A2 abundance under control conditions, while this difference was attenuated in the presence of MG132 in both LN229 and GSC-267 cells (Fig. 4F and G). Duolink PLA further showed that AARS1 depletion markedly decreased SLC38A2 lactylation signals (Fig. 4H and I). In 293T cells, AARS1 knockdown reduced SLC38A2 lactylation and increased ubiquitination, whereas l-lactate partially reversed these effects (Fig. 4J). Chain-specific assays demonstrated that this increase was predominantly K48-linked, rather than K63-linked, ubiquitination; hypoxia reduced K48-linked ubiquitination and increased lactylation, whereas AARS1 depletion reversed this shift (Fig. S5K). Finally, mutational analysis showed that K472R diminished SLC38A2 lactylation and markedly increased K48-linked ubiquitination, whereas K468R showed no appreciable difference from WT-OE; by contrast, the lactylation-mimetic K472Q mutant displayed substantially lower K48-linked ubiquitination than K472R in both LN229 and GSC-267 cells (Fig. 4K and L). l-Lactate also failed to stabilize K472R as effectively as the control constructs (Fig. S5L). Together, these data support a dual-regulatory model in which hypoxia induces SLC38A2 transcription through HIF-1α and promotes AARS1-dependent K472 lactylation, thereby limiting degradation-associated K48 ubiquitination and sustaining SLC38A2 protein stability.


### Zonisamide targets SLC38A2 to dismantle hypoxic metabolic adaptation
  To develop a clinically tractable strategy to target SLC38A2, we screened a library of 3,067 FDA-approved compounds. After Lipinski-based drug-likeness filtering, 1,955 compounds were retained and subsequently docked against the AlphaFold-predicted SLC38A2 structure using AutoDock Vina. Top-ranked candidates were then prioritized for experimental validation (Fig. 5A and Fig. S6A). Among the top-ranked hits, the brain-penetrant anticonvulsant zonisamide was selected due to its favorable safety profile and blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability [41]. Docking analysis predicted a defined binding pocket within SLC38A2, with zonisamide engaging key residues through a network of hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts (predicted binding free energy = −9.9 kcal/mol) (Fig. 5B and C). CETSA showed greater retention of SLC38A2 protein at elevated temperatures following zonisamide treatment compared with dimethyl sulfoxide, consistent with the enhanced thermal stability of SLC38A2 (Fig. 5D). SPR further demonstrated the concentration-dependent binding of zonisamide to SLC38A2, with an equilibrium dissociation constant of approximately 15.2 μmol/L (Fig. 5E). Dose–response analyses showed greater zonisamide sensitivity under hypoxia in LN229, GSC-267, and the independent GSC-20 model (Fig. S6B). To define its pharmacological action, we treated LN229 and GSC-267 cells with 0 to 1,600 μmol/L zonisamide under both oxygen conditions. Zonisamide did not substantially alter total SLC38A2 protein abundance but progressively reduced glutamine uptake and intracellular GSH, with the responses approaching a plateau at higher concentrations (Fig. S6C to E). Consistently, treatment at the selected experimental dose reduced intracellular glutamine and GSH most strongly under hypoxia (Fig. 5F), supporting the functional inhibition of SLC38A2-mediated transport rather than the down-regulation of the transporter. Genetic perturbation experiments further supported a contribution of SLC38A2 to the effects of zonisamide. In LN229 and GSC-267 cells, zonisamide decreased intracellular glutamine and GSH levels and increased apoptosis in sgCtrl cells. SLC38A2 knockout produced changes in the same directions, with no statistically significant additional effects of zonisamide detected in knockout cells (Fig. S6F to I). Phenotypically, zonisamide recapitulated the mitochondrial stress program induced by genetic SLC38A2 depletion. MitoTracker imaging showed hypoxia-aggravated mitochondrial fragmentation in LN229 and GSC-267 cells (Fig. 5G), accompanied by increased mitochondrial ROS (Fig. 5H) and total intracellular ROS in LN229, GSC-267, and GSC-20 cells (Fig. 5I and Fig. S7A). Mitochondrial mass was not significantly altered (Fig. S7B), indicating that the enhanced ROS signal reflected dysfunction rather than increased mitochondrial content. Ultrastructural analysis confirmed mitochondrial injury, particularly under hypoxia (Fig. S7C), and annexin V/propidium iodide staining demonstrated increased apoptosis in LN229, GSC-267, and GSC-20 cells (Fig. 5J and Fig. S7D). In patient-derived GBOs, zonisamide increased terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) and cleaved caspase-3 signals (Fig. 5K). Finally, to assess whether zonisamide can suppress SLC38A2-dependent glutamine uptake in vivo, we performed (2S,4S)-4-[18F]FEBGln PET–CT imaging in an intracranial xenograft model following systemic zonisamide administration (Fig. S7E). Zonisamide-treated tumors exhibited reduced tracer uptake compared with controls (Fig. S7F), consistent with impaired glutamine transport in the tumor microenvironment. Collectively, these findings support zonisamide as a functional inhibitor of SLC38A2-mediated glutamine transport that disrupts glutamine–GSH redox adaptation and increases the vulnerability of GBM cells to hypoxic stress.


### Zonisamide enhances the antitumor efficacy of anti-angiogenic therapy in vivo
  Bevacizumab is known to aggravate intratumoral hypoxia by suppressing angiogenesis [42], thereby creating a therapeutic window in which hypoxic GBM cells become highly reliant on SLC38A2-mediated glutamine acquisition for redox buffering. We therefore tested whether SLC38A2 inhibition could convert bevacizumab-induced hypoxia from a survival advantage into a metabolic liability in vivo. In an orthotopic GSC-267 xenograft model, genetic SLC38A2 depletion enhanced the antitumor efficacy of bevacizumab. Compared with either intervention alone, the combination resulted in a greater reduction in bioluminescent tumor burden and prolonged overall survival (n = 5; Fig. 6A to C). We next evaluated pharmacological SLC38A2 inhibition with zonisamide in combination with bevacizumab (Fig. 6D). Longitudinal imaging demonstrated that combining bevacizumab with zonisamide, a functional inhibitor of SLC38A2-mediated glutamine transport, reduced tumor burden and prolonged survival compared with bevacizumab alone (n = 5; Fig. 6E and F). Ex vivo analyses showed that combining bevacizumab with SLC38A2 inhibition increased mitochondrial ROS, reduced Ki67 staining, and increased cleaved caspase-3 positivity (Fig. 6G and H). Analogous antitumor effects were observed in the immunocompetent GL261 model, in which B20-4.1.1, a murine anti-vascular endothelial growth factor surrogate for bevacizumab, was used as the anti-angiogenic agent. Treatment was associated with reduced tumor burden, prolonged survival, increased MitoSOX signals, decreased Ki67 staining, and increased cleaved caspase-3 positivity (Fig. S8A to H). Longitudinal body-weight monitoring showed no significant treatment-associated weight loss after genetic SLC38A2 depletion, anti-angiogenic therapy, or anti-angiogenic therapy combined with zonisamide at 50, 100, or 150 mg/kg in either BALB/c nude or C57BL/6J mice (Fig. S8I to L), indicating that the tested regimens were generally well tolerated as assessed by body weight. Collectively, these results demonstrate that bevacizumab creates a hypoxia-enforced dependency on the SLC38A2 axis and that disrupting this axis with zonisamide converts hypoxia into a lethal oxidative trap that suppresses GBM growth and extends survival in vivo.


## Discussion
  GBM adapts to the hypoxic tumor microenvironment through extensive metabolic plasticity, which represents a major barrier to effective treatment [43–45]. In this study, we characterized the spatial organization of this adaptation, identifying a coordinated lactate–glutamine axis. Beyond its role as a glycolytic byproduct, we demonstrated that lactate functions as a local signaling molecule. Through AARS1-mediated lactylation, lactate stabilizes the glutamine transporter SLC38A2. Together with HIF-1α, this regulatory mechanism sustains GSH biosynthesis and mitigates oxidative stress (Fig. 7). These findings establish SLC38A2 as a key metabolic dependency enriched in the hypoxic tumor core.

  The metabolic flux analyses extend the function of SLC38A2 beyond glutamine uptake alone. SLC38A2 deletion caused a substantially greater reduction in ATP-linked respiration and spare respiratory capacity under hypoxia than under normoxia and impaired the compensatory increase in glycolysis and glycolytic capacity. The parallel loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, together with increased mitochondrial ROS, fragmentation, and ultrastructural damage, indicates that SLC38A2 is required to preserve mitochondrial energetic flexibility during hypoxic stress. GSH-MEE partially restored OCR- and ECAR-derived parameters and mitochondrial membrane potential, identifying impaired GSH-dependent redox buffering as a major contributor to the phenotype. The incomplete rescue also suggests that additional glutamine-dependent processes, including amino acid homeostasis and biosynthetic metabolism, contribute to SLC38A2 function.

  Clinically, these results provide a mechanistic rationale for overcoming resistance to anti-angiogenic therapies. Agents such as bevacizumab often fail to improve overall survival in GBM because they exacerbate intratumoral hypoxia, which promotes a metabolically resistant phenotype [46–48]. Our data indicate that this therapy-induced hypoxia can be targeted to induce synthetic lethality. By using zonisamide to inhibit SLC38A2, we effectively overcome the tumor’s hypoxic adaptation. Under these conditions, bevacizumab-induced hypoxia shifts from a mechanism of resistance to a vulnerability, leading to oxidative cell death and tumor suppression.

  The genetic epistasis experiments further strengthen the target assignment of zonisamide. In control cells, zonisamide reduced intracellular glutamine and GSH and induced apoptosis, whereas these additional pharmacological effects were largely lost after SLC38A2 knockout. Because genetic ablation had already reproduced the metabolic and cytotoxic phenotypes induced by zonisamide, the lack of an additive response indicates that the genetic and pharmacological interventions converge predominantly on the same SLC38A2-dependent glutamine–GSH pathway. Together with cellular target-engagement, direct-binding, dose–response, and in vivo glutamine-imaging data, these findings support SLC38A2 as a major mediator of the anti-GBM effects of zonisamide.

  The identification of zonisamide as an inhibitor of SLC38A2 offers a practical strategy for further clinical evaluation. Achieving adequate BBB penetration remains a primary challenge in neuro-oncology drug development [49–51]. As an FDA-approved anticonvulsant, zonisamide readily crosses the BBB and has a well-documented safety profile with established dosing regimens [52–54]. The enhanced antitumor efficacy observed in our orthotopic models supports repurposing zonisamide in combination with standard anti-angiogenic treatments. Because both bevacizumab and zonisamide are clinically available, this combination strategy accelerates potential clinical evaluation, supporting further clinical investigation to assess safety and efficacy in patients with recurrent GBM.


## Conclusions
  In this study, we elucidate a spatially coordinated lactate–glutamine metabolic axis that drives hypoxic adaptation and therapy resistance in GBM. We demonstrate that the hypoxic tumor microenvironment enforces a strict dependency on the glutamine transporter SLC38A2, which actively channels glutamine into GSH biosynthesis to neutralize oxidative stress. This dependency is maintained by a dual-regulatory mechanism: HIF-1α-mediated transcriptional activation and AARS1-dependent protein lactylation induced by local lactate signaling. By identifying the brain-penetrant anticonvulsant zonisamide as a potent SLC38A2 inhibitor, we provide a clinically tractable pharmacological intervention. Ultimately, our findings reveal that disrupting SLC38A2-mediated glutamine reprogramming converts bevacizumab-induced hypoxia from a survival advantage into a lethal oxidative vulnerability. This work provides a mechanistic rationale and a readily translatable combinational therapeutic strategy to overcome anti-angiogenic resistance in patients with GBM.

# Molecular mechanisms of AcrVA-mediated Cas12a inhibition and their potential applications


## Abstract
  CRISPR-Cas12a is widely used for genome editing because it is simple, efficient, and suitable for multiplex targeting. However, strong or prolonged Cas12a activity can increase unwanted cleavage at off-target sites. This problem is especially important when Cas12a is used in complex microbial communities, where unintended editing may affect non-target microorganisms or mobile genetic elements. Anti-CRISPR (Acr) proteins are natural inhibitors of CRISPR-Cas systems. Acr proteins targeting type V-A CRISPR-Cas12a systems are called AcrV proteins. Recent structural and biochemical studies have shown that AcrVA proteins inhibit Cas12a through highly different mechanisms. AcrVA1 induces cleavage of Cas12a-bound crRNA. AcrVA2 recognizes the nascent Cas12a polypeptide and promotes translation-dependent degradation of cas12a mRNA. AcrVA3 directly cleaves double-stranded DNA and indirectly reduces Cas12a activity. AcrVA4 allosterically blocks target DNA binding, whereas AcrVA5-AcrVA8 inhibit Cas12a by acetylating important lysine residues. These studies provide a structural basis for using AcrVA proteins as potential Cas12a off-switches. Importantly, AcrV proteins may reduce off-target effects by limiting the strength and duration of Cas12a activity after sufficient on-target editing has occurred. In this mini review, we summarize the structural mechanisms of AcrVA proteins and critically discuss the evidence and experimental requirements for their potential use in controlled Cas12a-based microbial engineering.


## Introduction
  CRISPR-Cas12 systems are adaptive immune systems that protect bacteria and archaea from foreign DNA (Barrangou et al., 2007). Cas12 nucleases are also useful genome-editing tools. Cas12 family proteins are programmable nucleases that can target and cleave specific DNA or RNA sequences, depending on the Cas12 subtype. Cas12a, the best-studied member, requires only one short CRISPR RNA (crRNA), recognizes a protospacer-adjacent motif (PAM), and produces staggered DNA breaks (Yamano et al., 2016; Zetsche et al., 2015). Cas12a can process its own crRNA array, allowing several genes to be edited or regulated simultaneously (Zetsche et al., 2017). These properties make Cas12a useful for microbial engineering. Cas12a can be used to improve metabolic pathways (Liu et al., 2019), control pathogens (Lee et al., 2025), and modify microbial communities for bioremediation or industrial production (Hou et al., 2025). However, Cas12a may also cleave DNA sequences similar but not identical to the intended target, resulting in off-target effects (Strohkendl et al., 2018). Off-target cleavage depends on the Cas12 ortholog, guide sequence, PAM, mismatch position, Cas12 concentration, and exposure time (Kleinstiver et al., 2016). The duration of Cas activity is an important factor. Most on-target editing occurs during the early period after Cas and guide delivery. If strong Cas activity remains for a long time, the nuclease has more opportunity to recognize and cleave partially matched DNA. Therefore, prolonged Cas12a activity may increase accumulated off-target editing. Limiting Cas12a activity after sufficient on-target editing may improve the ratio between on-target and off-target cleavage. This idea has been clearly demonstrated for Cas9 and may also provide an important strategy for controlling Cas12a activity (Shin et al., 2017).

  Anti-CRISPR proteins are small proteins produced mainly by phages and other mobile genetic elements. They inhibit CRISPR-Cas immunity and allow foreign DNA to escape bacterial defense (Bondy-Denomy et al., 2013). Acr proteins that inhibit type V-A CRISPR-Cas systems are named AcrVA proteins. Eight AcrVA proteins, AcrVA1-AcrVA8, have now been reported (Dong et al., 2019; Marino et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2025; Watters et al., 2018). Structural and biochemical studies show that AcrVA proteins have highly diverse structures (Figure 1A) and inhibit Cas12a through several distinct mechanisms, including crRNA cleavage, inhibition of Cas12a biogenesis, dsDNA cleavage, allosteric inhibition, and lysine acetylation. Thus, their structural and mechanistic diversity provides multiple ways to control Cas12a activity (Figure 1B).

  Understanding the structural basis and inhibitory mechanisms of Acr proteins is important for selecting and applying suitable inhibitors for specific purposes. In this mini review, we summarize recent structural and mechanism studies of AcrVA proteins. We focus on how individual AcrVA proteins inhibit Cas12a and how these mechanisms may be used for temporal control of Cas12a activity (Figure 1C). Such temporal regulation has the potential to reduce prolonged Cas12a activity and thereby limit off-target editing, although this possibility requires direct experimental validation.


## Cas12a activity and the risk of off-target editing
  Cas12a has two major lobes that form a central channel for crRNA and target DNA. The WED domain supports crRNA recognition and processing (Yamano et al., 2016). The PAM-interacting region first recognizes the PAM, and the REC domains help form the RNA-DNA heteroduplex (Yamano et al., 2016). DNA cleavage then occurs in the RuvC catalytic site. These steps include several structural changes that can be targeted by inhibitor proteins (Swarts and Jinek, 2019; Yamano et al., 2016).

  Cas12a specificity is not determined only by whether a sequence is complementary to the crRNA. Cas12a must first bind a PAM, open the DNA duplex, and extend an R-loop. A fully matched target normally passes these steps more rapidly and efficiently than a partially matched target. However, high Cas12a concentration or long exposure can allow slower interactions with partially matched sites. This increases the chance of unwanted cleavage. For this reason, reducing the total active time of Cas12a is a reasonable strategy to reduce off-target effects. RNP delivery already provides a shorter activity period than continuous expression. Inducible promoters and protein degradation tags can further limit activity. AcrVA proteins provide another option because they directly inhibit Cas12a or remove a component required for Cas12a function. Structural information is important for selecting an AcrVA with the correct timing, strength, reversibility, and Cas12a specificity.


## Structural mechanisms of AcrVA anti-CRISPR family

### AcrVA1 cleaves Cas12a-bound crRNA
  AcrVA1 is a broad inhibitor of several Cas12a orthologs. Cryo-EM structures showed that AcrVA1 binds to the central channel of the Cas12a-crRNA complex by contacting the PAM-interacting (PI), WED, REC, and nuclease regions (Figure 1B; Knott et al., 2019a; Zhang et al., 2019). This position interferes with target DNA binding. More importantly, AcrVA1 induces cleavage of the seed region of the Cas12a-bound crRNA. The shortened crRNA cannot recognize target DNA efficiently.

  AcrVA1 is a multiple-turnover inhibitor capable of inactivating several Cas12a–crRNA complexes (Knott et al., 2019b; Zhang et al., 2019). This property may enable efficient Cas12a shutdown under the tested in vitro conditions, although the rate and extent of inhibition may depend on the AcrVA1 concentration, delivery timing, and Cas12a ortholog. Careful timing is required because premature AcrVA1 expression may also suppress the desired on-target editing. Together, the cryo-EM structure, crRNA-cleavage assays, and multiple-turnover activity consistently support crRNA cleavage as the primary inhibitory mechanism of AcrVA1. This foundational work showed that AcrVA1 inactivates Cas12a–crRNA complexes by cleaving the bound crRNA and can act on multiple complexes, rather than simply blocking a Cas12a interaction surface. However, it remains unclear whether cleavage kinetics differ among Cas12a orthologs and how AcrVA1 and the cleaved crRNA are released from Cas12a in cells.


### AcrVA2 prevents Cas12a production
  The mechanism of AcrVA2 was unclear for a long time because AcrVA2 did not strongly inhibit purified Cas12a RNP in standard in vitro cleavage assays. A recent study showed that AcrVA2 acts during Cas12a synthesis (Marino et al., 2026). AcrVA2 recognizes conserved amino acids near the N-terminus of the newly translated Cas12a polypeptide. It then promotes selective degradation of cas12a mRNA in a translation-dependent manner. Conserved residues in the C-terminal region of AcrVA2 are also required for its association with ribosomes and polysomes. Thus, AcrVA2 suppresses new Cas12a production but does not inactivate pre-existing Cas12a-crRNA RNPs. It should therefore be considered a regulator of Cas12a biogenesis, not a rapid post-editing off-switch. The crystal structure of free AcrVA2 was determined at 2.30 Å resolution (PDB ID: 7CI1). In contrast, the proposed interaction between AcrVA2 and the nascent N-terminal Cas12a peptide is based on a predicted model (Marino et al., 2026). A high-resolution structure of AcrVA2 bound to a ribosome-nascent Cas12a complex will be important for understanding this unusual mechanism and for designing Cas12a variants with controlled AcrVA2 sensitivity. Thus, the translation dependence of mRNA degradation and the failure to inhibit purified Cas12a RNPs strongly support regulation at the biogenesis stage. Nevertheless, direct recognition of the nascent Cas12a peptide remains supported mainly by prediction and functional association, because neither a high-resolution ribosome-bound complex nor detailed mutational mapping of the Cas12a interaction site is currently available. It also remains unresolved whether AcrVA2 recruits a host RNA-degradation pathway or promotes mRNA decay through another mechanism.


### AcrVA3 cleaves dsDNA and indirectly inhibits Cas12a
  AcrVA3 uses a mechanism that is clearly different from other AcrVA proteins. Our recent study determined the AcrVA3 crystal structure at 1.73 Å resolution (Han et al., 2026). AcrVA3 is a compact monomer composed of four alpha helices and a mixed beta sheet. AcrVA3 inhibited Cas12a activity in a dose-dependent manner. However, no direct binding between AcrVA3 and Cas12a was detected. Structural comparison showed weak similarity to the DNA-cleaving endonuclease I-HmuI, although the sequence identity was very low. Biochemical assays then showed that AcrVA3 cleaves dsDNA, but not ssDNA or RNA. It also cleaves both linear and supercoiled plasmid DNA, suggesting that its activity is not strongly sequence specific. Structure-based analysis identified a basic region containing R50, R58, K59, K64, K88, and K93, with D60 located near the center. Mutations of R50, D60, N76, and K93 reduced dsDNA cleavage. The same mutants also showed weaker inhibition of Cas12a. These results suggest that AcrVA3 inhibits Cas12a indirectly by degrading or modifying the DNA substrate rather than by binding to the Cas12a protein itself. The parallel loss of dsDNA cleavage and Cas12a inhibition in several AcrVA3 mutants supports a functional relationship between these activities. However, this correlation does not by itself prove that DNA cleavage is the physiological anti-CRISPR mechanism, because the substitutions may also affect DNA binding or local protein stability. Separation-of-function mutants and infection-based assays are needed to establish the native substrate and biological role of AcrVA3.

  This DNA-centered mechanism expands the known functions of anti-CRISPR proteins. However, the nonspecific dsDNA-cleavage activity of AcrVA3 may cause unintended DNA damage and is incompatible with its direct use as a genome-editing safety regulator. AcrVA3 should therefore not be considered a practical cellular off-switch in its native form. Any use of its structural features to engineer substrate-directed inhibitors remains speculative and would require elimination of nonspecific DNA cleavage followed by rigorous experimental validation.


### AcrVA4 allosterically prevents target DNA binding
  AcrVA4 is a mainly alpha-helical protein that binds to the crRNA-processing region of Cas12a formed by the WED and REC2 domains, the bridge helix (BH), and crRNA (Figure 1B). This interaction restricts the conformational changes required for crRNA–DNA heteroduplex propagation and stable R-loop formation, thereby locking Cas12a in a catalytically inactive intermediate state. Full-length AcrVA4 can dimerize and bridge two Cas12a-crRNA complexes from Lachnospiraceae bacterium (LbCas12a), producing the 2:2 assembly observed in structural studies (Knott et al., 2019a; Zhang et al., 2019). The monomeric C-terminal Cas12a-binding domain retains inhibitory activity, whereas the AcrVA4 W178A mutant retains higher-order assembly but largely loses inhibitory activity (Knott et al., 2019a; Zhang et al., 2019). These results show that the essential mechanism is direct binding of the AcrVA4 C-terminal domain to Cas12a and the resulting allosteric restriction of Cas12a conformational changes required for productive target DNA binding and stable R-loop formation, rather than AcrVA4-mediated Cas12a dimerization. AcrVA4 is also selective for specific Cas12a orthologs. It strongly inhibits LbCas12a and related proteins but does not efficiently inhibit Cas12a from Acidaminococcus sp. (AsCas12a) because a helical region of AsCas12a blocks AcrVA4 binding. Structure-guided mutation of this region can change AcrVA4 sensitivity. This result shows that AcrVA4-Cas12a recognition can be engineered. Ortholog-selective inhibition may allow one Cas12a protein to be stopped while another Cas12a protein remains active in the same microbial system. Therefore, the structural observation of a 2:2 complex should be interpreted as an assembly state rather than evidence that dimerization is required for inhibition. The monomeric C-terminal domain and W178A results provide stronger functional evidence for the allosteric model. The conformational pathway linking AcrVA4 binding to impaired R-loop formation and the extent to which this pathway is conserved among Cas12a orthologs remain unresolved.


### AcrVA5-AcrVA8 inhibit Cas12a by acetylation
  AcrVA5 is an acetyltransferase with a GCN5-related N-acetyltransferase fold. It uses acetyl-CoA to acetylate Cas12a Lys635 from Moraxella bovoculi (MbCas12a), an important residue for PAM recognition (Dong et al., 2019). Acetylation neutralizes the positive charge and causes steric interference, preventing Cas12a from recognizing target DNA. The Lys-to-Arg mutant escapes AcrVA5 inhibition, supporting the importance of this PAM-recognition residue.

  AcrVA6, AcrVA7, and AcrVA8 were recently identified in Gluconobacter oxydans. These proteins also function as acetyltransferases, but they modify different lysine residues (Wang et al., 2025). Therefore, related AcrVA proteins can inhibit the same Cas12a protein at different structural positions. These findings increase the number of possible chemical off-switches for Cas12a. Acetylation can provide strong and persistent inhibition. However, its effect may depend on cellular acetyl-CoA and deacetylase activity. The bacterial deacetylase CobB can remove AcrVA5-mediated acetylation and reactivate Cas12a (Kang et al., 2022). Therefore, the host background should be considered when acetyltransferase-type AcrVA proteins are used. For AcrVA5, the acetyltransferase fold, detection of site-specific lysine acetylation, and escape of the Lys-to-Arg Cas12a mutant collectively support inhibition through modification of the PAM-recognition site. However, substitution of a PAM-contacting residue can also alter Cas12a function, and equivalent structural and mutational evidence is less complete for AcrVA6-AcrVA8. Important unresolved questions include their substrate-recognition determinants, ortholog specificity, and the extent to which host acetyl-CoA levels and deacetylases control inhibition in vivo.


## Potential use of AcrVA proteins for temporal control of Cas12a to limit off-target effects
  The potential of anti-CRISPR proteins to reduce off-target effects in Cas-based genome-editing systems has received sustained attention and has been experimentally validated, particularly in Cas9-based systems (Shin et al., 2017). However, comparable evidence based on paired measurements of on-target and off-target editing is not yet available for Cas12a–AcrVA systems. AcrVA proteins are natural Cas12a inhibitors, but they are not naturally selective for off-target complexes. If an AcrVA is expressed at the same time as Cas12a, it will inhibit both on-target and off-target editing. Therefore, the main strategy should be temporal control. In a simple model, Cas12a and crRNA are delivered first. Rapid on-target editing occurs during an early activity window. After sufficient on-target editing, AcrVA expression or delivery is induced. The AcrVA then reduces the remaining Cas12a activity and prevents slow off-target cleavage from continuing. In this way, the final on-target level may be maintained while accumulated off-target editing is decreased. The best delay and AcrVA dose should be experimentally optimized for each Cas12a, guide, host, and delivery method. Different AcrVA mechanisms may be suitable for different control purposes. AcrVA1 may provide rapid and broad termination by destroying crRNA. AcrVA2 may control Cas12a biogenesis or continued production but cannot inhibit pre-existing RNPs. AcrVA4 may allow ortholog-selective control. AcrVA5-AcrVA8 may provide strong inhibition through covalent modification. AcrVA3 is mechanistically distinct, but its nonspecific dsDNA cleavage currently precludes its direct use as a genome-editing safety regulator.

  AcrVA expression can be controlled by inducible promoters responding to temperature, light, small molecules, quorum sensing, pollutants, or cellular stress. AcrVA proteins can also be fused to degradation tags or divided into split proteins to control their stability and assembly. Another strategy is to express an AcrVA in microbial species that should be protected from a community-delivered Cas12a editor. If AcrVA expression is restricted to selected cells and reaches an effective level, Cas12a activity is expected to be preferentially retained in cells lacking the inhibitor; incomplete or heterogeneous AcrVA expression may reduce this selectivity. AcrVA-based control should be combined with other methods. Careful crRNA design and high-fidelity Cas12a variants reduce the initial probability of off-target recognition. Targeted phage, plasmid, conjugation, or nanoparticle delivery limits which cells receive the editor. AcrVA proteins then limit how long Cas12a remains active. These three layers - sequence specificity, delivery specificity, and time control - may provide safer microbial editing than any single method alone.


## Discussion and future perspectives
  Structural studies have revealed a surprisingly wide range of AcrVA mechanisms. AcrVA2 acts during Cas12a translation. AcrVA1 cleaves the crRNA in the Cas12a RNP. AcrVA4 traps Cas12a in a DNA-binding-incompetent complex. AcrVA5-AcrVA8 acetylate Cas12a. AcrVA3 acts on dsDNA instead of directly acting on Cas12a. Together, these studies show that Cas12a can be inhibited at multiple stages including protein production, crRNA integrity, conformational activation, and substrate recognition. These inhibitory mechanisms are well supported by structural and biochemical evidence. By contrast, their ability to improve genome-editing specificity has not yet been established. Evidence for improved editing specificity remains indirect. Studies of Cas12a specificity have quantified mismatch tolerance, cleavage kinetics, and genome-wide off-target sites (Kleinstiver et al., 2016; Strohkendl et al., 2018), whereas AcrVA studies have mainly characterized protein binding, biochemical inhibition, target cleavage, Cas12a production, or acetylation. These two bodies of evidence have not yet been directly connected in experiments that compare on-target efficiency and off-target outcomes with and without AcrVA under the same conditions.

  Nevertheless, AcrVA proteins may provide a promising strategy for addressing off-target editing, which remains a major limitation of Cas12a-based genome editing. Structural studies have established important mechanistic foundations for the rational use and engineering of AcrVA proteins for this purpose. However, several limitations remain, and further structural, biochemical, and cellular studies will be required before AcrVA-mediated control can be reliably applied to improve Cas12a editing specificity. First, more structures are needed for AcrVA proteins bound to different Cas12a orthologs. In particular, the complete AcrVA2-ribosome-nascent Cas12a complex has not been structurally determined. Second, structural information should be combined with kinetic analysis. Binding affinity alone cannot predict the best inhibitor because the rate of inhibition and the timing of expression are also important. Third, engineered AcrVA proteins should be tested in real microbial communities, not only in purified systems or single laboratory strains.

  Possible industrial or environmental uses of AcrVA proteins for regulating Cas12a genome-editing systems remain speculative because direct AcrVA-specific evidence is not yet available. Even if temporal AcrVA control is shown to reduce sequence-level off-target cleavage at unintended genomic sites, it would not by itself address delivery-related risks, including inefficient, heterogeneous, or persistent distribution of Cas12a and AcrVA components, or broader ecological risks, including effects on non-target organisms, horizontal gene transfer, microbial community composition, and ecosystem function. These distinct risks should therefore be evaluated separately before AcrVA-controlled Cas12a systems are considered for industrial or environmental applications.

  In conclusion, AcrVA proteins provide several natural ways to inhibit Cas12a. Their structural diversity allows selection of a suitable inhibitor according to the Cas12a ortholog, expression system, and required shutdown mechanism. When AcrVA proteins are combined with optimized guides, high-fidelity Cas12a variants, and targeted delivery, they may reduce off-target effects by preventing excessive and prolonged Cas12a activity. However, whether AcrVA regulation can preserve on-target editing while reducing off-target outcomes remains to be demonstrated experimentally.

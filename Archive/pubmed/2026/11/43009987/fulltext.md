## Introduction
  Breast cancer is one of the most serious health threats faced by women worldwide [1, 2]. According to the 2022 global cancer burden data released by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) of the world health organization, breast cancer ranks second in terms of new cancer cases, following only lung cancer [3]. Moreover, the majority of breast cancer cases are diagnosed at advanced stages, significantly compromising patient survival. The difficulty in early diagnosis is a major factor contributing to the high mortality rate of the disease [4]. Current diagnostic methods for breast cancer include physical examination, tumor marker screening, imaging techniques, and pathological analysis [5–7]. Among them, improving the detection efficiency of early-stage tumors through tumor marker analysis has become a major focus of current research [8, 9]. In recent years, microRNAs (miRNAs) have shown great potential as liquid biopsy biomarkers, and their stability in blood makes them promising targets for non-invasive cancer diagnostics [10, 11]. Multiple clinical studies have confirmed that miR155 is a key regulatory factor in the development of breast cancer. Its expression level is significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues, making it a valuable biomarker for the diagnosis and progression monitoring of breast cancer [12–14]. However, due to its small molecular size and low abundance, traditional detection methods face challenges such as complex procedures, long processing times, and limited quantitative precision [15]. Therefore, developing highly sensitive and specific biosensors for the detection of miR155 is of great significance for clinical applications.

  Low-abundance nucleic acid biomarkers in blood are primarily detected through signal amplification strategies. Among these, the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR-associated protein (CRISPR/Cas) systems have attracted widespread attention due to their high sensitivity and specificity in nucleic acid detection [16, 17]. CRISPR/Cas is an adaptive immune system that defends against nucleic acid invasion by recognizing and specifically cleaving foreign DNA sequences homologous to those of invading viruses [18, 19]. Initially renowned as a revolutionary tool in the field of gene editing, the CRISPR/Cas system has recently expanded into in vitro diagnostics [20, 21]. Cas12a, which contains a RuvC catalytic domain, is guided by a crRNA to cleave double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) containing a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequence [22]. Once dsDNA is cleaved, trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a is activated, allowing it to non-specifically cleave single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) reporter molecules labeled with a fluorescent signal, thereby amplifying the detection signal. Due to its exceptional sensitivity, CRISPR/Cas-based detection methods have advanced rapidly in nucleic acid diagnostics. For example, Jiang et al. developed a CRISPR/Cas12a-based amplification-free strategy for the detection of NF-κB p50, achieving a limit of detection (LOD) of 0.5 pM [23]. Similarly, Gong et al. utilized CRISPR/Cas12a to detect HPV-16, reaching an LOD of 1 pM [24]. These studies confirm the high sensitivity of CRISPR/Cas-based detection systems. However, since nucleic acid biomarkers are often present at extremely low concentrations in the early stages of disease, additional amplification techniques are still required to further enhance detection sensitivity.

  Isothermal nucleic acid amplification techniques enable the amplification of target molecules under ex vivo or constant temperature conditions. These methods are simple to operate, require no complex instrumentation, and offer advantages such as rapid detection and high efficiency, making them suitable for on-site, rapid, and effective diagnostics. Commonly used isothermal amplification methods include loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) [25], hybridization chain reaction (HCR) [26], catalytic hairpin assembly (CHA) [27], and rolling circle amplification (RCA) [28]. The emergence of isothermal amplification techniques has significantly enhanced detection sensitivity, and numerous studies have applied them to nucleic acid detection. Pan Fu et al. developed a peptide nucleic acid-modified gold electrode integrated with a CHA strategy, which amplified target RNA via CHA and achieved miRNA detection at a limit as low as 2.49 fM [29]. The results were consistent with those obtained by qRT-PCR. James P. Broughton and colleagues introduced LAMP for the sensitive detection of SARS-CoV-2, reaching a detection limit of 10 copies/µL, thus providing a more efficient and convenient method for real-time SARS-CoV-2 diagnostics [30]. Recent studies have combined isothermal amplification with CRISPR/Cas-based detection to further improve both the efficiency and accuracy of target detection, offering new possibilities for disease diagnostics. Huang et al. employed RCA to generate repetitive sequences for signal amplification [31]. The amplified dsDNA was subsequently transcribed into ssRNA, which activated the trans-cleavage activity of CRISPR/Cas13a, achieving a dual signal amplification strategy. This method attained an ultralow detection limit of 200 aM, three orders of magnitude lower than that achieved by direct Cas13a-mediated miRNA detection. Compared with PCR, which requires thermal cycling and complex operations, detection strategies based on isothermal amplification and CRISPR/Cas are simpler and more time-efficient, representing a highly promising approach for nucleic acid analysis [32, 33]. The “invading stacking primer” amplification reaction (ISAR) is a form of strand displacement amplification, which operates based on continuous stacked hybridization and toehold-mediated strand exchange, functioning through modulation of primer hybridization sites [34]. ISAR can convert a single miRNA molecule into a large number of DNA strands, thereby activating the trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a to enable highly sensitive miRNA detection. Therefore, this study integrates ISAR with CRISPR/Cas12a to achieve synergistic amplification.

  Despite the high analytical performance of existing nucleic acid detection technologies, the development of portable and visual detection techniques has become a crucial research direction in the field of molecular diagnostics, aiming to address limitations such as dependence on sophisticated instrumentation and complex operational procedures [35–37]. As a representative point-of-care testing (POCT) platform, lateral flow assay (LFA) strips have been widely used in applications such as pregnancy testing and infectious disease screening due to their ease of operation, stability at room temperature, and cost-effectiveness [38, 39]. However, in the context of early tumor diagnosis—particularly for the detection of low-abundance biomarkers—conventional LFA methods face significant sensitivity limitations and often fail to meet the required diagnostic thresholds [40]. Researchers have found that integrating nucleic acid amplification techniques with LFA can markedly enhance the sensitivity of test strips. For example, Peng et al. proposed an HCR-mediated multi-site exonuclease III amplification strategy for ultrasensitive fluorescence detection of bacterial 16 S rRNA [41]. This method captures the signal based on complementary base pairing and enables direct signal readout on the LFA strip. Tong et al. synthesized a novel colorimetric label by integrating gold-silver alloy nanoparticles, enabling qualitative visual screening of aflatoxin B1 at ultra-low concentrations (0.2 ng/mL) with the naked eye [42]. By rationally selecting and combining signal amplification techniques with LFA, POCT detection of low-abundance biomarkers becomes feasible, providing valuable support for early cancer diagnosis.

  Based on the aforementioned research background, this study designed a fluorescence probe for miR155 detection by coupling isothermal nucleic acid amplification with the CRISPR/Cas12a system (INACS). This probe leverages the combined use of enzymatic amplification and CRISPR-based cleavage systems to achieve dual signal amplification, thereby enhancing detection sensitivity. miR155 is first amplified using the ISAR strategy, generating double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) with a specific sequence. This dsDNA subsequently activates the trans-cleavage activity of the CRISPR/Cas12a endonuclease, which then cleaves single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) reporters (labeled with a fluorophore and a AuNP as quencher in close proximity) to release fluorescence and enable the quantitative detection of miR155. Building upon this quantitative platform, a visual lateral flow assay (LFA) strategy was further developed for miR155 detection. By modifying the reporter tag design, two types (colorimetric and fluorescent) of LFA strips were created. The fluorescence probe and LFA strips developed in this study will be applied to real sample analysis to evaluate their practical utility.

## Results and discussion

### Working principle of INACS fluorescence probe
  Scheme 1Illustration of the principle of INACS for miR155 detection

  The working principle of the INACS fluorescence probe for miR155 detection is illustrated in Scheme 1 The hairpin probe (HP) specifically recognizes miR155 and undergoes structural unwinding upon hybridization. Primer P then hybridizes with the opened stem region of HP, initiating target gene amplification under the action of Bst DNA polymerase and generating a specific sequence of dsDNA. This dsDNA binds to the Cas12a–crRNA binary complex, activating the trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a. The activated Cas12a cleaves reporter1 that is a single-stranded DNA labeled with AuNPs at the 5’ end and FAM at the 3’ end. Upon cleavage, the quenching effect of the AuNPs is eliminated, and FAM fluorescence is restored, producing a detectable fluorescent signal. Therefore, with increasing concentrations of miR155, the fluorescence intensity of FAM increases correspondingly. In the absence of miR155, dsDNA is not generated, the CRISPR/Cas12a system is not activated, and the FAM fluorescence remains quenched by the AuNPs, resulting in no fluorescence enhancement. Thus, the fluorescence intensity directly reflects the amount of miR155 present.

  Based on this mechanism, LFA strips were further developed. Reporter2, a single-stranded DNA modified with both AuNPs and biotin (Bio), was used for AuNPs-based LFA detection, while reporter3, a single-stranded DNA modified with red quantum dots (rQDs) and Bio, was used for fluorescence-based LFA detection. The control line (C line) was immobilized with streptavidin (SA)-modified single-stranded DNA, which specifically captured biotin-labeled reporter2 or reporter3. This design served as an internal control to ensure the reliability and functionality of the assay system. The C line generates a stable signal regardless of the presence of miR155. The test line (T line) captures signal molecules via complementary hybridization using tDNA to the single-stranded region of reporter2 or reporter3. In the presence of miR155, the reporter is cleaved by Cas12a, preventing hybridization with the T line and resulting in a reduced signal. As the miR155 concentration increases, the signal intensity on the T line decreases. Therefore, the higher the miR155 content, the weaker the T line signal relative to the C line, enabling semi-quantitative or quantitative visual analysis of miR155.


### HP design
  First, based on the principle of complementary base pairing, the HP sequence was designed to target miR155. HPs was performed using NUPACK software (Fig. 1A–D). To achieve low background and high-efficiency detection, the HP structure with the highest stability was selected by evaluating the fluorescence intensity ratio ΔF/F₀ of the system (where F and F₀ represent the FAM fluorescence intensity at 523 nm in the presence and absence of miR155, respectively, and ΔF = F − F₀). As shown in Fig. 1E, the probe constructed using HP-1 generated a strong background signal in blank samples. This may be due to insufficient structural stability of HP-1, leading to partial spontaneous unwinding of the hairpin structure and leakage, which, even without the target, could undergo polymerase reaction to generate dsDNA capable of activating Cas12a. The stability of hairpin structures is mainly influenced by the number of nucleotides in the loop region and the GC content in the stem [37]. When the number of bases in the loop is significantly greater than that in the stem, or when the GC content in the stem is low, the stability of the hairpin structure is reduced. The closing rate of the hairpin is inversely correlated with the number of bases in the loop region [43, 44]; hairpins with larger loops are less efficient at forming secondary structures, indicating that loop size has a critical impact on hairpin stability [45, 46]. To address this, HP-2 was designed by optimizing the number of bases in the loop region. As shown in Fig. 1E, the probe constructed with HP-2 exhibited a slightly reduced response signal in blank samples. However, the fluorescence ratio remained low at only 0.07, suggesting that HP-2 still faced similar issues as HP-1. Further optimization of the HP structure is therefore necessary to improve the sensitivity and specificity of the detection system.

  Fig. 1(A–D) Structural designs of four HP configurations. (E) Fluorescence intensity of the CRISPR/Cas12a using 4 types of HP

  Further structural optimization focused on increasing the GC content in the stem region. According to the Watson-Crick base pairing principle, GC base pairs form three hydrogen bonds, compared to only two in AT base pairs, thus requiring a higher free energy barrier to dissociate [47]. Owing to their higher thermodynamic stability, GC base pairs play a more critical role in stabilizing DNA structures than AT base pairs [48]. In the design of probe HP-3, the GC content in the stem was increased to 67%, and the number of bases in the loop region was optimized to 21 (Fig. 1C). NUPACK simulations calculated the Gibbs free energy change (∆G) of HP-3 as −14.59 kcal/mol, indicating greater stability compared to HP-1 and HP-2. Experimental results showed that HP-3 produced a significantly reduced background signal, confirming its high structural stability, in agreement with theoretical predictions. However, the signal ratio of the probe constructed with HP-3 was only 4.14, suggesting that the hairpin structure was overly stable, making it difficult for miR155 to bind effectively. To improve the binding efficiency between miR155 and HP-3, mismatched bases were introduced into the HP-3 design, resulting in the slightly less stable probe HP-4. As shown in Fig. 1E, the background signal of HP-4 increased slightly compared to HP-3, but the fluorescence intensity ratio ΔF/F₀ significantly increased to 10.9. A comparison of the ∆G values between HP-3 and HP-4 confirmed that HP-4 had slightly reduced stability, allowing the hairpin to unwind more readily. This explains, from a kinetic perspective, both the increase in background signal and the enhanced fluorescence recovery efficiency. Ultimately, HP-4 was selected as the optimized hairpin probe for subsequent experiments.


### Characterization of prepared materials
  To confirm the successful synthesis of AuNPs, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to characterize the prepared AuNPs. As shown in Fig. 2A, the AuNPs appeared spherical and well-dispersed, with an average diameter of approximately 17 nm, which is consistent with previous literature reports [49]. Similarly, rQDs were also characterized by TEM (Fig. 2B). The rQDs exhibited a near-spherical morphology with a densely packed surface of small-diameter CdSe/ZnS quantum dots. The overall particle size was uniform, with an average diameter of approximately 167 nm. Further characterization of the conjugation between DNA and nanomaterials was performed using UV–visible (UV-vis) spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 2C, AuNPs exhibited a maximum absorption peak at 520 nm. DNA, due to the presence of its -C = C- backbone, displays a characteristic absorption peak at 260 nm [50]. After centrifugation and purification, the UV-vis spectrum of AuNPs-DNA displayed both the characteristic peaks of AuNPs and DNA, confirming the successful synthesis of AuNPs-DNA conjugates [49]. As seen in Fig. 2D, rQDs showed terminal absorption around 200 nm but lacked a distinct peak. However, upon DNA modification, a characteristic absorption peak appeared at 260 nm, indicating successful conjugation of DNA with rQDs [51].

### Validation of experimental feasibility
  The design of HP is illustrated in Fig. 3A, in which the loop region “Ⅰ” serves as the recognition sequence for the target miR155. In the presence of miR155, specific binding occurs between miR155 and the “Ⅰ” region of HP, triggering the unwinding of the hairpin structure. The resulting HP–miR155 intermediate is extended by DNA polymerase to form a complete dsDNA, part of which is complementary to region “Ⅱ” of the crRNA, thereby activating the trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a. In contrast, the stem region “Ⅲ” of HP contains several mismatched bases relative to the “Ⅱ” region of crRNA, preventing the HP from binding to crRNA on its own. As a result, the HP cannot independently activate the trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a, effectively reducing the background signal of the probe.

  To verify the feasibility of the experimental system, Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) was used to validate the ISAR process. As shown in Fig. 3B, DNA double-stranded marker, miR155, and thermally denatured and reannealed HP were used as reference bands. Lane 1 shows the miR155 band, located just above the 20 bp marker. A band also appears around 80 bp due to miR155 self-dimerization at high concentrations (Figure S1), which significantly reduces its migration rate. In lane 3, compared with lanes 1 and 2, a new band appears above 40 bp, confirming that HP can hybridize with miR155. In lane 6, where the target miR155 is absent, the HP band remains unchanged, indicating that HP is highly stable with minimal initial leakage. In lane 7, after adding miR155, the HP band weakens and a new band appears around 60 bp, with a higher molecular weight than the HP–miR155 hybrid observed in lane 3. This suggests that the HP–miR155 intermediate was converted into dsDNA under enzymatic action, i.e., the ISAR product. These results demonstrate that the components of the probe do not interfere with each other and that the ISAR reaction occurs only in the presence of miR155.

  Building on this, the feasibility of the CRISPR reaction was further validated in this study. As shown in Fig. 3C, a significant fluorescence signal was observed only when the system contained the complete set of reaction components, including the target miR155 and the CRISPR/Cas12a complex. In other experimental groups, the signal intensity was nearly indistinguishable from that of the control group containing only the reporter strand, indicating that the components present in the system do not interfere with signal output. Upon introducing different concentrations of miR155 into the CRISPR/Cas12a system, the FAM fluorescence intensity was found to increase proportionally with miR155 concentration (Fig. 3D). This observation clearly demonstrates a correlation between the fluorescence response and miR155 concentration. Compared with the signal intensity in the absence of miR155, a notable signal enhancement was recorded. Moreover, the fluorescence signal at 0 M miR155 was very low, indicating that the detection system exhibits a minimal background signal. These results collectively confirm that the CRISPR/Cas12a system can serve as a highly sensitive tool for monitoring changes in miR155 concentration.

  This study confirmed the specific activation of Cas12a trans-cleavage activity by the target miR155. PAGE was used to analyze multiple miRNAs, including miR-21, miR-122, and miR155. As shown in Figure S2A, the initial position of the HP strand was located just below the 40 bp marker. In the presence of miRNA and HP, a distinct band above 40 bp was observed only in the miR155 group (lane 6), indicating the formation of the HP–miR155 hybrid strand. Notably, lane 7 showed that after the addition of polymerase and dNTPs, the intensity of the HP–miR155 band significantly decreased, and a new dsDNA band appeared at the 60 bp position. This confirms that miR155 is the only miRNA among those tested capable of triggering dsDNA formation. To verify the specificity of dsDNA design, CRISPR/Cas12a system was used to analyze various dsDNA constructs. As shown in Figure S2B, only dsDNA sequences that were perfectly matched to the crRNA sequence produced a significant fluorescence signal. This dsDNA can be specifically recognized by Cas12a, activating its trans-cleavage activity and enabling it to cleave the reporter strand, thereby generating a fluorescence signal. In contrast, other non-specifically designed dsDNA sequences failed to activate Cas12a. These results demonstrate the sequence-specific recognition capability of Cas12a, indicating that rationally designed dsDNA is essential for its activation. They also further support that CRISPR/Cas12a can serve as a highly specific detection method for monitoring the presence of miR155.


### Optimization of experimental conditions
  To enhance the sensitivity of the INACS probe constructed in this study for miR155 detection, various experimental conditions were optimized. A single-variable approach was used to evaluate the impact of different factors. The sensitivity of the prob for miR155 was assessed by comparing the fluorescence intensity ratio ∆F/F₀, where F and F₀ represent the FAM fluorescence intensities at 523 nm in the presence and absence of miR155, respectively, and ∆F = F − F₀. A higher ratio indicates greater probe sensitivity.

  As shown in Figure S3A, the ∆F/F₀ value was highest when the ISAR reaction was conducted at 37 °C. At lower temperatures, interactions between nucleic acid were weak, resulting in reduced ISAR reaction efficiency and lower fluorescence ratios. Conversely, excessively high temperatures destabilized nucleic acid strands, leading to uncontrollable background signals and a decrease in fluorescence response [52]. Figure S3B illustrates that the ISAR reaction proceeded continuously within 0–3 h, with ∆F/F₀ gradually increasing and peaking at 90 min. After 90 min, the ISAR reaction approached saturation. Therefore, the optimal ISAR reaction conditions were determined to be 37 °C for 90 min. Next, the CRISPR reaction temperature was optimized. According to Figure S3C, when the ISAR product reacted with the CRISPR system at 37 °C, the ∆F/F₀ value was highest, indicating that 37 °C is the optimal temperature for the CRISPR system. Figure S3D shows the optimization of CRISPR reaction time. After the trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a was activated, the reporter was cleaved, resulting in the recovery of FAM fluorescence intensity. ∆F/F₀ peaked at 60 min. After 60 min, due to the continuous nonspecific cleavage by Cas12a, the background signal increased, and ∆F/F₀ began to decline. Therefore, the optimal reaction time for CRISPR was set to 60 min.

  Further optimization was performed for the concentrations of key reaction components: Figure S4A shows the optimization of Bst DNA polymerase concentration. ∆F/F₀ peaked at 4 U/µL, thus 4 U/µL was selected as the optimal concentration. Figure S4B shows that when HP was at 100 nM, ∆F/F₀ reached its maximum. Further increase in concentration raised the background signal and caused ∆F/F₀ to decline. Therefore, 100 nM was chosen as the optimal amount of HP. Figure S4C shows the optimization of primer P concentration. ∆F/F₀ peaked at 100 nM and then plateaued, so 100 nM was chosen as the optimal concentration for P. Finally, the concentration of Cas12a protein, a key substrate in the CRISPR reaction, was optimized. As shown in Figure S4D, the FAM fluorescence intensity increased with the Cas12a concentration, and ∆F/F₀ peaked at 500 nM. Further increase in protein concentration led to elevated background signal and a reduction in ∆F/F₀. Thus, 500 nM was selected as the optimal Cas12a protein concentration.

  The running buffer for the test strip was also optimized. The sensitivity was evaluated by comparing the average gray value ratio ∆V/V₀ (where V and V₀ represent the signal intensities at the T-line in the presence and absence of the target, respectively, and ∆V = V – V₀). As shown in Figure S5A, with increasing BSA concentration, background signal decreased and ∆V/V₀ gradually increased, peaking at 0.2% BSA. Therefore, 0.2% BSA was selected. The content of Tween-20 was then optimized (Figure S5B). ∆V/V₀ increased with increasing Tween-20 concentration, reaching a maximum at 0.5%. Further increase in Tween-20 reduced ∆V/V₀, mainly because excessively high concentrations significantly lowered the surface tension of the solution, causing it to flow too quickly and reducing the effective reaction time, which weakened the signal on the test line. Therefore, PBS containing 0.5% Tween-20 was selected as the optimal running buffer.


### The INACS probe for miR155 detection
  Fig. 4(A) Fluorescence spectra of the INACS probe with different concentrations of miR155. (B) Linear fitting curve of fluorescence intensity ratio (∆F/F₀) versus Lg[miR155], n = 3. (C) Response of INACS to miR155 and interfering miRNAs. (D) Comparison of miR155 levels in serum from breast cancer patients and healthy individuals, n = 20 (***p < 0.001)

  Under optimized reaction conditions, different concentrations of miR155 were incubated with the detection probe to evaluate the quantitative analysis capability of the INACS probe for miR155. As shown in Fig. 4A, with increasing concentrations of miR155, the fluorescence intensity of FAM at 523 nm gradually increased, showing a positive correlation. To quantify this relationship, a linear fitting was performed between the fluorescence intensity ratio (∆F/F₀) and the logarithm of miR155 concentration (Lg[miR155]). As shown in Fig. 4B, ∆F/F₀ exhibited a good linear relationship with Lg[miR155] in the concentration range of 0.5–50 pM. The linear regression equation was: ∆F/F₀ = 6.112 × Lg[miR155] + 2.566 (r = 0.9961, n = 3). The LOD calculated as 3σ/k (where σ is the standard deviation of the blank and k is the slope of the calibration curve, n = 10), was 34.46 fM. The limit of quantification (LOQ), calculated as 10σ/k, was 114.87 fM. To evaluate the sensitivity of the INACS probe for miR155 detection, the LOD of the method developed in this study was compared with that of other existing miR155 detection techniques. As summarized in Table 1, the INACS probe demonstrated a lower or comparable LOD compared to other methods. The dual amplification achieved by combining isothermal nucleic acid amplification with the CRISPR/Cas12a system, together with the rational design of the HP to minimize background signal, enabled highly sensitive detection of miR155. This fluorescence probe features simplified preparation, easy operation, and versatility in target detection, offering clear advantages for miR155 analysis.

  To assess the specificity of INACS probe for miR155 detection, several commonly overexpressed miRNAs found in tumor patient serum (miR101, miR210, miR21, miR122) [58–61] were tested at concentrations of 10 pM. As shown in Fig. 4C, although the interfering miRNAs were present at five times the concentration of the target, only miR155 (2 pM) elicited a significant fluorescence response, demonstrating excellent selectivity. Among these, the single-base mismatched (SM) RNA and double-base mismatched (DM) RNA of miR155 exerted slightly stronger interference on the probe than other interfering substances, which may be attributed to the presence of excessive RNAs with identical sequences. However, when the probe was used to detect mixed samples (miR155 spiked with various interfering substances), the results were consistent with those for miR155 alone, indicating that these interfering substances did not actually affect the quantification of miR155. This high specificity is attributed to the HP being designed with a sequence complementary to miR155, enabling specific recognition of the target and ensuring high selectivity of the probe. The accuracy and precision of the INACS probe for miR155 detection were further evaluated. Low, medium, and high concentrations (1.00, 5.00, and 40.00 pM) were selected to calculate intra-day and inter-day accuracy and precision. As shown in Table S2, the intra- and inter-day accuracy across all three concentration levels ranged from 88.9% to 106.4%, and the relative standard deviations (RSDs) were all below 15%, meeting the requirements for bioanalytical detection. These results confirm that the INACS method developed in this study is suitable for the detection of miR155 in real biological samples.

  To evaluate the accuracy of INACS for miR155 quantification in serum samples, a standard addition method was employed. The recovery rates were calculated to assess the accuracy and reliability of the analytical method. As shown in Table S3, the recovery rates of the INACS probe ranged from 98.8% to 103.6%, with RSD values between 3.7% and 6.2%. These results demonstrate that the INACS method is suitable for detecting miR155 in blood samples. To further assess the practical applicability of the INACS method, the probe was used to analyze blood samples from 20 clinical breast cancer patients and 20 non-cancer individuals. The detection results for these 40 samples are shown in Table S4 and Figure S6. As shown in Figure S6, a comparative analysis of miR155 levels in the serum of breast cancer patients versus non-cancer individuals revealed that the miR155 concentration in the serum of 20 breast cancer patients was 64.89 ± 15.70 pM, while that of 20 non-cancer individuals was 14.38 ± 3.32 pM. The serum miR155 levels in breast cancer patients were significantly higher than those in the non-cancer group, and the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4D). According to the literature [62], tumor tissues can release a substantial amount of miR155 into the bloodstream, resulting in elevated levels of miR155 in the serum of breast cancer patients. Compared with healthy individuals, the miR155 levels in breast cancer patients’ serum can be 4 to 7 times higher [63, 64]. Therefore, miR155 holds promise as a tumor biomarker for distinguishing between healthy individuals and breast cancer patients.

  These results demonstrate that the INACS fluorescence analytical sensing platform developed in this study can be successfully applied in real-world samples and is capable of detecting and diagnosing the tumor biomarker miR155, thereby confirming its potential for practical application in complex environments.


### Establishment of LFA test strips for miR155 detection
  Fig. 5AuNPs test strips: (A) Concentration gradient color reference test strip, (B) Test strips for breast cancer patient serum, (C) Test strips for non-cancer patient serum.   LFA are intuitive, convenient, and have great potential for practical naked-eye detection applications. LFA test strips were used as carriers to visualize the detection results of the INACS probe. The AuNP-based test strip images are shown in Fig. 5. At the T line, as the concentration of miR155 increased from 0 M to 2 nM, the color of the AuNP bands gradually faded from dark to light, allowing clear distinction of miR155 concentrations as low as 25 pM. Using the concentration gradient color chart of the test strip in Fig. 5A as a semi-quantitative reference, actual samples were tested—16 samples in total, including 8 normal (Fig. 5B) and 8 breast cancer serum samples (Fig. 5C). In normal blood samples, miR155 levels were below 20 pM (Table S4), and no visible color change was observed on the T line of the test strip. In contrast, clear signals were detected in the breast cancer serum samples. Figure 5D shows the intensity of the T line from Fig. 5A, and Fig. 5E presents the average intensities of the T lines from Fig. 5B and C. The difference in signal intensity between the two groups was statistically significant (p < 0.001). When compared with the quantitative fluorescence detection results (where miR155 concentrations in breast cancer patient serum also ranged from 25 to 100 pM), the semi-quantitative detection by the test strips showed good consistency with the fluorescence quantification. This indicates that the test strip method can achieve semi-quantitative detection of miR155 in serum. The fluorescence method offers high sensitivity and is suitable for precise quantification in laboratory settings, while the test strips, with their excellent portability, are better suited for rapid on-site screening. Both methods have their strengths and complement each other, allowing for effective integration based on practical needs.

MiR155 concentrations as low as 25 pM can be clearly distinguished (Fig. 6B). In normal blood samples (Fig. 6C), where miR155 content is below 20 pM (Table S4), no visible color change is observed on the T line of the test strip. As shown in Fig. 6D and E, compared to normal blood samples, obvious signals are detected in breast cancer serum samples, with a significant difference in signal intensity between the two groups (p < 0.001). Comparing the T line signal intensity of the breast cancer group to the control test strip, the miR155 concentration in breast cancer patient serum is approximately in the 25–100 pM range. When compared with the fluorescent quantitative detection results, the semi-quantitative detection by test strips is consistent with the fluorescent quantitative results, confirming that this test strip detection method can be used for semi-quantitative detection of miR155 in actual serum samples. The successful demonstration of visual reading in the current study highlights the significant potential of this platform to be further developed in combination with a smartphone-based digital readout. This future direction promises to bridge the gap between laboratory accuracy and field deployability, offering a practical and affordable solution for large-scale screening of miR155-related diseases in resource-limited areas.


## Conclusions
  This study developed a miR155 detection probe based on isothermal nucleic acid amplification and CRISPR/Cas systems, named INACS probe. This probe utilizes nucleic acid amplification to convert the target miR155 into a biological signal recognizable by the CRISPR/Cas12a system, which is ultimately transformed into a fluorescent signal, enabling highly sensitive detection of miR155. Additionally, a paper-based LFA POCT method was introduced to achieve efficient and convenient visual detection, allowing naked-eye recognition of miR155 concentrations as low as 25 pM through color changes produced by the probe. This strategy combines the sensitivity of CRISPR detection with the convenience of LFA, enabling differentiation between serum samples from healthy individuals and breast cancer patients, and offers programmable versatility for analyzing various tumor markers. With continued deepening and expansion of scientific research, nucleic acid detection technologies are poised to achieve a series of milestone breakthroughs in disease diagnosis. These advances will not only optimize detection performance but also drive innovation and transformation in diagnostic paradigms, injecting new vitality into the development of healthcare.

CRISPR-associated transposons (CASTs) are naturally occurring mobile genetic elements that combine programmable RNA-guided DNA recognition with transposase-mediated integration, enabling site-selective insertion of kilobase-scale DNA without nuclease-induced double-strand breaks. Current structural and biochemical knowledge can be viewed in terms of three functional modules: the transposase module, which performs DNA cleavage and strand transfer; the AAA+ ATPase TnsC, which forms a recruitment and regulatory platform; and TnsD or CRISPR effector-TniQ targeting complexes, which position this platform at selected DNA sites. Here, we synthesize recent structural and biochemical advances in target recognition, recruitment, donor-end pairing, and strand transfer. Comparisons with Tn7 and phage Mu highlight conserved transposase and ATPase functions and system-specific differences in how these components are organized and regulated. We discuss the implications of these advances for engineering programmable DNA-insertion systems and highlight unresolved questions in CAST transposition.

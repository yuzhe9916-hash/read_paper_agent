# Structure-activity relationship and antibacterial mechanism of saponins from quinoa husks against Bacillus cereus and their application in rice


## Abstract
  Bacillus cereus (B. cereus) is a major foodborne pathogen and a persistent food safety concern. Although plant saponins are promising natural antimicrobials, the antibacterial activity of Chenopodium quinoa Willd. husk saponins against B. cereus remains poorly understood. This study investigated the structure–activity relationship and antibacterial mechanism of quinoa husk saponins against B. cereus and evaluate their potential application in rice preservation. In this study, less polar saponins exhibited stronger antibacterial activity against B. cereus than more polar saponins among the twelve tested saponins. Among them, phytolaccagenic acid (Q8), isolated from quinoa husks for the first time, exhibited the strongest antibacterial activity, with minimum inhibitory concentration and minimum bactericidal concentration values of 125 and 1000 μg/mL, respectively. Q8 showed low cytotoxicity in GES-1 and effectively inhibited B. cereus proliferation and spoilage in rice. Further assays showed that Q8 disrupted membrane stability, causing membrane potential dissipation, protein leakage, cell deformation, and reactive oxygen species accumulation. Metabolomic and lipidomic analyses revealed Q8 mainly affected glutathione metabolism, cysteine and methionine metabolism, fatty acid degradation, and glycerophospholipid/glycerolipid metabolism. Molecular docking, RT-qPCR, CRISPR-Cas9 knockout, and genetic complementation analyses confirmed that squalene-hopene cyclase is essential for hopanoid biosynthesis, and hopanoids protect B. cereus against Q8-induced membrane damage. These findings support the potential application of quinoa saponins as natural antibacterial agents in food systems.


## Introduction
  Food safety is a critical global public health concern, with microbial contamination presenting a challenge to human health and the food industry. Among various foodborne pathogens, Bacillus cereus (B. cereus) is a ubiquitous, gram-positive bacterium that frequently contaminates starch-rich agricultural products, particularly rice and cereals (Y. Zhang et al., 2025). This rice-associated risk is exemplified by the improper storage of cooked rice, which can promote B. cereus proliferation and toxin production, potentially causing emetic food poisoning often referred to as “fried rice syndrome” (Y. Zhang et al., 2025). Moreover, the overuse of antibacterial agents such as antibiotics and disinfectants, accelerates the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) B. cereus strains (Rahnama et al., 2023). Although, synthetic preservatives are widely employed to extend food shelf-life, their prolonged application can trigger bacterial stress responses, leading to adaptive tolerance and cross-resistance (McMahon et al., 2007). In contrast, natural preservatives offer a promising alternative. Their multi-target mechanisms and broad-spectrum bioactivities decrease the risk of resistance development while conferring supplementary health benefits (Rachwał and Gustaw, 2025). Thus, seeking natural, safe, and effective antimicrobial alternatives to enhance food safety has become crucial.

  Among various natural preservatives, plant saponins, the second-largest group of secondary metabolites, stand out due to their diverse structures and multiple bioactivities (Wang et al., 2024). Our previous studies showed that ginsenosides effectively inhibit foodborne pathogen, but their high cost limits their practical application in the food industry (Tsutamoto et al., 2024). In contrast, the outer seed coats of Chenopodium quinoa Willd. (quinoa) are rich in triterpenoid saponins. While these saponins are traditionally considered anti-nutritional by-products during processing (Tabatabaei et al., 2022), provide an abundant, low-cost, and sustainable source of saponins. Crude quinoa saponins have been extensively documented to possess potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory activities, alongside excellent biocompatibility (Otterbach et al., 2021). Crucially, they also exhibit broad-spectrum inhibitory effects against various foodborne pathogens (Dong et al., 2020). Consequently, these characteristics make quinoa saponins a highly promising natural food preservative. However, current research predominantly focuses on their bioactive activity, and the specific antibacterial efficacy and underlying mechanisms of quinoa saponin remain unexplored.

  Plant saponins, functioning as natural amphiphilic agents, possess typical surfactant-like properties. In eukaryotes, these bioactive compounds exert their lytic effects by inserting into the lipid bilayer to complex with sterols, assembling into transmembrane pores, ultimately causing irreversible membrane rupture and cell death (Jiang et al., 2025). However, bacterial cell membranes inherently lack sterols. Instead, many bacteria synthesize hopanoids, a class of pentacyclic triterpenoid lipids that serve as structural and functional analogues to sterols (Nettersheim et al., 2024). These lipid components are indispensable for modulating membrane fluidity, maintaining structural integrity of the lipid bilayer, and conferring cellular resistance against various environmental stresses (Y.-M. Zhang and Rock, 2008). Despite their fundamental importance in bacterial envelopes, it remains unclear whether natural saponins can specifically target these sterol analogues. Particularly in B. cereus, the potential involvement of hopanoids in saponin-induced membrane instability has yet to be systematically investigated.

  Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the structure-activity relationship of quinoa saponins, evaluate their antibacterial activity and underlying mechanisms against B. cereus, and assess their potential application as food preservatives. The in vitro antibacterial activity was determined via MIC, MBC, and inhibition zone assays, while membrane permeability and structural alterations were analyzed to evaluate bacterial damage. In addition, the preservative effect of Q8 during rice storage was evaluated through plate-counting. Most importantly, untargeted metabolomics, lipidomics, molecular docking and gene knockout were combined for the first time to comprehensively elucidate the antimicrobial mechanisms of Q8 against B. cereus.


## Materials and methods

### Strain and materials
  Bacillus cereus ATCC 4579 (Guangdong Microbial Culture Collection Center, Guangzhou, China), Bacillus cereus ATCC 49064, a food-derived isolate associated with gastroenteritis outbreaks, Bacillus cereus DSM 4312, a representative emetic-type strain, and Bacillus cereus CICC 21261, a certified reference strain used as a positive control in food inspection (Bena Culture Collection, Xinyang, China), were stored in glycerin tubes. Prior to use, each strain was streaked onto LB agar plates and incubated inverted at 37 °C overnight. Single colonies were suspended in sterile saline, and the bacterial concentration was adjusted to 1 × 108 CFU/mL using the McFarland turbidity standard.

  Chenopodium quinoa Willd husks were purchased from Qinghai Shangkang Biotech Co., Ltd (Qinghai, China). Liquid-phase methanol anhydrous, acetonitrile, ammonium acetate, LIVE/DEAD BacLigh Bacterial Viability and Counting Kit, Rhodamine 123, SYTOX Green Dead Cell Dye, Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Fluorometric Assay Kit were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (China) (Shanghai, China); GES-1 (human gastric mucosal cells) was purchased from Wuhan Shanen Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). The CCK-8 kit were purchased from White Shark Biotechnology (Anhui, China). 2 × Phanta Flash Master Mix (Dye Plus), Ultra GelRed (10,000 ×), DL5000 DNA Marker, ClonExpress Ultra One Step Cloning Kit, FastPure Gel DNA Extraction Mini Kit, Lysozyme, HiScript II One Step qRT-PCR Probe Kit purchased from Nanjing Novozan Biotechnology (Nanjing, China). Hedera (Hed) and Phytolaccagenic acid (PA-H) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology (Shanghai, China), and ginsenoside Re were purchased from Chengdu Refensi Biotechnology (Chengdu, China). Quinoa saponins Q1-Q8 were prepared by section 2.2. Ginsenoside Rk1 were prepared by the laboratory with HPLC purity > 98% (Xue et al., 2020).


### Preparation and identification of quinoa saponin monomers Q1-Q8
  Quinoa saponin monomers Q1- Q8 were isolated following our previously established protocol (Dong et al., 2020). Briefly, the crude quinoa were fractionated on a C18 reversed-phase silica column using a stepwise methanol gradient (60%, 80%, and 100%; 2 bed volumes each). Fractions containing the target compounds were further purified by preparative LC (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). For structural characterization, Q7 and Q8 were dissolved in 1 mL of deuterated methanol. NMR spectra were acquired at the Institute of Chinese Materia Medica China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences, and data were processed using MestReNova software.


### Determination of minimal inhibit concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)
  The MICs and MBCs of quinoa saponins (Q1-Q8, Hed, PA-H), saponin controls (Re, Rk1), and positive control (cefradine) were determined via the broth microdilution method. Compounds in DMSO were twofold serially diluted in 96-well plates (15.625-1000 μg/mL). After 16 h incubation at 37 °C, MIC was recorded as the lowest concentration inhibiting visible bacterial growth. Aliquots from clear wells were then subcultured onto LB agar; MBC was defined as the lowest concentration yielding no colonies.


### Determination of the antibacterial activity of saponin monomers using the disk method
  Disk diffusion assays were performed according to the protocol by (Dong et al., 2020). Sterile filter paper discs were loaded with saponin solution at their respective MIC. These discs were placed onto agar plates inoculated with a B. cereus suspension (1 × 108 CFU/mL). After 16 h incubation at 37 °C, the diameters of the inhibition zones were measured.


### Preparation of B. cereus protoplasts
  Protoplasts of B. cereus (OD600 = 0.9) were prepared according to (Figueroa et al., 2018). Briefly, cells were treated with 5 mg/mL lysozyme in a lysis buffer (1 M sucrose, 0.04 M maleic acid, 0.04 M MgCl2, 3% TSB) at 37 °C and 180 rpm for 1 h. Following microscopic confirmation of cell wall degradation (Zeiss Axiolab 5), protoplasts were washed, resuspended in the lysis buffer, and tested for saponin susceptibility (MIC, MBC, and inhibition zones) as described in Section 2.4.


### Determination of GES-1 apoptosis
  The cytotoxicity of saponin monomers against GES-1 cells was assessed via the CCK-8 assay (Chen et al., 2023). Briefly, cells (5 × 105 cells/mL) were cultured in 96-well plates to 80% confluence (37 °C, 5% CO2, 24 h). Following PBS washing, cells were treated with saponins at their respective MICs for 22 h. CCK-8 reagent was then added for an additional 2h incubation before measuring absorbance at 450 nm. Cell viability was calculated as follows:Cellviability(%)=[OD(sample)−OD(blank)]/[OD(control)−OD(blank)]×100


### Antimicrobial activity assay in rice
  A rice preservation model was established to evaluate the efficacy of Q8. Rice samples (20 g/group) were divided into five groups: blank (untreated), Q8 (1×MIC/g Q8 only), control (PBS + B. cereus), Q8+B. cereus (1×MIC/g), and CED + B. cereus (1×MIC/g cefradine). Respective treatments (Q8, CED, or PBS) were sprayed onto the rice and incubated (30 °C, 30 min) for absorption. Designated groups (control, Q8+B. cereus, CED + B. cereus) were then inoculated with B. cereus (1 × 109 CFU/g). Preservative effects were assessed by quantifying bacterial survival on MYP agar and photographically monitoring visible spoilage on days 1-4.


### Laser confocal microscopy
  Bacterial suspensions (1 × 109 CFU/mL) were treated with DMSO, cefradine, or saponins (Q3, Q8, Re, Rk1) at 0.5× and 1×MIC. Following two PBS washes, cells were resuspended in 100 μL PBS and co-stained with SYTO-9 and PI in the dark for 15 min at room temperature (Chen et al., 2023). The stained samples were mounted on glass slides and visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems, TCS SP8, Wetzlar, Germany).


### Cell membrane potential determination
  To assess membrane potential, pretreated bacteria (referring to sections 2.8) were washed with PBS and exposed to 20 μg/mL Rhodamine 123 for 30 min in the dark (Sun et al., 2019). The stained cells were harvested (8,000 rpm, 5 min), resuspended in 1 mL PBS, and transferred to 96-well plates (100 μL/well). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was then measured using a Molecular Devices microplate reader (Molecular Devices, California, USA) at 480 nm excitation and 530 nm emission.


### Membrane flow determination
  Pretreated bacteria (Section 2.8) were washed twice with PBS, incubated with 0.5 μM SYTOX Green in the dark for 30 min (Li et al., 2024), and analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSAria™ III, Franklin Lakes, USA) with excitation and emission wavelengths of 488 nm and 523 nm, respectively.


### Determination of protein leakage by Bradford's method
  Pretreated bacteria (Section 2.8) were incubated at 37 °C for 6 h and centrifuged (8,000 rpm, 5 min, 4 °C). The resulting supernatants were filtered through 0.22 μm membrane filters to remove residual cells. Cytoplasmic protein concentration in the cell-free supernatants was determined at 595 nm using the Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard (Chen et al., 2023).


### Transmission electron Microscope(TEM)
  B. cereus was washed twice with PBS and fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde. The cells were then applied to carbon-coated copper grids and negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid for 2 min (Dong et al., 2020). Cell morphology was observed using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi High-Tech, HT770, Wetzlar, Germany).


### Analysis of ROS
  Following pretreatment (Section 2.8) and PBS washing, B. cereus cells were stained with 10 μM DCFH-DA (37 °C, 30 min, in the dark). Cells were then washed, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed via flow cytometry (BD FACSAria™ III, Franklin Lakes, USA) alongside an untreated control (Bai et al., 2023).


### Untargeted metabolomics assays
  Untargeted metabolites from pretreated cells (Section 2.8) were extracted based on (Ji et al., 2024) with modifications. Briefly, cell pellets were resuspended in 500 μL cold acetonitrile/methanol (1:1, v/v), sonicated for 30 min, incubated at −20 °C for 1 h, and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C). Supernatants were dried under nitrogen. Residues were reconstituted in 1.5 mL methanol, sonicated for 15 min, and subjected to the same incubation and centrifugation steps. Final supernatants were transferred for HRMS analysis. Quality control (QC) samples comprised 100 μL pooled aliquots from each sample.

  Raw data were acquired using the HRMS method described by (Ji et al., 2024) and processed in Compound Discoverer 3.3 SP for peak identification and alignment. Differential metabolite analysis utilized SIMCA 14.1. Volcano plots and metabolic pathways were generated via bioinformatics and MetaboAnalyst 5.0, respectively.


### Lipidomics assay
  Pretreated bacterial samples (500 μL, Section 2.8) were extracted with 200 μL water and 480 μL methyl tert-butyl ether/methanol (5:1, v/v). The mixture was homogenized (1,000 rpm, 12 min), sonicated on ice (15 min), incubated at −20 °C (1 h), and centrifuged (13,000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C). The supernatant (380 μL) was vacuum-dried. Lipid residues were reconstituted in 200 μL dichloromethane/methanol (1:1, v/v), sonicated, and centrifuged. LC-MS analysis followed (Tu et al., 2018), with feature extraction, peak alignment, and metabolite identification executed in Progenesis QI.


### Molecular docking
  The 3D structures of Q7 and Q8 were generated and energy-minimized in Chem3D using the MM2 force field. A homology model of B. cereus SHC was constructed using SWISS-MODEL. Prior to docking, water molecules were removed from the receptor, and polar hydrogens and Gasteiger charges were added using AutoDockTools. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina (Trott and Olson, 2010), and the binding pose with the lowest predicted binding energy was selected for further analysis. The resulting protein-ligand complexes were visualized using PyMOL.


### Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction analysis (RT-qPCR)
  Total RNA from pretreated B. cereus (Section 2.8) was extracted using the TRIzol method (Han et al., 2025) and spectrophotometrically quantified (Dinghaoyuan Biotechnology, China). The relative expression of shc was quantified with the One Step qRT-PCR Probe Kit. Amplification was performed in a 20 μL reaction volume following thermal cycling: 50 °C for 15 min, 95 °C for 30 s, and 45 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Expression levels were normalized to the 16S gene (primers in Table S3) and calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method.


### Deletion and complementation of shc
  The shc gene was knocked out using the pJOE-sg336-mediated Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats -CRISPR associate protein 9 (CRISPR-Cas9) system (Supplemental Fig. S1A). The vector, containing Cas9 and a homology-directed repair template (templates: pJOE8999, pUC-tsPurple, and B. cereus genome), was assembled via one-step cloning and introduced into B. cereus by electroporation. Transformants were verified by PCR and DNA sequencing (primers in Table S1). To eliminate the pJOE-sg336 plasmid, positive mutants were serially subcultured in antibiotic-free LB broth. Colonies sensitive to 25 μg/mL kanamycin were selected, confirmed to be plasmid-free, and verified by PCR (Supplemental Fig. S1B). and sequencing before being designated as B. cereus Δshc. The restored expression of shc was completed by using the plasmid pJOEshc harboring the replicon region form the plasmid pJOE8999 and the shc gene from the B. cereus genome (primers in Table S1). In addition, the plasmid pJOEshc was introduced into the B. cereus Δshc strain to generate the complemented strain. Positive transformants were verified by PCR (Supplemental Fig. S1C) and sequencing and designated as B. cereus Δshc-pJOEshc.


### Hopanoid extraction and quantification by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS)
  B. cereus and B. cereus Δshc mutant were cultured in 30 mL LB broth (37 °C, 180 rpm, 48 h). Harvested cells (5,000 rpm, 5 min, 4 °C) were subjected to total lipid were extracted using a modified Bligh and Dyer method (Doughty et al., 2014). Briefly, pellets were twice extracted with 10 mL methanol/dichloromethane (DCM)/water (10:5:4, v/v/v) via sonication (30 min). Following centrifugation (5,000 rpm, 10 min), pooled supernatants were partitioned by adding 20 mL DCM/water (1:1, v/v). The aqueous layer was re-extracted with 10 mL DCM/water (1:1, v/v). Finally, the combined organic phases were N2-dried and reconstituted in 1 mL DCM for analysis.

  Lipid extracts were analyzed using a GC-2030 gas chromatograph coupled to a TQ8050 NX (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Based on the method of (H. Li et al., 2015), separation was achieved on a DB-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm×0.25 μm). The oven temperature program was initiated at 50 °C (held for 2 min), increased to 250 °C at 15 °C/min, and finally increased to 320 °C at 15 °C/min (held for 30 min). Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.2 mL/min. The mass spectrometer was operated in full-scan mode (50-750 amu) with an ion source temperature of 225 °C and an interface temperature of 320 °C.


### Statistical analysis
  All experiments were performed in triplicate copies, and the results are presented as averages. Experimental data were analyzed by GraphPad Prism 9. The standard deviation shown in the figure is an error bar, and the data are analyzed using the T-test. The difference was considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.


## Results and discussion

### Preparation and identification of quinoa saponin monomers
  Eight saponin monomers (Q1-Q8) were isolated from quinoa husks and structurally characterized using HPLC-MS and NMR (Fig. 1; Table1). Among them, Q1-Q6 had been previously reported in quinoa husks (Dong et al., 2020), whereas Q7 was identified as 3-O-β-D-arabinose-phytolaccagenic acid. Notably, Q8 was identified as phytolaccagenic acid, which was isolated from quinoa husks for the first time (Li et al., 2020). Plant saponins are important defense-related secondary metabolites and exhibit diverse biological activities, including the molluscicidal activity of saponins from Sapindus mukorossi and the immunoadjuvant activity of saponins from Polygala senega (Sparg et al., 2004). In quinoa husks, saponins may contribute to chemical defense and protection against biotic stress. Therefore, their antibacterial activity and potential application value were further evaluated.Fig. 1Schematic of quinoa saponin purification, with HPLC profiles and structures of isolated monomers (Q1-Q8).


### Quinoa saponin monomer inhibiting B. cereus activity
  The purified quinoa saponins were evaluated for antibacterial activity against B. cereus. Among the eight compounds tested, only Q7 and Q8 showed antibacterial activity, whereas Q1-Q6 were inactive under the assay conditions (Fig. 2A, Table2). Q8 exhibited stronger inhibitory activity than Q7, with MIC and MBC values of 125.00 and 1000.00 μg/mL, respectively. In contrast, Q7 showed an MIC of 250.00 μg/mL while no MBC was detected within the tested concentration range. In agar diffusion assays performed at their respective MICs, Q7 and Q8 produced inhibition zones of 0.71 ± 0.03 and 1.07 ± 0.04 cm, respectively. Overall, Q8 showed the strongest antibacterial activity against B. cereus among the quinoa saponins tested. To test whether the activity of Q8 was strain-specific, three additional B. cereus strains were included: ATCC 49064, a food isolate linked to gastroenteritis outbreaks; DSM 4312, an emetic-type strain; and CICC 21261, a reference strain for food safety testing. All three strains showed MIC and MBC values identical to those of the type strain ATCC 14579 (125.00 and 1000.00 μg/mL, respectively), with no significant differences in inhibition zone diameters among the four strains (Table S2; Supplemental Fig. S2). These results indicated that the antibacterial activity of Q8 was not strain-dependent regardless of the isolation source, and that it holds potential as a natural preservative against B. cereus in the food industry.Fig. 2(A)Inhibition zones of B. cereus treated with control (C), cefradine (T), and saponin monomers. (B) Gram staining and respective inhibition zones of B. cereus cells and protoplasts. (C) Live/Dead (SYTO-9/PI) staining of B. cereus treated with control, cefradine, Q8, and Re (all at 1×MIC).

  To explore the structure-activity relationship (SAR) of quinoa saponins, its activity against B. cereus was compared with quinoa saponin aglycones (Hed, PA-H) and ginsenosides (Re, Rk1) (Supplemental Fig. S3). The results suggested an apparent association between antibacterial efficacy and compound polarity (Fig. 2A, Table2). The more glycosylated and presumably more polar saponins Q1- Q6, as well as Re, showed no detectable activity under the same assay conditions. By contrast, the less polar aglycones Hed and PA-H exhibited MIC values of 250.00 μg/mL, although no MBC was detected within the tested concentration range. Moreover, Rk1, a less polar ginsenoside than Re, showed markedly stronger activity (MIC = 15.625 μg/mL; MBC = 31.25 μg/mL), with MIC and MBC values of 15.625 and 31.25 μg/mL, respectively.

  This polarity-activity trend is consistent with observations in ginsenosides (Xue et al., 2020). These findings suggest that the antibacterial activity of quinoa saponin-derived compounds may be associated with reduced polarity caused by deglycosylation and increased exposure of the hydrophobic triterpenoid aglycone scaffold, which could facilitate interactions with bacterial membranes. Because crude quinoa saponins have been reported to induce membrane instability and intracellular leakage (Dong et al., 2020), a membrane-targeting mechanism was hypothesized for Q8. The MIC, MBC, and inhibition zone data were consistent across the tested B. cereus isolates, confirming that the antibacterial activity was not strain-dependent. The ATCC 14579 type strain was therefore employed as the representative organism for mechanistic studies, and protoplast assays were performed to probe whether Q8 acts through a similar membrane-level mechanism.


### Q8 targets the cell membrane of B. cereus
  To evaluate the role of the cell wall in Q8 antibacterial activity, B. cereus protoplasts were prepared (Fig. 2B). As shown in Table2, Q8 exhibited similar MIC, MBC values, as well as similar inhibition-zone diameters, against protoplasts and intact cells. Similar patterns were also observed for Q7, PA-H, and the positive control. These findings suggest that the peptidoglycan cell wall is unlikely to be the primary target of Q8 or a major permeability barrier limiting its activity against B. cereus.

  Since the cell wall did not appear to be the main determinant of Q8 susceptibility, we further hypothesized that Q8 target the bacterial cell membrane. SYTO 9/PI staining staining a loss of selective permeability, evidenced by increased PI fluorescence following Q8, cefradine, or Rk1 treatment (Fig. 2C). This aligns with previous reports that crude quinoa saponins and structurally related ginsenosides destabilize the membranes of F. nucleatum and B. cereus (Xue et al., 2020). As amphiphilic compounds, saponins like Q8 partition into the lipid bilayer, directly inducing membrane instability. Nevertheless, given the potential non-specific toxicity of membrane-targeting agents, evaluating the cytocompatibility of Q8 with normal mammalian cells remains essential before considering its practical application as a food-related antibacterial agent.


### Cytotoxicity evaluation of Q8 in GES-1 cells
  As shown in Fig. 3A, Q8 maintained 100% GES-1 viability at 250.00 μg/mL, indicating low cytotoxicity and good biocompatibility. Although some saponins may exhibit surfactant-like membrane activity associated with hemolysis or gastrointestinal irritation (Lin et al., 2021), Q8 caused minimal cytotoxicity in GES-1 cells. Generally, the irritancy of saponins depends on their binding affinity with membrane cholesterol (Lorent et al., 2014). The preserved viability of GES-1 cells suggests that Q8 may have relatively weak membrane-disruptive effects on these mammalian cells under the tested conditions. This structural or functional divergence selectively restricts its membrane-disrupting activity to prokaryotes, supporting its preliminary biosafety and potential as a natural food preservative candidate. To further evaluate its practical efficacy in a food matrix, Q8 was tested in a rice model.Fig. 3(A) Cytotoxicity of Q8 on GES-1 cells. (B) Images of rice samples under various treatments during 4-day storage at 30 °C. (C) B. cereus counts in rice over the 4-day storage. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01.


### Antibacterial activity assay in rice
  Q8 significantly delayed B. cereus-induced yellowing and visible spoilage in rice, maintaining a relatively fresh, white appearance comparable to the uninoculated group and the positive control (Fig. 3B). This effect was accompanied by significantly reduced B. cereus counts, which reached 9.2 lg CFU/g in the Q8 + B. cereus group compared with 11.4 lg CFU/g in the control group on day 4 (Fig. 3C). These results indicated that Q8 significantly suppressed B. cereus proliferation in rice. Preserving the original sensory quality of the food matrix is a major challenge in developing natural preservatives. Some botanical preservatives can cause undesirable sensory changes; for instance, carvacrol, a pungent phenolic compound, can impart a strongly irritating mouthfeel (Albaridi et al., 2022). However, the effects of Q8 on the sensory and textural properties of cooked rice require further evaluation through standardized sensory testing and texture profile analysis.


### Q8 disrupts cell membrane integrity in B. cereus

#### Q8 induced membrane depolarization in B. cereus
  Given the essential role of membrane potential in bacterial viability, we examined whether Q8 affects the membrane potential of B. cereus. As shown in Fig. 4A, Q8 induced rapid, dose-dependent membrane depolarization in B. cereus, with a depolarization profile comparable to that of Rk1. In contrast, the inactive analogues Q3 and Re caused little or no depolarization, suggesting that the Q8-induced effect is associated with antibacterial activity. This biophysical alteration is consistent with the membrane-active properties previously reported for other saponins. For example, tea saponins have been shown to depolarize C. albicans membranes (Chen et al., 2023), while heat-transformed ginsenosides disrupt the membrane potential of P. aeruginosa and S. aureus (Xue et al., 2017). Collectively, these results indicate that rapid membrane depolarization is an early membrane-associated event in Q8-treated B. cereus, suggesting functional membrane impairment and warranting further assessment of membrane structural integrity.Fig. 4(A)Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) and (B) flow cytometry histograms of B. cereus under various treatments. (C) Extracellular protein concentration. (D) TEM images of treated B. cereus. (E) ROS levels (DCFH-DA staining). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.


#### Q8 increased membrane permeability in B. cereus
  To determine whether Q8-induced membrane depolarization was accompanied by membrane permeabilization, SYTOX Green uptake was measured by flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 4B, Q8 markedly increased SYTOX Green fluorescence in B. cereus, with relative fluorescence intensities reaching 1.90 and 2.44 a.u. at 0.5×MIC and 1×MIC, respectively. In contrast, the inactive analogues Q3 and Re, as well as the untreated control, showed much lower fluorescence signals. These results suggest that Q8 increased membrane permeability and impairs membrane barrier function. Together with the membrane depolarization data, these findings provide evidence for membrane dysfunction in Q8-treated cells. This membrane-permeabilizing effect is consistent with previous reports on bioactive plant saponins, including tea saponins disrupting C. albicans (Chen et al., 2023) and quinoa saponins exhibiting antifungal activity against Botrytis cinerea (Stuardo and San Martín, 2008).


#### Q8 induced intracellular protein leakage in B. cereus
  Protein leakage was further confirmed irreversible membrane damage. As shown in Fig. 4C, Q8 induced marked protein leakage compared with the untreated control and the inactive analogues Q3 and Re, with extracellular protein concentrations reaching 5.87 and 5.98 mg/mL at 0.5 × MIC and 1 × MIC, respectively. These levels were comparable to those induced by the active control Rk1 (6.23 and 6.27 mg/mL, respectively). The substantial leakage of intracellular proteins indicates severe disruption of membrane integrity and barrier function. Similar leakage of intracellular constituents has also been reported for other bioactive plant saponins, such as ginsenoside Rh2 triggers protein efflux in F. nucleatum (Xue et al., 2020) and tea saponins in C. albicans (Chen et al., 2023). TEM analysis was subsequently performed to visualize Q8-induced ultrastructural damage in B. cereus.


#### Q8 caused morphological damage to B. cereus
  As shown in Fig. 4D, untreated cells (T) and cells treated with Q3 or Re showed intact cell envelopes, regular morphology, and well-preserved intracellular structures. In contrast, cells exposed to Q8 or Rk1 at 0.5×and 1×MIC exhibited pronounced deformation, cell envelope disruption, and cytoplasmic leakage. These morphological alterations were consistent with the membrane depolarization and protein leakage results, further supporting Q8-induced disruption of membrane integrity. These findings accord with previous evidence that ginsenoside Rh2 compromises membrane integrity in F. nucleatum (Xue et al., 2020). And that kalopanaxsaponin A induces morphological disruption in C. albicans (Y. Li et al., 2020).


### Q8 induced ROS accumulation in B. cereus
  In addition to direct membrane damage, oxidative stress may also be involved in the antibacterial action of Q8. As shown in Fig. 4E, Q8 induced a concentration-dependent increase in intracellular ROS fluorescence relative to the control. Fluorescence intensities reached 0.24 and 0.48 a.u. at 0.5 × MIC and 1 × MIC, respectively, showing a pattern comparable to that observed for Rk1. Tea saponin has likewise been shown to trigger ROS accumulation in C. albicans (Y. Li et al., 2020). Similarly, aronia melanocarpa anthocyanins have been reported to increase intracellular ROS levels and disrupt membrane integrity in E. coli O157:H7 (Deng et al., 2022), suggesting that ROS-mediated membrane damage may be a common antibacterial mechanism among plant-derived bioactive compounds. Together, these observations suggest that ROS generation may contribute to Q8-mediated antibacterial activity. Given the association between oxidative stress, membrane damage, and metabolic perturbation, untargeted metabolomic and lipidomic analyses were subsequently conducted to characterize the broader cellular response to Q8 exposure.


### Untargeted metabolomic and lipidomics analysis
  Untargeted metabolomic analysis identified 98 significantly altered metabolites (50 up-regulated, 48 down-regulated) following Q8 treatment (Fig. 5A, Table S3). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed significant perturbations in glutathione metabolism, cysteine and methionine metabolism, and fatty acid degradation (Fig. 5B). Because B. cereus relies on thiol-dependent redox buffers, including glutathione, cysteine, and methionine, to withstand environmental stresses encountered during food processing (Duport et al., 2016), perturbation of these pathways suggests that Q8 may compromise thiol-based redox homeostasis. This interpretation is consistent with reports that magnolol disrupts redox homeostasis in Mycoplasma synoviae (Qiao et al., 2023).Fig. 5(A) Volcano plot of differentially expressed metabolites and (B) Corresponding KEGG pathway enrichment in Q8-treated B. cereus. (C) Volcano plot of differentially expressed lipids and (D) corresponding KEGG enrichment from lipidomics.

  In parallel, lipidomic profiling identified 22 significantly altered lipid species after Q8 treatment, including 15 up-regulated and 7 down-regulated (Fig. 5C, Table S4). Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted glycerophospholipid metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, and unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis as the major affected lipid-related pathways, suggesting substantial remodeling of membrane lipid composition (Fig. 5D). Several unsaturated lipids, including linolenyl palmitatoleate, MGDG (16:0/18:2), and PE (P-16:0/18:4), were markedly reduced after Q8 treatment. Because unsaturated fatty acyl chains are susceptible to ROS-mediated oxidation, this depletion may reflect oxidative lipid damage, consistent with ROS-associated lipid injury reported in quercetin-treated Staphylococcus aureus (Yuan et al., 2024). Together, the metabolomic and lipidomic data suggest that Q8 disrupts redox balance and lipid homeostasis, ultimately contributing to membrane destabilization in B. cereus.


### Molecular docking and RT-qPCR analysis
  Given the observed alterations in lipid metabolism after Q8 treatment, we further examined whether Q8 might affect membrane-stabilizing components. Squalene-hopene cyclase (SHC) catalyzes a key step in bacterial hopanoid biosynthesis (Fig. 6A), which contribute to membrane rigidity and oxidative stress resistance (Belin et al., 2018). Molecular docking was performed to evaluate the potential interaction between Q8 and SHC. As shown in Fig. 6B, Q8 showed a stronger binding affinity to SHC than Q7, with binding energies of −8.51 and −6.19 kcal/mol,respectively. Q8 formed predicted interactions with GLN-313, LYS-319, ARG-378, and ASP-386, whereas Q7 interacted with ASN-178, LYS-179, TYR-223, and LEU-225.   RT-qPCR analysis showed that Q8 decreased shc expression by 1.89- and 2.81-fold at 0.5 × MIC and 1 × MIC, respectively, whereas Q3, Re, and Rk1 did not significantly suppress shc expression (Fig. 6C). The involvement of shc in hopanoid biosynthesis has been demonstrated in hopanoid-producing microorganisms such as Rhodopseudomonas palustris (Welander et al., 2009), and Schizosaccharomyces japonicus (Rao et al., 2025).Based on these findings, Q8 may affect the SHC-associated hopanoid pathway in B. cereus, which could weaken hopanoid-dependent membrane stabilization and contribute to membrane destabilization.


### Hopanoid deficiency enhances B. cereus sensitivity to Q8
  To investigate whether hopanoid biosynthesis is involved in the antibacterial mechanism of Q8, an shc-disrupted B. cereus (Δshc) mutant was constructed using CRISPR-Cas9 (Supplemental Fig. S1A). After transformation and plasmid curing, PCR analysis confirmed the successful insertion of the marker (Supplemental Fig. S1B). Consistent with the expected phenotype, hopanoids were undetectable in the Δshc mutant after 48 h of cultivation, whereas they were detected in the wild-type (WT) strain (Fig. 6D). Hopanoid production was restored in the complemented strain B. cereus Δshc-pJOEshc (Fig. 6D), confirming the functional of shc. These results indicate that shc is involved in hopanoid biosynthesis in B. cereus, consistent with the conserved role of SHC in hopanoid biosynthesis reported in other microorganisms, including R. palustris (Welander et al., 2009), and S. japonicus (Rao et al., 2025).

  The susceptibility of the Δshc mutant to Q8 was then compared with that of the WT strain. After Q8 treatment, the inhibition zone of the Δshc mutant was significantly larger than that of the WT strain, increasing from 1.07 ± 0.04 cm in the WT strain to 1.57 ± 0.06 cm in the Δshc mutant (Fig. 6E, Table3). Furthermore, the MIC and MBC values for the Δshc mutant decreasedto 62.50 μg/mL and 500.00 μg/mL, respectively, relative to those of the WT strain (Table3). To further confirm the involvement of shc, a complemented strain was constructed by reintroducing shc into the Δshc mutant. The complemented strain exhibited MIC, MBC, and inhibition zone values comparable to those of the WT strain, with no significant differences observed between the two strains (Fig. 6E, Table3). The increased susceptibility of the Δshc mutant to Q8 supports an important protective role of hopanoids in maintaining membrane stability under Q8 stress. This is consistent with previous findings that hopanoid-deficient strains show increased sensitivity to diverse environmental stresses (Rao et al., 2025; Welander et al., 2009).


### Membrane integrity and ROS analysis of the Δshc mutant
  To examine whether impaired membrane stability contributed to the enhanced Q8 susceptibility of the Δshc mutant, membrane integrity was compared between the mutant and WT strains. At 62.50 μg/mL, the Δshc mutant already showed substantial loss of membrane integrity. Membrane depolarization in the Δshc mutant reached 8756.33 a.u. (Fig. 7A), approaching the level observed in the WT strain under stronger Q8 exposure (9457.03 a.u.; Fig. 4A). At the same concentration, the mutant also exhibited greater membrane permeability and protein leakage than the WT strain, with values of 2.77 a.u (Figs. 7B). and 6.37 mg/mL (Fig. 7C), respectively, compared with 2.40 a.u. and 5.98 mg/mL in the WT strain.   Consistent with this biochemical data, TEM revealed severe structural damage and cytoplasmic loss in Δshcmutant at its MIC (62.50 μg/mL; Fig. 7D). Collectively, these results indicate that SHC-derived hopanoids are important membrane-stabilizing lipids in B. cereus, and that their absence increases vulnerability to Q8-induced membrane damage, thereby enhancing the antibacterial effect of Q8. This finding is consistent with the sterol-like membrane-stabilizing function of hopanoids reported in other bacteria, including B. diazoefficiens, where they protect cells against temperature and pH stresses (Pan et al., 2024).


## Conclusion
  Quinoa husks, a routinely discarded by-product rich in diverse saponins, provide an abundant, low-cost, and sustainable source of saponins. Evaluation of twelve saponins against B. cereus demonstrated that less polar saponins exhibited stronger antibacterial effects than more polar saponins. Phytolaccagenic acid (Q8), isolated from quinoa husks for the first time, showed strong antibacterial activity, low cytotoxicity toward GES-1 cells, and effective inhibition of B. cereus proliferation and spoilage in a cooked rice model. The antibacterial action of Q8 involved membrane disruption, intracellular ROS accumulation, and interference with hopanoid biosynthesis through shc transcriptional inhibition and interaction with SHC, which weakened hopanoid-mediated membrane stabilization and increased bacterial susceptibility to oxidative damage. These findings provide new insights into the structure-activity relationship and multi-target antibacterial mechanism of quinoa saponins against B. cereus. To support the further development of Q8 as a potential food preservative for industrial applications, future studies should compare its preservative performance with approved food-grade preservatives such as Nisin and validate its activity against multiple B. cereus isolates and other foodborne or spoilage microorganisms. In addition, its thermal stability, in vivo safety, sensory properties, and effects on the texture of rice-based and other food matrices should be systematically evaluated.

# Genetic vulnerability and dynamic mechanical stress synergistically drive sarcomere failure in human iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes


## Abstract
  Mechanical stress plays a critical role in regulating cardiomyocyte structure and physiology. However, mechanobiology studies still rely on static systems that failed to capture the progressive nature of native cardiac remodeling. Building on our prior shape memory polymer (SMP)-based platform, the present study integrates microcontact printing onto SMP substrates to achieve single-cell geometric control and on-demand dynamic mechanical modulation. Using CRISPR/Cas9-engineered MYBPC3- and BAG3-mutant hiPSC-CMs along with isogenic wild-type (WT) control, we systematically assessed sarcomere organization and contractile behavior under static and dynamic mechanical conditions. WT hiPSC-CMs displayed robust morphological adaptability and coordinated sarcomere remodeling in response to dynamic mechanical cues, whereas MYBPC3- and BAG3-deficient hiPSC-CMs exhibited mutation-specific defects in sarcomere integrity and contractile behavior that were exacerbated by dynamic mechanical stress. These results demonstrate that this SMP-based platform enables us to systematically compare genotype-based mechanical sensitivity to develop more severe cardiomyopathy phenotypes.


## Introduction
  Sarcomere is the fundamental contractile unit of cardiomyocytes, generating force through coordinated shortening of highly aligned myofibrils [1]. Myofibril disarray and sarcomere lesions within individual cardiomyocytes are widely recognized as hallmarks of heart diseases, particularly cardiomyopathies [2,3]. However, some recent studies showed that hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) carrying cardiomyopathy-prone genetic mutations might not exhibit clear sarcomere disorganization[[4], [5], [6]]. These findings suggest that genetic mutations alone may be insufficient to fully recapitulate the myofibrillar disarray observed in advanced cardiomyopathy diseases. Instead, extracellular stresses, such as cardiac overload and the resulting cytoskeletal tension, appear to unmask these pathological phenotypes [7,8].

  Mechanical stress has been recognized as an essential player in controlling cardiomyocyte pathophysiology in the native human heart [[9], [10], [11]]. Pathological conditions in cardiomyopathy diseases are often associated with the increase of mechanical stress from cardiac overload, which leads to cell shape changes, myofibril disarray and contractile dysfunctions [12,13]. For example, single hiPSC-CMs under relatively low mechanical stress showed highly aligned sarcomere structure and efficient contractions, while they developed myofibril disruptions and low contractile output under mechanical overload [14]. Mechanical overload could deteriorate the contractile deficits from the cardiac microtissues with titin mutations [15] or C-protein knockout mutation [16]. Knockout mutation on cytoskeletal integrity (αB-crystallin) or mechanical coupling (plakophilin-2) demonstrates that both cardiomyocyte structural stability and electrophysiological function are highly sensitive to mechanical tension [17,18]. Collectively, these studies highlighted that mechanical loading incorporates key niche elements that regulate physiological cardiac functions and induce severe pathological phenotypes of hiPSC-CMs with genetic defects.

  Current understanding of cardiomyocyte mechanobiology is still largely based on static systems [19]. However, static systems do not capture the dynamic nature of cardiac remodeling, which is critical to understanding the progressive phenotypic changes of cardiomyocytes from a healthy status to a diseased status. To achieve on-demand, dynamic mechanical modulation, shape memory polymers (SMPs) can be programmed to a temporary shape and then triggered to return to their original shape. SMPs have been used to create topographically dynamic substrates that direct cell alignment, migration, and cytoskeletal remodeling [[20], [21], [22]]. In our previous work, we demonstrated that SMP-driven topographic transitions mimicked ECM dynamics to promote hiPSC-CM elongation and progressive myofibril reorganization [20,23]. Further, dynamic mechanical stress from SMP shape recovery induced sarcomere impairment in BAG3−/− hiPSC-CMs, characterized by reduction in both sarcomere distance and Z-line length [24]. Although these findings showed that BAG3 deficiency and dynamic mechanical stress synergistically drive sarcomere disarray, uncontrolled cell shape introduced substantial variability and limited the ability to model eccentric remodeling in a systematic manner.

  To address this limitation, we integrated SMP substrates with microcontact printing to define single-cell geometry with precise aspect-ratio control. Micropatterning has been used to investigate how geometric constraint regulated hiPSC-CM structure and function. By defining cell size, aspect ratio (AR), and boundary, micropatterns promoted CMs elongation and enabled systematic study on sarcomere organization and anisotropic behaviors. For instance, hiPSC-CMs micropatterned with higher AR exhibited anisotropic organization of sarcomeres compared with circular geometries [[25], [26], [27]]. SMPs provide dynamic substrate actuation but lack single-cell geometric control, while micropatterning enforces defined cell geometries but cannot impose active mechanical stimulation. Their combination uniquely enables dynamic longitudinal cell lengthening within a geometrically defined single-cell context.

  In this study, we developed a pathology-inspired assay that integrates SMP-based active biomaterials with microcontact-printed fibronectin patterning to achieve simultaneous geometric control and dynamic mechanical modulation at the single-cell level. This platform allows systematic dissection of the interplay between cell shape, genetic susceptibility, and evolving mechanical load in driving cardiomyopathy phenotypes. Therefore, we incorporated MYBPC3-deficient hiPSC-CMs alongside BAG3-deficient cells, allowing systematic, side-by-side assessment of genotype-specific sensitivity to dynamic mechanical conditions. Using this platform, we reveal for the first time that BAG3 and MYBPC3 deficiencies exhibit fundamentally different mechanical sensitivities with direct implications for understanding phenotypic heterogeneity in cardiomyopathy patients.


## Method

### Fabrication of SMP polymer
  The SMP was synthesized by crosslinking tert-butyl acrylate (tBA, Acros Organics, Supplemental Table S1) and butyl acrylate (BA, Sigma-Aldrich) using triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEG-DMA, Sigma-Aldrich) as the crosslinker and 2,2-dimethoxy-2-phenylacetophenone (DMPA, Sigma-Aldrich) as the photo-initiator [20,28,29]. The monomer mixture was prepared by combining tBA and BA at a 98:2 mass ratio with 5 wt% TEG-DMA and 0.054% DMPA. A solution of 98 wt% tBA to 2 wt% BA was chosen to ensure high stretchability and stability of strained SMPs at 28 °C during the period of cell culture before SMP recovery is triggered. The solution was injected between two Rain-X–coated glass slides separated by a 1-mm Teflon spacer and UV-crosslinked (Spectronics XL-1000) for 10 min on each side. Unreacted monomers were removed by soaking the polymer in 100% methanol overnight. The tBA/BA polymer was then dried under vacuum for 48 h at room temperature followed by 24 h at 50 °C. After drying, SMP samples were heated and cut into dog-bone shapes using a hot-press. After cooling, the edges of the dog-bones were sanded to remove imperfections that could result in the SMP tearing during stretching. Dog-bones were loaded into a custom-made manual stretcher, preheated at 58 °C, and uniaxially stretched to 50%, 100%, or 150% strain. After cooling to room temperature, samples were stored at 4 °C to prevent premature recovery and cut into rectangular pieces of uniform size for micropatterning.


### Fabricating PDMS stamps for microcontact printing
  Micropattern dimensions (square: 45 μm × 45 μm; rectangular: 26 μm × 78 μm) were selected based on previously published single-cell micropatterning studies showing these geometries accommodate single hiPSC-CMs with spread areas of 1000–2500 μm2 and aspect ratios of 1:1 to 1:3 [[30], [31], [32]]. Photomasks were fabricated using a Heidelberg Mask Writer (DWL2000). The chrome layer on the mask was etched, and the mask was washed prior to use. A silicon wafer was coated with SU-8 50 and spun using a photoresist spinner to obtain a thickness of 25 μm. The coated wafer was pre-baked at 65 °C for 3 min, followed by a soft bake at 95 °C for 7 min, then allowed to cool to room temperature on a flat surface. The photomask was placed on top of the SU-8–coated wafer and exposed to UV light. The exposed wafer then underwent a post-exposure bake at 65 °C for 1 min and 95 °C for 3 min, at which point the patterns became visible. Uncrosslinked SU-8 was removed by immersing the wafer in SU-8 Developer for 4 min, followed by two rinses with isopropanol (IPA) and drying with nitrogen.

  Crosslinked PDMS elastomers was prepared from a Sylgard-184 kit by mixing with the base and curing agent at a 10:1 ratio. The mixture was poured onto the patterned silicon wafer, then cured at 60 °C overnight to ensure complete crosslinking and replicating the patterns. After curing, the PDMS was carefully peeled from the wafer and cut into small stamps compatible with SMP substrates for microcontact printing. PDMS stamps were sterilized under UV light and incubated with rhodamine labeled fibronectin (FN) (100 μg/mL) at 37 °C for 1 h to allow protein adsorption onto the stamp surface. The stamps and substrates were transferred to an environmental control room with constant 50% relative humidity. The humidified environment improves FN transfer from the stamps to the substrates. Excess FN solution was removed from the stamp by wicking it away with KimWipes, then an SMP substrate was pressed onto the PDMS stamp under a 20 g weight for 2 min. This contact ensured efficient and reproducible transfer of FN onto the SMP, giving well-defined micropatterns for subsequent single cell seeding.


### Quantification of SMP recovery
  The recovery rates of SMPs with different mechanical strain were compared by measuring the sample width before and after 24 h of incubation at 37 °C. The recovery dynamics of SMP with 150% strain was assessed by tracking the dimensional changes of fluorescently labeled fibronectin patterns on the SMP surface using shape recovery at 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 h. Dimensional metrics, including length, width, and area, were averaged across three islands from three SMP replicates for all the pattern designs. The recovery rate was calculated based on the fibronectin patterns before and after recovery by the following equation:(1)RecoveryRate=(LengthWidth)beforerecovery−(LengthWidth)afterrecovery(LengthWidth)beforerecovery×100%


### hiPSCs culture
  MYBPC3 and BAG3 knockout hiPSCs were induced by the CRISPR/TALEN system in the Conklin Lab at Gladstone Institute [16,33,34]. Isogenic WT hiPSC line and four mutant hiPSC lines (MYBPC3−/−, MYBPC3+/−, BAG3−/−, BAG3+/−) were cultured in 6-well plates (Corning) in Essential 8 (E8) medium with Geltrex (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Supplemental Table S2) coated surfaces. iPSCs were passaged when the confluency reached 90% with a density of 2.5x104 cells/cm2. For the first 24h, the medium was supplemented with 10 μM of ROCK inhibitor (Y-27632; Stem Cell Technology, Ca# 72308). E8 medium was refreshed daily. All hiPSC lines underwent standard quality control prior to use, including karyotype analysis, immunostaining for pluripotency markers (OCT4, NANOG, SOX2), and mycoplasma testing. Knockout lines were validated by Sanger sequencing and/or PCR to confirm indel insertion at the targeted locus by previous publications [16,34].


### hiPSC-CM differentiation and purification
  The protocol for hiPSC differentiation and purification into cardiomyocytes (CMs) followed our previously published methods [[22], [23], [24]]. Mesoderm induction was initiated by treating hiPSCs with 10 μM GSK3 inhibitor (CHIR99021) in RPMI supplemented with B27 minus insulin (B27/–I) for the first 24 h (Day 0). Cells were then treated with 5 μM WNT inhibitor (IWP4) in B27/–I for 2 d hiPSC-CMs were maintained in RPMI supplemented with B27 complete medium (B27/+C) until Day 20. hiPSC-CMs were purified using lactate medium after replating on Day 20. The lactate medium consisted of 4 mM lactate in DMEM without glucose, supplemented with MEM Non-essential amino acids (NEAA), GlutaMAX. Purified CMs were subsequently maintained in B27/+C medium, refreshed every 2 days.


### Cell seeding on SMPs
  While preparing cells for seeding, SMP surfaces not coated with FN were passivated with 0.4% Pluronic F-127 for 1 h. Excessive Pluronic will be removed by three PBS washes. hiPSC-CMs were dissociated into single cells and seeded at 2 × 104 cells/mL with ROCK inhibitor onto fibronectin-patterned SMPs (dynamic, pre-recovered, and unstrained). Cells were cultured at 28 °C with 5% CO2 for 48 h for initial attachment and spreading before a temperature increase to 37 °C to induce shape-memory recovery. After SMP triggering, cells were maintained at 37 °C for an additional 24 h prior to characterization.


### Immunocytochemistry staining
  The CMs were fixed in a 4% (vol/vol) paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution for 20 min, followed by permeabilization by 0.2% Triton X-100 solution for 5 min. CMs were blocked with 2% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) for 30 min before incubation with the primary and secondary antibodies (Supplemental Table S3), for 2 h and 1.5 h respectively, at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI solution for 10 min followed by several PBS washes. Brightfield images were acquired using a Nikon Eclipse Ti microscope with a Zyla 4.2 PLUS sCMOS camera, and fluorescence images were captured using a Leica Thunder microscope.


### Myofibril characterization
  Myofibrils were stained with sarcomere α-actinin (ACTN2) and troponin T (TNNT2). Sarcomere structural features were quantified using the zlineDetection MATLAB algorithm [35]. Z-line parameters, including mean Z-line length and sarcomere distance, were computed by overlaying color-coded segments, and the binary skeleton to quantify Z-line structural order. Z-line length refers to the lateral extent of individual Z-disc segments; sarcomere distance refers to the longitudinal spacing between consecutive Z-lines along the myofibril axis. Sarcomere density was determined by counting the total number of sarcomeres through ImageJ and normalizing counts to individual cell area. Under dynamic conditions, cell area decreases due to SMP shape recovery, which is expected to increase apparent sarcomere density independently of sarcomere number changes, which is acknowledged as a caveat when comparing static and dynamic groups.


### Contractile function analysis
  Videos of single beating hiPSC-CMs were recorded using a Nikon Ti-E inverted microscope equipped with an Andor Zyla 4.2+ sCMOS camera. Brightfield videos were captured at 100 frames per second. Contractile behavior was quantified using a motion-tracking algorithm that analyzed pixel displacement [36]. The velocity traces displayed characteristic dual-peak waveforms corresponding to contraction followed by relaxation. From these waveforms, beat rate (beats per minute), maximal contraction velocity (μm/second), maximal relaxation velocity (μm/second), time interval between contraction peak and corresponding relaxation peak (C–R interval), and time interval between contraction peak and following contraction peak (C-C interval) were extracted. Embedding dimension, computed via phase space reconstruction on mechanical contraction waveforms, captures the nonlinear dynamic complexity of beating signals; higher values reflect greater irregularity as an indicator of pro-arrhythmic behavior [37,38]. In this study, we deployed this metric as a readout for contractile irregularity that proxies arrhythmogenic tendency, but not as a direct electrophysiological measure. Image-based contractile-motion analysis has been shown to correlate with extracellular field-potential recordings from multielectrode arrays (MEA) in hiPSC-CMs, supporting its use as a functional proxy for arrhythmogenic electrophysiology rather than a direct electrophysiological measurement [39].


### qRT-PCR
  Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol™ Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Supplemental Table S4). Cells were lysed in 1 mL TRIzol, followed by the addition of 0.2 mL chloroform, vortexing, and centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C) to separate phases. The aqueous phase was collected, and RNA was precipitated with 0.5 mL isopropanol (10 min RT, then 12,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). The RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol and resuspended in nuclease-free water. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop™ One (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

  cDNA was synthesized using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad), with 20 μL total reaction containing 5X iScript Reaction Mix, iScript Reverse Transcriptase, RNA template, and nuclease-free water. Reverse transcription was performed on a thermal cycler: priming at 25 °C for 5 min, reverse transcription at 46 °C for 20 min, and enzyme inactivation at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by a 4 °C hold. Gene expression was quantified using TaqMan gene expression assays and Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each 10 μL reaction contained Master Mix, the corresponding TaqMan assay, nuclease-free water, and cDNA template. Reactions were run in 96-well plates on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with cycling conditions of 50 °C for 2 min and 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Relative gene expressions for ACTN2, MYL2, and MYL7 were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, normalized to the endogenous control gene GAPDH, and expressed as fold change relative to control.


### Statistical analysis
  We collected data from at least five cells per SMP (technical replicates) and three independent SMPs (biological replicates), ensuring robust and reproducible results. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism and presented as individual data points with box plots. Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey multiple comparison test. Significance was defined as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p < 0.0001 (****). Sample sizes for each condition were n ≥ 12 for both sarcomere and functional analysis.


## Results

### Dynamic shape changes of fibronectin patterns on micropatterned SMPs
  The SMP was synthesized by crosslinking tBA-BA copolymer (Fig. 1a), manually stretched to 50%, 100%, and 150% strain (Fig. 1b), and then cut into small pieces. Each piece of SMP was then micropatterned using micro-contact printing technique (Fig. 1c and Supplemental Fig. S1), resulting in fibronectin surface patterns with both rectangular (R) (AR = 1:3, 26 μm × 78 μm) and square (S) (AR = 1:1, 45 μm × 45 μm) geometries on the SMP surface.   The SMP substrate completed dynamic shape recovery within 24 h, which resulted in narrowing the fibronectin patterns (Supplemental Fig. S2). To profile the SMP recovery dynamics, the shape of fibronectin patterns (square and rectangle) was measured every 4 h at 37 °C under water immersion for 24 h (Fig. 1e and f). There was an increasing trend of shape recovery with the increase of programmed strain (Fig. 1g). The SMP with 150% strain led to the highest recovery ratio. Rapid shape change occurred in the first 8 h and ceased by 24 h (Fig. 1h–k). After 24 h, both square (S) and rectangular (R) patterns had a ∼50% increase in length with a ∼50% decrease in width (Fig. 1h & i). The area of individual patterns decreased from ∼2000 μm2 to ∼1600 μm2 due to the strain recovery (Fig. 1j). We further observed a 3-fold increase of the aspect ratio for both square pattern (1:1 to 1:3) and rectangular patterns (1:3 to 1:9) (Fig. 1k).

After 48 h of culture at 28 °C, hiPSC-CMs exhibited strong confinement within both square (S) and rectangular (R) fibronectin micropatterns across all groups.   We recorded spontaneous contraction of WT hiPSC-CMs on both S- and R-patterns, in all three mechanical conditions, at the 28 °C pre-activation stage and at 37 °C after temperature elevation (Supplemental Fig. S3c and d). Beat rate was not significantly different between 28 °C and 37 °C in all conditions. Contraction velocity and relaxation velocity were slightly increased at 37 °C on pre-recovered and dynamic compared to unstrained group.   The hiPSC-CMs were stained for sarcomere α-actinin (ACTN2, Z-lines) and cardiac troponin T (TNNT2, thin filaments) to compare sarcomere organization between different conditions for both S-patterns (square-shaped fibronectin patterns on SMP, Fig. 2b) and R-patterns (rectangle-shaped fibronectin patterns on SMP, Fig. 2c). Overall, hiPSC-CMs grown on R-patterns had much better structural alignment and organization than the cells on S-patterns due to the high-AR cell shapes (Fig. 2d–g). For static S-patterns, there was no significant difference on sarcomere density (Fig. 2d) and Z-line length (Fig. 2e) between unstrained and pre-recovered groups, but significant increase in sarcomere distance (Fig. 2f) and organization (Fig. 2g) in pre-recovered group due to its high cell AR. Similar trend was observed from the cells grown on static R-patterns that higher AR of pre-recovered groups had a longer sarcomere distance. For dynamic groups, cells grown on S-patterns only showed an increase on sarcomere organization, while cells grown on R-patterns showed significant increase in sarcomere density, distance and organization. These results indicated that cell elongation improved hiPSC-CM structural alignment, and hiPSC-CMs became more sensitive to dynamic mechanical modulation when they were initially elongated. To determine whether static and dynamic culture conditions affected cardiomyocyte gene expression, we measured the sarcomeric marker ACTN2 and the ventricular and atrial myosin light-chain genes MYL2 and MYL7, respectively, in WT hiPSC-CMs cultured on non-patterned static and dynamic substrates (Supplemental Fig. S4a–d). Although hiPSC-CMs cultured under dynamic conditions exhibited higher expression of all three markers, the differences were not statistically significant because of substantial variability among samples. The dynamic group also showed a higher MYL2/MYL7 ratio, suggesting a trend toward enhanced ventricular maturation (Supplemental Fig. S4d).

  Following 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, spontaneous beating of hiPSC-CMs cultured on the SMPs was recorded and quantitatively analyzed. Beat rate did not differ significantly across either micropattern geometries (S- vs. R-patterns) or mechanical conditions (static vs. dynamic) (Supplemental Fig. S5a). On the S-patterns, hiPSC-CMs exhibited minimal differences in functional parameters among the two static and one dynamic conditions. In contrast, hiPSC-CMs cultured on the R-patterns displayed a modest increase in contraction-to-contraction (C–C) interval under dynamic loading (Supplemental Fig. S5b), accompanied by more pronounced increases in contraction-to-relaxation (C–R) interval (Supplemental Fig. S5c), contraction velocity (Supplemental Fig. S5d), and relaxation velocity (Supplemental Fig. S5e) across the different mechanical conditions. Additionally, the X/Y contraction ratio decreased in both the S- and R-pattern groups, indicating increasingly anisotropic contractile behavior as cell aspect ratio (AR) increased (Supplemental Fig. S5f). Overall, healthy WT hiPSC-CMs exhibited enhanced anisotropic contractile function in association with cell elongation induced by high-AR micropatterns. In comparison, dynamic mechanical stimulation produced relatively modest functional effects, suggesting that contractile behavior was more strongly regulated by cell geometry than by mechanical loading. Based on the superior structural and functional maturation observed in WT hiPSC-CMs, the R-patterned platform was selected for subsequent studies of diseased hiPSC-CMs.


### Interplay between BAG3 deficiency and mechanical cues in disease manifestation
  Bcl2-associated athanogene 3 (BAG3) is a critical regulator of protein quality control in cardiomyocytes, supporting autophagy, inhibiting apoptosis, and stabilizing sarcomeres [[41], [42], [43]]. While predominantly associated with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), BAG3 mutations have also been linked to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) and restrictive cardiomyopathy (RCM) [44,45]. hiPSC-CMs with BAG3 mutations exhibit disrupted myofibrils, impaired chaperone networks, and reduced contractile performance, particularly under proteostasis stress [34,46]. We examined BAG3 ± and BAG3−/− hiPSC-CMs on micropatterned R-patterns with all three groups.   Sarcomere analysis showed that BAG3-mutated CMs did not show significant difference from WT CMs when grown in the unstrained static group, and only an increase in sarcomere distance was observed from BAG3−/− CMs in the pre-recovered static group (Fig. 3b–e). In comparison, under dynamic mechanical stress, BAG3−/− CMs showed shorter Z-line length (Fig. 3b), longer sarcomere distance (Fig. 3c), and lower sarcomere organization (Fig. 3e) than WT CMs. When comparing BAG3−/− CMs among three groups, cells from the dynamic group showed longer sarcomere distance but higher sarcomere density than the unstrained static group. Sarcomeres were overstretched to a longer distance during forced cell elongation during dynamic shape recovery, while sarcomere density increased due to the decrease of cell area. WT healthy hiPSC-CMs were able to adapt to morphological and structural remodeling, while BAG3 deficiency disrupted such adaptive structural remodeling and caused sarcomere fragmentation.

  From functional analysis, BAG3 deficiency remarkedly disrupted the spontaneous beating rhythm of hiPSC-CMs, shown as the dramatic reduction in beat rates (Fig. 3f), prolongation of C-C interval (Fig. 3g) in a mechanically independent manner. Neither BAG3 genotype nor mechanical cues significantly affected the C–R interval, indicating preserved diastolic function (Fig. 3h). Embedding dimension analysis revealed a strong dependence on pattern shapes, since both BAG3 ± and BAG3−/− hiPSC-CMs increased signal complexity under higher cell AR, indicating arrhythmogenic behaviors (Fig. 3i). However, BAG3 mutation did not induce significant contractile impairment in any group, showing no difference on contraction velocity, relaxation velocity and X-Y ratio between WT and diseased CMs (Fig. 3j–l).


### Interplay between MYBPC3 deficiency and mechanical cues in disease manifestation
  Myosin-binding protein C3 (MYBPC3), a thick-filament–associated protein, plays key roles in sarcomere assembly, myofibril alignment, and cross-bridge cycling [47,48]. Mutations in MYBPC3 represent one of the most prevalent genetic causes of HCM, as well as an implication in DCM [[49], [50], [51], [52]]. From sarcomere images, we found that MYBPC3 knockout mutations resulted in much more severe sarcomere disorganization in iPSC-CMs, compared to BAG3-mutated cells (Fig. 4a). In particular, MYBPC3−/− CMs from dynamic groups showed poor sarcomere integrity and zigzag-shape distortion. MYBPC3-mutated CMs from unstrained static groups did not show significant structural abnormalities compared to WT CMs, but it manifested more strongly in the pre-recovered static group (Fig. 4b–e). Importantly, sarcomere disarray was exacerbated in dynamic groups (Fig. 4b, d & 4e). ### Genotype-specific mechanosensitive cardiomyocyte binucleation
  Binucleation is a hallmark of cardiomyocyte maturation but is also associated with aberrant cytokinesis and cardiomyopathy-related pathology. We therefore quantified the fraction of binucleated cells across all genotypes under three mechanical conditions (Supplemental Fig. S6a-f). In the unstrained condition, the binucleated fraction was comparable among genotypes, indicating no genotype-intrinsic differences in the absence of cell elongation. WT hiPSC-CMs remained relatively stable across mechanical conditions (Supplemental Fig. S6g). In contrast, the binucleated fraction in BAG3-deficient hiPSC-CMs progressively decreased with increasing aspect ratio (AR), whereas it increased in MYBPC3-deficient hiPSC-CMs, indicating a cell shape-dependent, genotype-specific divergence in nucleation behavior. MYBPC3-deficient cells exhibit heightened sensitivity to their mechanical environment, while pathological cardiac loading and hypertrophy have been associated with increased cardiomyocyte multinucleation resulting from incomplete cytokinesis [53]. Accordingly, the elevated binucleation at higher aspect ratios may indicate maladaptive nuclear remodeling or cytokinesis failure rather than maturation alone. However, direct measurements of cell-cycle progression, cleavage-furrow formation, DNA content, and hypertrophic signaling are required to distinguish among these possibilities.


## Discussion

### Geometric micropatterns regulate cardiomyocyte physiology
  Micropatterning techniques have been widely studied for their ability to promote cell elongation, enhance sarcomere alignment, and improve contractile function of cardiomyocytes [14,30,31,54,55]. Under static conditions, we systematically increased cellular aspect ratio (AR) from 1:1 to 1:9 across unstrained S-patterns (AR = 1:1), pre-recovered S-patterns (AR = 1:3), unstrained R-patterns (AR = 1:3), and pre-recovered R-patterns (AR = 1:9). Increases in AR resulted in improved sarcomere organization (Fig. 2), alongside enhanced anisotropic contraction (Supplemental Fig. S3h). Importantly, the enhancement in contraction velocity and prolongation of C-R interval are much more prominent when AR increased from 1:3 to 1:9 than from 1:1 to 1:3 (Supplemental Fig. S3e and S3f). This nonlinear response suggests a threshold-dependent transition in sarcomere mechanics; wherein higher ARs more effectively affect sarcomere organization and excitation–contraction coupling. Although pre-recovered S-patterns shared a similar AR (1:3) with unstrained R-patterns, they had a smaller pattern area due to SMP shape recovery. However, no significant difference was observed between these two conditions, indicating sarcomere alignment and contractile function are governed primarily by geometric anisotropy rather than absolute cell area. Collectively, these static micropatterns allow us to study how fixed geometric constraints regulate cardiomyocyte physiology.

  During disease progression, cardiomyocytes undergo dynamic morphological, structural and functional remodeling in response to evolving mechanical loads, which are not captured by static systems. In DCM, a leading cause of heart failure, ventricular dilation occurs disproportionately to wall thickening, imposing chronic volume overload that drives maladaptive eccentric remodeling. At the cellular level, this remodeling is characterized by preferential longitudinal elongation of cardiomyocytes without a corresponding increase in transverse diameter [54,56]. Analyses of myocardial biopsies from patients with familial DCM have shown a reduction in cardiomyocyte cell area despite progressive cell lengthening due to eccentric remodeling [41]. Eccentric hypertrophy is commonly modeled in vitro by uniaxial stretching of cardiomyocytes cultured on the elastic substrates. Such stretching systems have demonstrated that acute or cyclic mechanical stretch induces rapid cardiomyocyte elongation, sarcomere lengthening, and transient enhancement of contractile force, followed by adaptive remodeling that restores sarcomere length toward an optimal range [31,57,58]. In contrast, excessive or prolonged mechanical stress provoked sarcomere lesions indicating the onset of maladaptive structural damage due to pathological eccentric remodeling [59,60].


### WT hiPSC-CMs adapt to dynamic cell remodeling through adhesion-coupled signaling
  In this study, we integrated SMPs with micropatterning to achieve robust control over cell geometry, while enabling dynamic longitudinal cell lengthening that mimics eccentric remodeling of hiPSC-CMs. Under dynamic cell lengthening, WT hiPSC-CMs on elongated R-patterns showed more pronounced improvements in sarcomere organization and contractile performance compared to the cells on square S-patterns. This enhanced response indicates that initial geometric anisotropy pre-sensitizes cardiomyocytes to subsequent dynamic mechanical modulation, likely by biasing focal adhesion kinase activity, cytoskeletal tension and longitudinal force transmission prior to active remodeling. In previous work, we established that focal adhesion rearrangement precedes and likely templates downstream myofibrillar remodeling [22]. Paxillin mediates the initial adhesion turnover response, whereas zyxin helps couple reorganized adhesions to actin stress fiber reinforcement [61]. Vinculin- and talin-deficient hearts exhibit lessened hypertrophic responses to pressure overload, while FAK, paxillin, and p130Cas translocate to the Z-disc during load-induced remodeling [62]. In WT hiPSC CMs, dynamic cell lengthening is expected to engage integrin–FAK signaling at costameres, promote cytoskeletal reinforcement, and induce YAP/TAZ nuclear translocation, thereby supporting adaptive sarcomere remodeling [61,[63], [64], [65]]. Stretch-induced deformation of β1-integrins at costameres recruits and activates FAK, Src, RhoA/ROCK, and PKCε, converting mechanical input into outside-in signaling that drives sarcomere assembly and adaptive remodeling [48]. FAK/Src activation also represents a central node linking ECM-stiffness sensing to cardiomyocyte hypertrophic and survival signaling [49]. These adhesion-mediated signals converge on the Hippo effector YAP, forming a feed-forward loop that stabilizes the actin cytoskeleton under sustained mechanical load [47]. Supported by previous literature, our results demonstrate that healthy WT hiPSC-CMs were able to retain the capacity for structural and functional adaptation under dynamic cell lengthening, thereby establishing a dynamic single-cell platform for modeling eccentric remodeling.


### BAG3 deficiency compromises proteostatic sarcomere organization under mechanical stress
  While WT hiPSC-CMs exhibited physiological adaptation to dynamic remodeling, cardiomyocytes harboring BAG3 deficiency displayed maladaptive responses characterized by sarcomere fragmentation and impaired contractile rhythmicity, reflecting acute proteostasis failure under mechanical stress. BAG3-deficient hiPSC-CMs showed localized sarcomere fragmentation that became more pronounced under dynamic eccentric remodeling, indicating an impaired capacity to maintain sarcomere integrity during sustained longitudinal strain. This response is consistent with our prior observation that M-line disruption in BAG3−/− hiPSC-CMs was undetectable at 5 h but emerged by 24 h, suggesting that disease-associated sarcomere vulnerability is both structure-specific and time-dependent [24]. BAG3 is required to preserve myofibrillar stability during mechanical stress, in part through interactions that protect Z-disc and thin-filament anchoring machinery [[66], [67], [68]]. Specifically, impaired BAG3–HSP70 chaperone activity at the Z-disc may prevent clearance of force-unfolded sarcomere proteins, leading to progressive fragmentation under mechanical load [67]. Therapeutically, BAG3 deficiency reflects proteostatic failure at the Z-disc rather than altered cross-bridge mechanics. Pharmacological interventions such as chaperone induction or enhancement of autophagic and aggresomal clearance have been proposed for proper treatment [67,68]. For instance, HDAC6 inhibition promotes removal and repair of force-unfolded sarcomeric proteins. In principle, this would help maintain myofibrillar integrity under mechanical load.

  In contrast to mechanically dependent structural defects, BAG3-mutant hiPSC-CMs also exhibited reduced beating rates, prolonged C–C intervals and increased arrhythmic behavior regardless of whether cells were cultured under static or dynamic mechanical conditions. This mutation-driven functional impairment likely reflects proteostatic failure rather than altered cross-bridge regulation. These findings align with the clinical features of BAG3 cardiomyopathy, in which truncating BAG3 mutations are associated with both Z-disc mislocalization and arrhythmias [69]. In BAG3-deficient engineered heart tissue (EHT) models and DCM patient biopsies, sarcomere-turnover defects and contractile impairment were observed, similar to those in our BAG3−/− hiPSC-CMs [41,68,69]. Notably, cMyBP-C has been identified as a shared sarcomeric client that co-purifies with the BAG3 chaperone complex, suggesting potential mechanistic convergence between BAG3-mediated proteostasis and MYBPC3-dependent sarcomere regulation.


### MYBPC3 deficiency drives stress-amplified, genotype-dependent contractile divergence
  Mechanistically, MYBPC3 loss reduces the population of myosin heads in the energy-conserving super-relaxed state (SRX) and shifts myosin toward the disordered-relaxed, force-generating state (DRX), thereby altering cross-bridge availability and contractile kinetics [47,52,70]. When partial cMyBP-C function remains, as in MYBPC3 ± cells, this shift can manifest as hypercontractility. In contrast, severe MYBPC3 deficiency can produce energetic stress, contractile failure, and sarcomere destabilization. Clinically, MYBPC3 haploinsufficiency is the most common genetic cause of HCM, whereas homozygous or compound MYBPC3 loss can drive DCM-like disease [4,47,71,72], matching the biphasic genotype-dependent response observed in our platform. Mechanically, MYBPC3 ± EHTs develop energetic defects, calcium-handling abnormalities, and troponin mislocalization only when contracting against physiologic, stiffness-dependent load, paralleling load-sensitive phenotypes reported in desmoplakin-, titin-, and MYH7-mutant EHT models [15,16,73,74]. Therapeutically, SRX-stabilizing myosin inhibitors, such as mavacamten, have been proposed to treat MYBPC3-associated hypercontractility, but not for DCM-like contractile deficits [4,47]. However, drug responses are also mechanosensitive; for example, the verapamil dose required to attenuate force in MYBPC3 ± hiPSC-CMs increased with mechanical load [75].

  In our system, MYBPC3 deficiency induced severe sarcomere disorganization in hiPSC-CMs in both genotype-dependent and mechanical-dependent manner. Under dynamic mechanical loading, MYBPC3-mutant hiPSC-CMs exhibited markedly exacerbated sarcomere disarray, demonstrating that disease phenotypes are amplified by forced eccentric remodeling. More importantly, MYBPC3 ± cells exhibited a hypercontractile phenotype consistent with HCM, whereas MYBPC3−/− cells exhibited impaired contractility and severe sarcomere destabilization characteristic of DCM. Forced eccentric remodeling elevates mechanical demand during active contraction, thereby amplifying these mutation-dependent differences in sarcomere control and explaining the biphasic divergence we observed between MYBPC3+/− (HCM-like hypercontractility) and MYBPC3−/− (DCM-like failure) under dynamic remodeling. These findings reveal a genotype-dependent divergence in mechanical response that is minimally apparent under static conditions but becomes pronounced under dynamic remodeling, underscoring the critical role of MYBPC3 in coupling sarcomere mechanics to evolving mechanical load.


### Genotype-specific mechanical sensitivity
  Our results demonstrate that BAG3 and MYBPC3 deficiencies both lead to sarcomere structural disorganization under dynamic mechanical loading, but they produce distinct genotype-dependent contractile phenotypes (Supplemental Table 5). BAG3 deficiency primarily reflects proteostatic failure at the Z-disc, where impaired BAG3–HSP70 chaperone function limits the clearance and repair of force-damaged sarcomeric proteins [66,76]. As a result, BAG3-mutant hiPSC-CMs exhibit robust mutation-driven defects, particularly mechanically independent abnormalities in beating behavior. In contrast, MYBPC3 deficiency primarily disrupts thick-filament regulation and cross-bridge mechanics [47,77]. Accordingly, MYBPC3-associated phenotypes are more tightly coupled to mechanical demand, with dynamic loading revealing or amplifying genotype-specific differences that are relatively absent under static culture. Thus, BAG3-associated disease appears to represent a failure of sarcomere maintenance under stress, whereas MYBPC3-associated disease reflects impaired regulation of force generation under load. This distinction highlights two complementary modes of genetic mechanosensitivity in cardiomyopathy: proteostasis-limited mechanical resilience in BAG3 deficiency and load-amplified contractile dysregulation in MYBPC3 deficiency.

  Binucleation provides an additional readout of genotype-specific responses to mechanical remodeling. In cardiomyocytes, binucleation arises largely through acytokinetic mitosis during postnatal maturation and is closely associated with cell-cycle exit and terminal differentiation. However, altered ploidy and multinucleation are also features of pathological remodeling and mechanical overload in vivo [53]. In MYBPC3-deficient hiPSC-CMs, increasing aspect ratio increased the multinucleated fraction, consistent with a hypertrophic, overload-associated polyploidization response. Dynamic mechanical stimulation therefore appears to unmask a propensity for failed cytokinesis or cell-cycle dysregulation that was not apparent in unstrained cells. In contrast, BAG3-deficient hiPSC-CMs showed a decrease in multinucleated fraction with increasing aspect ratio. Because binucleated cardiomyocytes are typically larger and carry greater sarcomeric and proteostatic demands, impaired BAG3-dependent chaperone-assisted selective autophagy and sarcomere protein turnover may therefore lead to failure to generate and retain the binucleation process during mechanical remodeling [66,76]. Whereas MYBPC3 deficiency appears to promote an overload-associated hypertrophic/binucleation response under dynamic remodeling, BAG3 deficiency may limit the maintenance of mechanically stressed, highly elongated binucleated cardiomyocytes.


### Limitations
  hiPSC-CMs lack organized T-tubules, express fetal troponin and myosin isoforms, and depend on trans-sarcolemmal Ca2+ influx more than SR release, although anisotropic patterning and mechanical cues can promote partial maturation [55,78,79]. In our platform, hiPSC-CMs cultured on elongated R-patterns exhibited significantly increased sarcomere density, Z-disc spacing, and sarcomere organization compared with cells on square S-patterns, indicating improved myofibrillar maturation. However, binucleation in WT hiPSC-CMs remained stable across mechanical conditions. WT hiPSC-CMs exposed to dynamic remodeling also showed a consistent upward trend in maturation-associated markers, including ACTN2, MYL2, and MYL7, as well as an increased MYL2/MYL7 ratio relative to static culture, although these changes did not reach statistical significance because of sample-to-sample variability. Together, these structural, nuclear, and transcriptional readouts indicate that the platform promotes partial myofibrillar maturation but remains limited in driving full adult-like hiPSC-CM maturation.

  One likely contributing factor is substrate stiffness. The tBA–BA SMP substrate used in this study has a hydrated stiffness of approximately 2.0–2.8 GPa at 37 °C, which is orders of magnitude stiffer than native myocardial tissue [61]. By contrast, previous studies have shown that micropatterning hiPSC-CMs on softer substrates in the 2–5 kPa range support cardiomyocyte maturation more effectively [55,80]. CMs showed improvement in sarcomere alignment, higher nuclear elongation, and more adult-like contractility and cytosolic Ca2+-transient kinetics over long-term culture. This underscores substrate compliance as a key regulator of hiPSC-CM maturation. Therefore, although the SMP–micropatterning platform provides precise geometric control and dynamic mechanical actuation, its high stiffness may limit maturation-associated electrophysiological and calcium-handling phenotypes.

  As a result, disease phenotypes that require mature dyadic architecture are likely underestimated in this system. Diastolic dysfunction and arrhythmias are hallmarks of HCM and arise in part from impaired SR Ca2+ handling, altered excitation–contraction coupling, and early or delayed afterdepolarizations, all of which depend on mature T-tubule/SR organization [60,67]. Because hiPSC-CMs possess immature transverse-tubule networks and rely heavily on sarcolemmal Ca2+ entry, diastolic dysfunction and afterdepolarization-driven arrhythmic phenotypes are likely underrepresented in our single-cell platform. Capturing these later-stage and electrophysiologically complex disease features will require additional maturation strategies, such as metabolic or hormonal conditioning, prolonged culture, and three-dimensional engineered tissue formats that promote T-tubule formation and tighter SR–sarcolemma coupling [74,78].

  In addition, our model captures subacute rather than chronic remodeling. Specifically, the platform primarily reproduces the acute-to-subacute phase of eccentric remodeling, triggering early mechanotransductive and sarcomere-level responses to altered mechanical load. In contrast, chronic remodeling develops over weeks to months in vivo and additionally involves interstitial fibrosis, chamber-level hypertrophy, metabolic rewiring, and electrical remodeling, processes that cannot be fully recapitulated by the current single-cell platform [81]. Consequently, disease phenotypes that emerge after prolonged, sustained loading are likely underrepresented. Capturing these later-stage disease features would require repeated or continuous cyclic actuation combined with longer-term culture [82,83]. In future studies, repeated thermomechanical programming of SMP substrate, microfluidic cyclic loading, or biaxial actuation could extend the current platform toward modeling chronic cardiomyopathy progression more faithfully.


## Conclusions
  In summary, we establish a single-cell platform for dynamic eccentric remodeling that integrates genetic susceptibility with dynamic mechanical stress. While geometric elongation promoted adaptive sarcomere organization and contractile enhancement in WT hiPSC-CMs, these responses were blunted in cells carrying pathogenic mutations. Critically, dynamic mechanical loading synergistically amplified disease phenotypes in BAG3-and MYBPC3-deficient hiPSC-CMs, revealing structural and functional abnormalities that were not fully evident under static conditions. Particularly, MYBPC3 deficiency exhibited heightened sensitivity to dynamic stress, manifested as severe sarcomere disorganization and genotype-dependent contractile dysfunction. Together, these findings demonstrate that cardiomyopathy emerges from the interplay between genetic defects and evolving mechanical loading, highlighting the necessity of dynamic mechanobiological models for studying disease mechanisms and therapeutic responses.
